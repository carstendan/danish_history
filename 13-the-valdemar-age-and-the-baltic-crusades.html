The Valdemar age and the Baltic crusades

1157 – 1241 · Valdemar den Store · Absalon · Saxo · Knud 6. · Valdemar Sejr

Eighty-four years in which Denmark turned outward for the last time, took a Baltic empire, and lost it in an afternoon. It is also the period that produced the state's first written laws, its first history, and its first budget.

  2. Were the Baltic campaigns crusades, or was that the paperwork on a land grab?

01 / NARRATIVEThe man who rode off Grathe Hede

Danish terms in this section

Hvide
The magnate family from Zealand that supplied Absalon, his brother Esbern Snare and two archbishops. The Valdemar state is in large part a family firm.
lensed
The oath of a vassal to a lord. In 1162 a Danish king swore one to the German emperor, which Danish accounts have been embarrassed about ever since.

Chapter 12 left Valdemar at twenty-six, wounded in the thigh, sole king of Denmark because the other two claimants were dead. He would rule for twenty-five years and be called den Store — the Great — though not in his lifetime.

What he had going for him was not obvious in 1157. The treasury was empty, the coasts had been raided by the Wends for a century, and the man he had beaten at Grathe Hede had been the choice of a substantial part of the kingdom. What he had was a friend.

Absalon was a year or two older than the king, from the great Zealand family later called the Hvide, and had been brought up with Valdemar in the same household. In 1158 he became bishop of Roskilde; in 1178 archbishop of Lund. He was a soldier as much as a churchman, and for four decades he was the second man in Denmark and sometimes the first.

The pattern is worth naming early, because it explains this whole page: Denmark in the Valdemar age is run by one family and its clients. Absalon's brother Esbern Snare built the castle at Kalundborg. His nephews Anders and Peder Sunesen became archbishop of Lund and bishop of Roskilde. Their family monastery at Sorø received their bodies and their endowments, and a monk somewhere in their orbit wrote the history that tells us they were magnificent. It was extremely effective and it was not disinterested.

There is one fact that Danish accounts tend to hurry past. In 1162, at Dole in Burgundy, Valdemar knelt to the Emperor Frederick Barbarossa and swore fealty to him. Denmark was, formally and for a period, a fief of the Holy Roman Empire. Valdemar needed imperial backing against rivals and against Norway, and he paid for it in the coin that mattered most. Twenty years later Absalon advised Valdemar's son not to repeat the oath, and he did not — but the precedent had been set by the king whose statue now sits on horseback in the middle of Copenhagen.

02 / NARRATIVERingsted, 25 June 1170

Danish terms in this section

skrinlæggelse
The translation of a saint's relics into a shrine: the ceremony that completes a canonisation.
medkonge
Co-king. Crowning a son in the father's lifetime is how an elective monarchy is quietly turned into a hereditary one.
kroning
Coronation with anointing — a church ceremony, not a Nordic one. Denmark's first was in 1170.

On a June day in 1170 the whole apparatus of the new Danish state was assembled in the church at Ringsted for a double ceremony, and the two halves of it are the two halves of Valdemar's achievement.

The first half: the bones of Knud Lavard, murdered at Haraldsted in 1131, were lifted into a shrine above the altar. Papal approval had been obtained. Denmark's second royal saint was Valdemar's own father, which meant that the man Valdemar had spent his life avenging was now formally a martyr, and that his killers — the family of Magnus, the losing side of chapter 12's civil war — had been not merely defeated but damned.

The second half: Valdemar's seven-year-old son Knud was crowned and anointed as co-king by the aged archbishop Eskil. It was the first coronation in Danish history. Before this, Danish kings were acclaimed at the assemblies of the three lands — Jutland, Zealand, Skåne — and the assemblies could in principle acclaim somebody else. A crowned and anointed child, blessed by the church, with a sainted grandfather in a box above the altar, is a different kind of claim.

Between them, in one afternoon, these two acts did what twenty-six years of fighting had not. They made the succession hereditary in fact, they made opposition to it sacrilege, and they attached the crown to the church in a way that both sides expected to profit from. Chapter 12 showed the dynasty acquiring holiness by accident, when Knud den Hellige was murdered by his own subjects in 1086. Here it is done deliberately, as policy.

Eskil, who performed the ceremony, resigned the archbishopric seven years later and retired to Clairvaux. Absalon took his place. The independent-minded churchman of chapter 12 was replaced by the king's foster-brother, and for the next quarter-century the crown and the archbishopric were, in practice, the same interest.

03 / NARRATIVEArkona, June 1169

Danish terms in this section

vendere
The Wends: the West Slav peoples of the southern Baltic coast — Abodrites, Rani, Pomeranians. Not one people, and not one polity.
Svantevit
The four-headed god whose temple stood at Arkona on Rügen. Nearly everything we know about him comes from Saxo, who was not a sympathetic witness.
vendertogter
The Wendish campaigns, roughly 1160–85 — annual summer expeditions against the southern Baltic coast.

For most of the eleventh and twelfth centuries the traffic across the Baltic had been going the wrong way. Wendish fleets raided the Danish islands and the coasts of Zealand and Lolland-Falster persistently enough that Saxo claims a third of Zealand lay waste — an exaggeration, but the depopulation of coastal districts is visible in the settlement record, and southern Zealand and Falster acquired defensive earthworks.

From about 1160 Valdemar and Absalon reversed it. Almost every summer for twenty-five years a Danish fleet went south. The target in 1168–69 was the island of Rügen and the fortified headland at its northern tip, where the Rani kept the temple of Svantevit.

Vignette · a prince watches his god come down

Arkona falls in June 1169. The defences burn, the garrison surrenders, terms are agreed — and among the Rani who agree them is Jaromar, one of the island's two princes, who is about to make the calculation that decides the rest of his life.

Inside the temple stands a wooden figure taller than a man, with four heads facing four ways, a sword at its side and a horn of wine in its hand. The priests refilled the horn each year and read next year's harvest from how much had evaporated. Danish soldiers put ropes round its neck and pull it down. Saxo says the Rani expected the god to strike; nothing happened. The figure was dragged out, cut up, and used to cook the army's dinner — and Jaromar, standing there, accepted baptism.

It was not only piety and it was not only fear. He kept his principality, held it of the Danish king, and founded a line that ruled Rügen for a century and a half. His grandson Jaromar II would invade Zealand in 1259 on behalf of an imprisoned Danish archbishop — which is chapter 14. The god that did not defend itself bought a dynasty three hundred years of standing.

Jaromar, prince of the Rani · Arkona, on the northern tip of Rügen · June 1169

Rügen was attached to the diocese of Roskilde and stayed Danish for the next century and a half — long after everything else on the southern shore had been lost. It is the one durable conquest of the whole Valdemar expansion, and it was held not by garrison but by the loyalty of a converted local dynasty, which is a great deal cheaper.

04 / NARRATIVEWas it a crusade?

Danish terms in this section

korstog
Crusade. Whether the Danish Baltic campaigns count as one is an argument that is still live in Danish scholarship.
aflad
Indulgence: the remission of penance granted to those who took the cross. It was extended to the Baltic campaigns from 1147 onwards.

The honest answer is that the question is a modern one, and that both available answers are partly right.

The case for crusade is strong and has been pressed hard in recent Danish scholarship, notably by Kurt Villads Jensen. The campaigns were papally authorised. Participants took the cross and received indulgences on the same terms as men going to Jerusalem — that had been established for the Baltic by the crusade of 1147, in which Danish forces took part. Bishops sailed with the fleets and sometimes led them. The stated purpose was conversion, and conversion did in fact follow, permanently. An archbishop of Lund would later die on campaign in Estonia. These were not men cynically borrowing a vocabulary; the vocabulary was the point, and the older Danish habit of reading the Baltic wars as straightforward national expansion with a religious veneer looks, now, like a nineteenth-century reading imposed on a twelfth-century war.

The case on the other side is equally concrete. The campaigns produced tribute, plunder, captives and land. The Rani prince kept his position by becoming a vassal, not by becoming a saint. The Danes were competing directly with Saxon and later German princes for the same coast, and the competition was about who collected the tribute, not about who did the baptising. And the Wends had been raiding Denmark for a hundred years, which meant the campaigns had a perfectly good secular justification that did not need a pope at all.

What is worth holding onto is that twelfth-century people did not experience these as two alternatives. A war that enriched you, secured your coast, and remitted your sins was not a war with a cover story. It was simply a good war. The suspicion that one of those motives must be the real one and the others decoration is our habit, not theirs.

Meanwhile in Europe

The Baltic in these decades is one long frontier, and Denmark is only one of the parties working it. Lübeck was refounded by Henry the Lion in 1159 and became the hinge of German trade into the Baltic; the association of towns that grew from it is the beginning of the Hanse, which will dominate chapters 15 to 18. Bishop Albert founded Riga in 1201 and with it the Sword Brothers, a military order for the conversion of Livonia; the Teutonic Order arrived in Prussia in the 1220s.

So the Danish campaigns were not opening an empty frontier. They were racing German churchmen, German princes and German merchants for the same coastline, and by the middle of the thirteenth century the Germans had won. What Denmark keeps out of the whole enterprise is Rügen, Estonia for 127 years, and a permanent commercial relationship with Lübeck that will turn out to be far more consequential than either.

Further off: Thomas Becket was murdered in Canterbury in 1170, five months after Ringsted; Jerusalem fell to Saladin in 1187; and in 1204 a crusade aimed at Egypt sacked Constantinople instead. Popes had discovered that crusades were difficult to steer.

05 / NARRATIVEA castle at Havn

Danish terms in this section

Havn
'Harbour'. The village on the Sound that becomes Købmandshavn, merchants' harbour, and then København.
Valdemarsmuren
'Valdemar's wall' — the brick facing added to the Danevirke around 1170, and the largest brick structure in northern Europe when built.
borg
Castle. Before this period Denmark had almost none; the trelleborge of chapter 10 were three centuries dead.

An expansion needs bases, and the Valdemar age is the first great building period in Danish history since Harald Bluetooth. What went up was of brick, which had arrived in Denmark in the middle of the twelfth century and changed what was possible.

In 1167 Valdemar granted the village of Havn, on the Sound, to Absalon, who put a castle on the islet beside it. The castle's foundations are under Christiansborg and you can go down and look at them. The rest followed slowly: a market, a harbour, walls, and eventually a capital. What did not happen is the version on the plinth. Absalon did not found Copenhagen; excavation has found a working settlement at Havn well before 1167, with a market and a church, and the town's later importance owes as much to the herring in the Sound as to anybody's fortress.

Elsewhere: Vordingborg on the crossing to Falster, the staging point for the southern campaigns. Sprogø in the middle of the Great Belt. Kalundborg, built by Absalon's brother Esbern Snare, with the extraordinary five-towered church still standing beside it. Nyborg. These are the first Danish castles in the European sense — permanent, garrisoned, and belonging to somebody in particular.

And in the south, around 1170, the Danevirke was rebuilt. The earth-and-timber rampart of chapters 07 and 10 was given a face of brick nearly seven metres high and almost four kilometres long. It was one of the largest brick structures in northern Europe at the time, and it is worth pausing on what it means that a king pointing his fleets south-east into the Baltic was simultaneously spending that much on a wall facing south-west. The Wends were an opportunity. Germany was the problem. That is the southern border thread in one construction project, and every chapter from here to 1920 will return to it.

06 / NARRATIVEHow a fleet became a tax

Danish terms in this section

leding
The naval levy: the duty of the farming population to man and provision the fleet.
skipæn
A ship-district, owing one ship of about forty oars. Not from skib but from skipa, to arrange or equip.
hafnæ / havne
The smallest unit inside a ship-district: the group of farms that between them equipped one man.
ledingsafgift
Leding-money: the cash payment that replaced the duty to turn up. About three marks a year per havne.

Here is the machinery underneath the campaigns, and it is the most important thing on this page that has no battle attached to it.

The old leding divided the coastal country into ship-districts, each owing one ship with a crew of forty or forty-two oars, subdivided into small groups of farms each of which equipped one man. Estimates from the thirteenth-century law texts put the full levy somewhere between six and eight hundred ships. It was the largest thing the Danish state could do, and it could hardly ever do it, because summoning it took every able man off the land in the season when the land needed them.

Saxo says that after Rügen, in 1169, Valdemar reformed it: the general levy of farmers was replaced by a levy of every fourth ship, manned by better-equipped men. That cuts the paper fleet by three-quarters and makes it usable most summers instead of once in a crisis. Whether it happened quite as Saxo describes is disputed — he is describing a reform that suited his patron's family, and the surviving law texts are half a century later — but the direction is not in doubt.

The second change is the one that matters for everything after this page. Increasingly, the obligation was commuted: instead of turning up, a district paid. The going rate in the thirteenth-century evidence is around three marks a year from each havne.

Read that slowly. Chapter 12's whole problem was that the Danish crown had no annual tax and had to live on land, fines and windfalls, while the church collected a tenth of the grain every year. Within two generations the crown has one too — and it got there not by inventing a tax but by converting a military duty everyone already owed into money. Nobody had to consent to a new imposition. They simply stopped rowing and started paying.

The money bought armoured cavalry, which was what wars were being won with by 1200, and the men who provided that cavalry were exempted from paying. That exemption is the seed of the Danish nobility as a legally distinct class, and the mortgaged realm (14) will watch it grow teeth.

07 / NARRATIVESkåne says no

Danish terms in this section

bispetiende
The bishop's third of the tithe. In Skåne, the specific thing people refused to pay.
ombudsmand
The king's or bishop's local agent. In Skåne in 1180 they were Zealanders, which was much of the problem.
ægt
Carting duty — compulsory labour with your own draught animals. Extended in Skåne to hauling stone for castle-building.
landsting
The assembly of one of the three lands. Skåne's met at Lund.

Chapter 12 promised that the tithe would be resisted and that the resistance would surface properly in the 1180s. Here it is, and it is the best-documented popular movement in medieval Denmark.

Absalon became archbishop of Lund in 1178. He was a Zealander; he appointed Zealanders as his agents in Skåne; he enforced the bishop's third of the tithe, which had been let slide; and he required carting duty from the peasantry, including hauling material for building work. In 1180 Skåne rose, and Absalon had to leave for Zealand.

Vignette · a decision at the assembly

The Skåne rising is not a riot. It is a decision, taken in the proper place by the proper procedure, and that is what makes it interesting.

The farmers meet first at their local assemblies and then at the landsting at Lund. They debate. They resolve, collectively, that the bishop's tithe will not be paid — and the resolution binds everyone in the assembly's district, with penalties fixed at the assembly for anyone who breaks ranks. They then choose a spokesman to put the case to the king: a man named Harald Skrænk, well enough connected to get a hearing, and — as the sources note with some alarm — a possible alternative king if it came to that.

This is a legal culture doing what it was built to do. The men who refused Absalon's tithe were not rejecting authority; they were using one authority, the assembly, against another, the archbishop. It is the same institution that will produce Jyske Lov in chapter 14, and the same reflex that Danish political history keeps returning to.

Harald Skrænk and the farmers of Skåne · the landsting at Lund · 1180–1182

It took the king's army to put down. Valdemar attempted a settlement in 1181 and then imposed one by force; there was serious fighting, and it flared again after Valdemar's death in 1182, when Skåne refused to acclaim his son. The eventual terms are the interesting part. The Zealander agents stayed. And Absalon did not get his bishop's tithe.

So they lost, and they won. The most powerful man in Denmark after the king, backed by the crown and by an army, was unable to collect a tax that canon law said he was owed, because the population of a province had decided at a lawful assembly not to pay it. Hold that against the picture of the Valdemar age as a triumph of centralised royal power, because both things are true at once.

One more thing the rising exposes. The chroniclers are clear that Jutland magnates sympathised with the Skåne farmers against the Zealanders. Denmark in 1180 is three lands with three assemblies, three legal traditions and a good deal of mutual suspicion, held together by a dynasty. Saxo hoped that fighting the Wends together would make one country of it. It did not, quite — and it is worth remembering that landsmand in this period still meant a man from your own land, not from your own kingdom.

08 / NARRATIVESaxo

Danish terms in this section

Gesta Danorum
'The deeds of the Danes' — sixteen books of Latin, and the foundation of written Danish history.
Amleth
The prince of Jutland in Saxo's third book, who feigns madness to avenge his murdered father. Shakespeare got him at two removes.

Nearly every specific thing on this page comes, ultimately, from one man, and the chapter would be dishonest if it did not stop and say who he was.

Saxo — he acquired the nickname Grammaticus, the learned, three centuries later — was a cleric in Absalon's household. Around 1190 Absalon commissioned him to write a history of the Danes. He worked at it for perhaps twenty years, finishing under Absalon's nephew Anders Sunesen, and produced sixteen books of ambitious, difficult, deliberately classical Latin: the first eight on the legendary past, the last eight on the historical kings, and the final books on the Valdemars, whom he had watched.

What Saxo gave Denmark is enormous. Before him the country had annals, a short chronicle and some saints' lives. After him it had a national history in the register of Livy, with Danish kings as serious historical actors and a Danish past worth taking as seriously as Rome's. He also preserved, in book three, the story of a Jutland prince who feigns madness to avenge his father — Amleth, who reaches Shakespeare through a sixteenth-century French retelling.

And he was a house historian. Absalon commissioned the book, appears in it constantly, and comes out of it magnificently. The Hvide family are the heroes. Valdemar's oath to Barbarossa is handled delicately. The Wends are treacherous by nature, which is convenient for a book that must justify twenty-five years of campaigns against them. Where Saxo can be checked against foreign sources — the German chroniclers, or Henry of Livonia — he is often at variance, and the variance usually runs in his patrons' favour.

This is not a reason to discard him. It is a reason to read him the way you would read any official history: excellent on what happened, unreliable on why, and most suspect exactly where he is most stirring. The series has said the same about Adam of Bremen and about Ælnoth, and it is the single most useful habit a reader of medieval Danish history can acquire.

The manuscript very nearly did not survive. Gesta Danorum was known in the Middle Ages, then largely lost; it was printed in Paris in 1514 by Christiern Pedersen — the same Christiern Pedersen who will appear at the end of this page for quite another reason — working from a manuscript that has since disappeared. Four leaves in what may be Saxo's own hand turned up in Angers in 1863.

09 / NARRATIVEThe north German years

Knud 6. succeeded in 1182, the crowned child of Ringsted now nineteen. Advised by Absalon, he refused to renew his father's oath to the emperor. In 1185 he took the title rex Danorum Slavorumque — king of the Danes and the Wends — after Pomerania submitted. The expansion had stopped being raiding and become rule.

His brother Valdemar 2., called Sejr, the Victorious, succeeded in 1202 and pushed it as far as it would go. Holstein was taken. Hamburg was taken. Lübeck accepted Danish overlordship. And in 1214 the Emperor Frederick II, who needed Danish support in his own succession war and had nothing to lose by giving away land he did not control, formally ceded to Valdemar everything north of the Elbe and the Elde.

This is the largest extent of Danish power between Cnut and the Kalmar Union. From Hamburg to Estonia, the southern Baltic ran on Danish terms.

It rested on almost nothing. There was no Danish administration in Holstein or Mecklenburg beyond the local counts who had submitted and would unsubmit the moment it was safe. There was no shared law, no shared church province, no settlement of Danes. It was a structure of personal oaths to one man, exactly like Cnut's North Sea Empire in chapter 11 — and, as in chapter 11, the question was never whether it would come apart but when.

10 / NARRATIVEReval, 15 June 1219

Danish terms in this section

Lyndanisse
The headland on the Estonian coast where Valdemar landed and built a castle. The Estonian name for the town that grew there, Tallinn, is usually read as Taani linn — 'Danish castle'.
Dannebrog
The Danish flag. The name is first attested in a source of 1380; the second element means a piece of cloth.

Estonia was worth having for reasons that had little to do with Estonia. The trade routes to Novgorod and the Russian interior ran that way, and the Germans at Riga were already moving up the coast. Estonian raiders had also been attacking Skåne and Blekinge. Danish expeditions had been going east since 1206.

In June 1219 Valdemar landed at Lyndanisse with a large fleet, accompanied by Archbishop Anders Sunesen and several bishops, and began building a castle. On 15 June the Estonians attacked the camp from several directions at once and very nearly destroyed it. The Danes held, counter-attacked, and won. Northern Estonia became Danish, and stayed Danish for a hundred and twenty-seven years, until Valdemar Atterdag sold it to the Teutonic Order in 1346.

That is the battle. The other thing that is supposed to have happened at Lyndanisse is worth setting out carefully, because it is the most famous story in Danish history.

The legend says that at the critical moment a red banner with a white cross came down out of the sky, a voice promised victory under it, and the Danes rallied. Some versions add Anders Sunesen holding his arms up in prayer, in the manner of Moses at Rephidim, the Danes advancing while his arms are up and falling back when they drop.

The oldest surviving accounts of the falling flag are two Danish histories written in the 1520s, by Christiern Pedersen and by the Franciscan Peder Olsen — three hundred years after the event. Both place it not at Lyndanisse in 1219 but at Fellin, in central Estonia, in 1208. They do not appear to be copying each other, and Peder Olsen cites an older Latin source, now lost. Peder Olsen then changed his mind: a miracle that important, he reasoned, must belong to the important battle, and he moved it to Lyndanisse. Arild Huitfeldt followed him around 1600, and Huitfeldt's history became the foundation of everything written afterwards.

Meanwhile the contemporary account of the Estonian campaigns — Henry of Livonia's chronicle, written by a man who was in the country at the time — describes the fighting and mentions no flag at all.

Meanwhile in the world

1219 sits in a crowded decade. In 1214 the battle of Bouvines settled the balance of western Europe and made Frederick II's position, which is why he could afford to give Valdemar the Elbe lands. In 1215 the Fourth Lateran Council legislated for the whole church — annual confession, clerical discipline, and the machinery of crusade — and in the same year an English king was made to seal Magna Carta. In 1206 Temüjin had been proclaimed Chinggis Khan; by 1219 Mongol armies were in Central Asia, and within twenty years they would reach Hungary and Poland, two hundred kilometres from territory Valdemar had claimed.

Anders Sunesen, the archbishop of the Moses story, was a serious intellectual: he had studied in Paris, Bologna and Oxford, wrote a long theological poem, and produced the Latin paraphrase of the Skånske Lov that is a principal source for early Danish law. He resigned in 1223, a leper, and died on Ivø in Skåne.

11 / NARRATIVELyø, a night in May 1223

Danish terms in this section

Lyø
A small island in the South Funen archipelago, about four kilometres long, with good hunting.
løsesum
Ransom. Valdemar's was among the largest paid by any European ruler in the thirteenth century.

The empire did not fall to an invasion. It fell because of a hunting trip.

Vignette · the night on Lyø

On the night of 6–7 May 1223 the king of Denmark is asleep on a small island off the south coast of Funen, where he has been hunting. With him is his eldest son and co-king, also called Valdemar. The retinue is small — this is a private outing in the safest part of the kingdom, two hundred kilometres from any frontier.

One of the guests is Henry, count of Schwerin, a German vassal with a grievance about inheritance and about a wife's lands. In the dark his men take the king and his son, get them into a boat, and are away before the coast can be roused. By morning both kings of Denmark are on a ship heading south, and there is nobody in the country with the authority to order anything.

He was held for nearly three years. The negotiations ran through the Emperor and the Pope; the Danish army sent to recover him was beaten at Mølln in 1225. He came home in December 1225 having agreed to give up every conquest south of the Eider except Rügen, to pay an enormous ransom in silver, and to leave his younger sons behind as hostages.

Valdemar Sejr and Henry of Schwerin · Lyø, in the South Funen archipelago · the night of 6–7 May 1223

What the kidnapping exposes is the same weakness the last section described. An empire held together by personal oaths to one man has exactly one point of failure, and a minor count with a grudge and thirty men found it.

Valdemar did try to get out of it. The Pope, reasonably enough, absolved him of an oath extracted under duress — a promise made in captivity was not binding. He raised an army and went south to take it all back.

12 / NARRATIVEBornhöved, and the book

On 22 July 1227, at Bornhöved in Holstein, a coalition of north German princes and the city of Lübeck met the Danish army and destroyed it. Valdemar himself was wounded and got away with difficulty. Everything between the Eider and the Elbe was gone, permanently. Rügen was kept. Estonia, being across the sea and nobody's priority, was kept too.

Denmark would not hold territory south of the Eider again until 1460, and would not be a Baltic great power again until the Kalmar Union. The date is a real hinge, and it is worth noticing that it is barely known outside specialist circles, while 1219 — a battle in Estonia that changed rather less — is a public holiday.

Valdemar Sejr lived another fourteen years after Bornhöved, and they were not idle. Deprived of an empire, he turned to the kingdom, and what came out of those years is the reason his reign is remembered as a high point rather than a catastrophe. The provincial laws were written down — the Skånske Lov, the Sjællandske Lov, and, in the last year of his life, the Jyske Lov, which chapter 14 opens with. Towns received privileges. His son Abel was made duke of Schleswig, an arrangement that will cause two centuries of trouble.

And around 1231 somebody in the royal chancery, probably working at Sorø, compiled a survey of what the king owned and what the kingdom owed.

Kong Valdemars Jordebog is a hundred and fifty-three leaves of parchment, and it is one of the strangest and most valuable objects in Danish history. It lists the crown's estates. It lists the income due from each district. It lists, land by land, the ledingsafgift — that annual payment from section 06. It records hundreds of Danish place names in writing for the first time, and a good many Estonian ones. It is not a census and it is not a budget in any modern sense; historians from Kristian Erslev onwards have argued about what exactly it was for, and the manuscript is a compilation of several different texts rather than one document.

But look at what it presupposes. Somebody could ask the question what does the king have, and what is owed to him, everywhere in the realm — and expect a written answer. In 1050, at the start of chapter 12, there was no institution in Denmark capable of asking that question, let alone answering it. There was a fleet, a levy, and a king who had to be personally present for anything to happen.

That is the real inheritance of these two chapters, and it is not the empire. The empire lasted thirteen years and died on a July afternoon in Holstein. What survived was smaller and duller and enormously more durable: a diocesan grid, two thousand parish churches, a tax that arrived every year whether or not the king was winning, a written law for each of the three lands, a national history in sixteen books, and a ledger recording what was owed and by whom.

The next chapter opens in 1241 with the first sentence of the Jyske Lov, which every Dane can still quote, and with the king who commissioned it dead within weeks of its promulgation. His sons then spend a century tearing the whole thing apart.

Myth-check

"Dannebrog fell from the sky at Lyndanisse on 15 June 1219."
The two oldest surviving versions of the story, written in the 1520s by Christiern Pedersen and Peder Olsen, both put the falling flag at Fellin in 1208 — not Lyndanisse, and not 1219. Peder Olsen moved it himself, on the reasoning that so great a miracle belonged to so great a battle, and Arild Huitfeldt canonised the change around 1600. Henry of Livonia, who was in the country at the time, describes the campaign and mentions no flag. The name Dannebrog first appears in a source of 1380, and the oldest picture of it is in a Dutch armorial of the 1370s or 1380s. What is true is that a red banner with a white cross is a crusader's banner, and that Danish kings of this period were crusaders.
"Absalon founded Copenhagen in 1167."
He was granted an existing village called Havn and built a castle beside it. Excavation has found a settlement there with a market and a church well before he arrived. He gave it a fortress and a landlord; the harbour, the herring and the Sound gave it everything else.
"Valdemar den Store made Denmark independent and strong."
He was called the Great long after his death. In 1162 he swore fealty to the Holy Roman Emperor and held Denmark as an imperial fief — the one thing the whole Danish twelfth century was supposedly about avoiding. It was his son, on Absalon's advice, who refused to renew it.
"The Valdemar age was the triumph of central royal power over the people."
In 1180 the farmers of Skåne met at their lawful assembly, resolved not to pay the archbishop's tithe, drove him out of the province, and in the end were not made to pay it. It took the royal army to restore order and the settlement still went partly their way. Strong monarchy and effective popular resistance are both in this period, at the same time.
"Estonia was a Danish colony."
Denmark held northern Estonia from 1219 to 1346 through a handful of castles, a bishop and local vassals, with almost no Danish settlement. It was sold to the Teutonic Order for cash when it became inconvenient. Whether that makes it a colony is a fair question — it is worth asking it here, and then asking it again at the Atlantic slave trade (27).

Summary

IF YOU REMEMBER FIVE THINGSThe page in five

  1. At Ringsted in June 1170, Knud Lavard was made a saint and Valdemar's seven-year-old son was crowned in the first Danish coronation. In one afternoon the dynasty became holy, hereditary and anointed.

  2. Twenty-five years of campaigns against the Wends culminated at Arkona on Rügen in 1169, where the temple of Svantevit was destroyed. Whether these were crusades or conquests is a live argument; twelfth-century people did not experience it as a choice.

  3. The naval levy was cut to a quarter after 1169 and then commuted into an annual cash payment. The Danish crown finally had a regular tax, and it got there by converting a duty rather than imposing a new burden.

  4. In 1180 Skåne resolved at its assembly not to pay Absalon's tithe, drove him out, and in the settlement was not made to pay it. Royal power and popular resistance are both real in this period.

  5. Valdemar Sejr held everything from the Elbe to Estonia by 1219, was kidnapped from a hunting trip in 1223, and lost the entire southern empire at Bornhöved in 1227. What survived the collapse was written: the provincial laws, Saxo's history, and Kong Valdemars Jordebog.

Questions & discussion

FOUR KINDSWork the material

Recall

Did the facts land?

  • What two things happened at Ringsted on 25 June 1170, and why did each matter?
  • What was destroyed at Arkona in 1169, and what happened to the island afterwards?
  • What was a skipæn, and what replaced the duty to supply its ship?
  • What did the farmers of Skåne refuse to pay in 1180, and who did they choose to speak for them?
  • Who commissioned Saxo, and how does that show in the text?
  • How did Valdemar Sejr come to be a prisoner in Germany for three years?

Causal

Why did that follow from this?

  • Why does crowning a child in his father's lifetime change the nature of the monarchy?
  • Why did cutting the fleet to a quarter of its size make it more useful?
  • Why does commuting the leding into money eventually produce a legally privileged nobility?
  • Why did an empire stretching from Hamburg to Estonia collapse because of one kidnapping, when Denmark itself did not?
  • Why is a brick wall at the Danevirke, built by a king campaigning eastward, the most revealing building project of the reign?

Counterfactual

Pull one thread and see what moves.

  • Suppose Henry of Schwerin's men had missed their boat on Lyø. Does Denmark hold the Elbe lands — and does a Danish-run southern Baltic prevent the Hanse?
  • Suppose Absalon had won in Skåne in 1180 and collected his tithe in full. Does that make the Danish church stronger, or does it make the assemblies more determined, and does chapter 14 look different?
  • Suppose Saxo had never been commissioned. What would we not know — and how much of what we think we know would we not falsely believe?

Contested

Where historians actually disagree.

  • Were the Baltic campaigns crusades? Kurt Villads Jensen has argued that they were, in the full sense, and that the older Danish reading of them as national expansion in religious dress is an anachronism. What kind of evidence could settle a question about motive at eight hundred years' distance?
  • Does the Dannebrog legend have any thirteenth-century root at all, or is it entirely a creation of about 1500 — perhaps by clergy wanting to strengthen the crown, perhaps prompted by the royal banner lost in Ditmarsken in 1500? The Fellin/1208 problem is the crux.
  • Did the leding reform of 1169 happen as Saxo describes? He is the only source, he was writing for the reformer's family, and the law texts that describe the system are fifty years later.
  • What is Kong Valdemars Jordebog actually for? A tax register, a private royal memorandum, a compilation made for some other purpose entirely — the argument runs from Erslev in the 1870s to the present, and how much of a functioning state you read into the period depends on the answer.
  • Was the Skåne rising of 1180–82 about the tithe, about forced labour, or about Zealanders running Skåne? The sources are Zealand sources, and each reading produces a different picture of what medieval Danish farmers could and could not do.

Sources

LIGHT SOURCINGWhat this is built on

  • Saxo, Gesta Danorum, c. 1190–1208 The main source for almost everything here, written in Absalon's household, with Absalon as its hero. Indispensable and partisan in equal measure.
  • Henry of Livonia, Chronicon Livoniae, c. 1227 A contemporary in the country during the Estonian campaigns, writing from the German side. The check on Saxo, and the reason the Dannebrog story is in trouble.
  • Kong Valdemars Jordebog, c. 1231 The crown's survey of its own income. In the Rigsarkivet, and the single most important source for Danish society in this period.
  • danmarkshistorien.dk Aarhus University. Absalon, the Valdemars, Dannebrog, the Skåne risings, and the Wendish campaigns.
  • Kurt Villads Jensen, on the Baltic crusades The case that these campaigns were crusades in the full sense, and the current centre of gravity of the argument.
  • Skånske Lov & Anders Sunesen's paraphrase The oldest of the Danish provincial laws, with a Latin commentary by the archbishop who was at Lyndanisse.

Go and look

STILL THEREPlaces you can visit

  • Sankt Bendts Kirke, Ringsted Where the double ceremony of 1170 took place. Knud Lavard, Valdemar den Store, Knud 6. and Valdemar Sejr are all buried here — the dynasty's own church, in brick, and one of the oldest brick buildings in Denmark.
  • Sorø Klosterkirke The Hvide family's monastery. Absalon's grave is behind the altar, and the Jordebog was probably compiled somewhere in this building.
  • Ruinerne under Christiansborg, Copenhagen Absalon's castle of 1167 is in the basement, along with its successor. Ten minutes and worth it, if only for the change of scale.
  • Vor Frue Kirke, Kalundborg Esbern Snare's five-towered church, c. 1170. There is nothing else like it in Scandinavia.
  • Danevirke, Schleswig The Valdemarsmur — the brick facing of about 1170 — is exposed in places, and the museum is on the rampart. The most eloquent single site of the southern border thread.
  • Kap Arkona, Rügen The headland is still there, though the sea has taken much of the site. The temple platform is a grassed enclosure on a cliff.
  • Toompea, Tallinn The upper town on the site of the 1219 castle, in a city whose name most likely means 'Danish castle', with a Danish King's Garden below it.
