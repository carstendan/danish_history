First farmers and the megalith builders

c. 3,950 – 1,700 BCE · Neolithic · bondestenalderen · yngre stenalder

The most consequential two hundred years in Danish prehistory, followed by the largest building programme the country would see before the medieval churches — and then by a second replacement of the entire population.

Introduction

WHAT THIS PAGE ANSWERSFive questions

  1. What actually happened around 3,950 BCE — did Danes become farmers, or did farmers become Danes?
  2. How does a scattered population of early farmers build tens of thousands of stone monuments, and why do they stop?
  3. Why does the ice-margin line from chapter 1 reappear as a cultural frontier three thousand years later?
  4. What happened around 2,850 BCE, and why is it the single most uncomfortable fact in Danish prehistory?
  5. What of this period is still standing in the landscape, and still in the population?

01 / NARRATIVETwo hundred years

Danish terms in this section

bondestenalderen
'The farmer Stone Age' — the Danish name for the Neolithic.
tragtbægerkulturen
The Funnel Beaker culture, c. 3,950–2,800 BCE, named after its flared-neck pots.
slebet flint
Polished flint — the ground and polished axe that defines the period.
landnam
'Land-taking': the first large-scale clearance of forest for farming.

Chapter 2 ended with a refusal. For something like a thousand years, farmers lived a few hundred kilometres south of the Danish coast and the people here declined to join them, while cheerfully borrowing their pottery.

Then, around 3,950 BCE, it stops. Within a couple of centuries there are cattle and sheep in Denmark, barley and wheat in cleared plots, timber longhouses, polished flint axes, funnel-necked pots, and — very quickly — earthen long barrows raised over the dead. The oyster middens thin out. The whole material world changes at once.

Danish archaeologists have argued about this for a century and a half, and the argument had a recognisable shape: was this a local population adopting new ideas, or new people arriving? The respectable modern answer, for most of the twentieth century, was ideas. Pots are not people, went the slogan; cultures spread by imitation and trade, and the invasion-happy prehistory of the nineteenth century was best left buried.

That answer is no longer tenable, and the evidence that broke it is Danish.

02 / NARRATIVEWho arrived — and what became of the people already here

Danish terms in this section

aDNA
Ancient DNA — genetic material recovered from archaeological bone and teeth.
befolkningsudskiftning
Population replacement or turnover.
anatolsk herkomst
Anatolian-derived ancestry — the genetic signature of the first European farmers, out of what is now Turkey.

In January 2024 a group led from the Lundbeck Foundation GeoGenetics Centre in Copenhagen published one hundred sequenced ancient genomes from Danish skeletons spanning 7,300 years, combined with isotope data for diet and mobility and with pollen records for vegetation. It is the densest such dataset assembled for any single country, and it was published as one of four linked papers in Nature on the same day.

The result for this chapter is stark. The Mesolithic population of chapters 1 and 2 — Maglemose, Kongemose and Ertebølle — forms a single genetic cluster, essentially stable from about 8,500 BCE right through to the end. Three archaeological cultures, three changes of material world, one population.

Then, at roughly 3,950 BCE, farmers with Anatolian-derived ancestry arrive, and the local hunter-gatherer contribution to what follows is small. The transition, delayed by more than a millennium relative to central Europe, turns out to have been abrupt when it came, and to have involved less genetic mixing than in southern Europe. The incoming farmers did carry some hunter-gatherer ancestry — but they had picked it up generations earlier, in central Europe, on the way north.

Then it happens again. The Funnel Beaker population lasts about a thousand years and is in turn replaced, around 2,850 BCE, by people carrying ancestry derived from the Pontic-Caspian steppe — the Yamnaya-related expansion that swept across Europe and, in Denmark, produced the Single Grave culture. That second turnover is as rapid and as complete as the first, and its genetic profile is substantially the one modern Danes carry.

It is worth being careful about what this does and does not mean. "Population replacement" is a statement about ancestry proportions in the skeletons we can sample, not a description of an event. It does not by itself tell us whether people were killed, displaced, absorbed at very low rates, or simply out-reproduced by incomers with a faster demographic cycle. Farming populations grow quickly; foraging populations do not. A few centuries of differential growth can produce a near-total ancestry shift with no violence at all.

What it does rule out is the comfortable twentieth-century picture in which the Ertebølle people looked over the fence, liked what they saw, and became farmers. Most of them did not become anything. Their descendants are not, in the main, us.

Meanwhile in Europe

Denmark is late. Farming reached Greece around 6,500 BCE, the Rhine by 5,300, Britain and Ireland at almost exactly the same moment as Denmark, around 4,000. The north-western fringe of Europe converts as a block after a long pause — which suggests the pause had a common cause, and that whatever ended it ended it everywhere at once.

The megalithic tradition Denmark joins is older than Denmark's part of it: monumental stone tombs begin in Brittany around 4,500 BCE, in Ireland, Scotland and Wales after 3,800. Danish building starts around 3,500. Whether the idea spread by sea along the Atlantic façade or arose repeatedly is still argued.

03 / NARRATIVELandnam: unmaking the forest

Danish terms in this section

landnam
Land-taking; in Danish pollen science, a recognisable clearance horizon in the record.
elmefaldet
'The elm fall' — a sharp drop in elm pollen around 4,000 BCE, visible across northern Europe.
svedjebrug
Slash-and-burn cultivation.
ard
The ard or scratch-plough, which breaks the soil without turning it.

The arrival of farming is visible in pollen cores before it is visible in artefacts. Elm pollen drops sharply around 4,000 BCE — elmefaldet, recorded right across northern Europe — and is followed by charcoal, by weeds of cultivation like plantain, and by cereal pollen. Then, in many cores, the forest partly recovers, and the sequence repeats.

Johannes Iversen, whom we met in chapter 1 naming the Bølling phase, identified this pattern in 1941 and called it landnam, borrowing the Old Norse word for the taking of land in Iceland. The interpretation was that farmers cleared a patch of forest by ringing and burning, cropped it for a few years until fertility fell, and moved on, letting the woodland close behind them.

Burning down a wood to see if it works

Iversen was not content to infer. In the 1950s he and colleagues went into Draved Skov in southern Jutland — one of the last patches of something like ancient woodland in the country — and cleared a plot of it the Neolithic way. They felled trees with hafted flint axes, let the timber dry, burned the plot, and sowed grain in the ash.

Two things came out of it. The flint axes worked far better than anyone had expected; a polished flint axe is not a toy, and a competent user could fell a substantial tree in minutes. And the pollen signature the experiment produced in the local soil could be compared directly with the signature in the ancient cores.

It is one of the founding experiments of what is now called experimental archaeology, and it belongs to a distinctively Danish habit visible throughout this series: when the evidence will not speak, go outside and do the thing.

Johannes Iversen and colleagues · Draved Skov, 1950s

The picture has been refined since. The elm fall is now widely thought to owe something to disease — an early elm bark beetle outbreak, analogous to twentieth-century Dutch elm disease — rather than being purely the work of axes. And the neat cycle of clearance and abandonment has given way to a messier one, with permanent fields in some places and shifting plots in others. But the large fact stands: within a few centuries of 3,950 BCE, people began removing the primeval forest of chapter 2, and they never stopped. Denmark's landscape has been a human artefact ever since.

Two technologies made it stick. The polished flint axe cleared the ground. The ard, arriving in this period, broke it: a wooden scratch-plough dragged by oxen, which does not turn a furrow but scores the soil in criss-crossing passes. Ard marks survive, beautifully, under the sealed earth of burial mounds — the last ploughing of a field before someone built a monument on top of it.

04 / NARRATIVEThe farm

Danish terms in this section

langhus
Longhouse — the post-built farmhouse, two-aisled with a central row of roof-bearing posts.
emmer / enkorn
Emmer and einkorn, the first wheats; hulled, and threshed with difficulty.
nøgen byg
Naked barley — the cereal that comes to dominate Danish fields.
stolpehul
Post-hole: the dark stain in the subsoil that is usually all that survives of a house.

The monuments are what survive above ground, and they distort the picture. Most people in this chapter spent their lives on a farm, and the farm is much harder to see.

What survives is stolpehuller — patches of darker soil where the roof posts rotted in place. From these, excavation recovers ground plans: rectangular, post-built houses, mostly two-aisled, with a line of heavy posts down the middle carrying the ridge. Early Neolithic houses are modest, ten to twenty metres long. At Ullerødgård near Hillerød a scatter of house sites sits around a former wetland within a couple of hectares. This is not a village in any later sense; it is a loose cluster of farmsteads, and the pattern across most of the period is dispersed settlement rather than nucleated ones.

The interpretive traps here are real. Barkær on Djursland, dug in the 1930s by P.V. Glob, was for decades presented as the oldest village in Denmark — two enormous longhouses side by side, and a Danish answer to the Danubian longhouses of central Europe. Barkær is now read as a pair of long barrows. The postholes were real; the houses were an inference.

The crops were emmer and einkorn wheat and naked barley, sown in small plots and reaped with flint sickles. Barley gradually wins, as it will keep doing in Danish agriculture for the next five thousand years, because it tolerates poor soil and a short season. Livestock meant cattle above all — for meat, for milk, and, once the ard arrives, for traction — with sheep, goats and pigs alongside. Hunting and fishing do not disappear, but they stop being the point.

That shift is measurable. The isotope data in the 2024 genome study tracks the change in what people ate directly, and it moves in step with the change in ancestry: the marine-heavy diet of chapter 2 gives way to a terrestrial, agricultural one at the same moment the population changes. Genotype, diet and land use shift together, which is precisely what makes the case for incoming people rather than converted locals so difficult to argue away.

By the end of the period the houses have grown considerably. At Limensgård on Bornholm, house after house was built on very nearly the same spot from the Middle Neolithic through to the early Bronze Age — generations of rebuilding in place, which implies inherited rights to a specific piece of ground. That idea, that a family holds a named farm and passes it on, is arguably the single most durable institution this chapter produces. It outlasts the megaliths.

05 / NARRATIVEThe megalith explosion

Danish terms in this section

dysse
Dolmen: a closed stone chamber under a mound, round (runddysse) or long (langdysse).
jættestue
Passage grave — literally 'giant's room' — a larger chamber entered by a stone-built passage.
storstensgrav
'Great-stone grave': the collective Danish term for both.
randsten
Kerbstones: the ring or rectangle of upright stones edging the mound.
kulthus
Cult house: a small non-domestic building beside a grave group.

Then, from about 3,500 BCE, the country fills with stone.

First dysser — chambers of a few great boulders, roofed with a capstone, set in a round or rectangular mound edged with kerbstones. They held one body or a few. Then, from around 3,400, jættestuer: bigger chambers, often rectangular, entered by a built passage from the long side so that they could be opened and re-entered. These are collective tombs. Some have held towards a hundred individuals.

The numbers are the hard part, because most of these monuments are gone — quarried for road metal, field walls and foundations, or simply ploughed flat. Roughly 2,400 to 2,500 survive as visible monuments today. Some 7,000 are on record in one form or another. Local intensive surveys, where archaeologists have walked every field in a small district, consistently find far more than the registers list, and the estimate in Jørgen Jensen's standard work for the original number in Denmark as a whole is on the order of 40,000.

Take a moment with that figure. Forty thousand stone monuments, in a country of this size, built by a scattered farming population within a few hundred years. Each one required finding, moving and setting boulders weighing several tonnes, with rollers, levers, ramps of earth and a great many people pulling. At the peak there must have been building sites visible from almost anywhere in the settled landscape. Nothing on this scale would be attempted again in Denmark until the parish churches went up in the twelfth century — and there are about 1,800 of those.

How they were built is partly recoverable. The stones are glacial erratics — the boulders chapter 01 watched the ice drop across eastern Denmark — sometimes used whole, sometimes split. Moving them needed levers, rollers, ropes and a great many people; raising a capstone onto uprights was generally done by building an earth ramp against the chamber, hauling the stone up it, and then digging the ramp away. The dry-stone packing between the uprights, and the birch-bark sealing found in some chambers, show that these were meant to be watertight rooms, not piles of rock.

And they were used, repeatedly, for centuries. A passage grave can be opened; that is the point of the passage. Bones inside were pushed aside and stacked to make room, so that what excavators find is rarely a tidy body but a chamber's worth of accumulated ancestors. Outside the entrance, layer upon layer of deliberately smashed pottery built up, renewed over generations — the debris of ceremonies conducted at the door rather than inside it. Later people kept coming: Bronze Age burials were inserted into these chambers a thousand years after they were built, and occasionally Viking Age ones three thousand years after.

Some of the tombs were finished with a facing of white burnt flint, which would have made them blaze against green country. They were not modest.

06 / NARRATIVESarup: where everyone came together

In the 1970s, excavations at Sarup on south-west Funen revealed something nobody had a name for: a large enclosure marked out with palisades and systems of interrupted ditches, on a promontory, built and used intensively for a short period and then deliberately closed. Similar sites — now called Sarup-anlæg after the type site — have since been identified at over twenty places across Denmark. They are all built within roughly 3,400 to 3,100 BCE. The smallest, at Bjerggård east of Horsens, covers 1.6 hectares; the largest, such as Lokes Hede near Hobro, run to more than twenty.

What went on at them is inferred from what was put in the ditches: broken pottery, burnt flint, animal bone, fragments of human bone. The best reading is that these were periodic gathering places for the population of a whole district — for feasting, for exchange, and for handling the dead before their final burial in the tombs.

The clinching detail is the local landscape. Around Sarup itself, within a few kilometres, are some twenty contemporary settlements and around 125 megalithic graves. This was not an isolated monument; it was the centre of a working system. And when the enclosures fall out of use around 3,200–3,100 BCE, the megalith building stops at the same moment. Whatever social machinery required people to assemble and to build together, it ran down all at once.

Twenty-eight pots on a floor

On a small plateau west of Tustrup on Norddjursland, within about a hundred metres of each other, stand two dolmens and one of the largest passage graves in Denmark. The tombs had been visible in the landscape since they were built. What nobody knew until the excavations of 1954–57 was what stood between them.

It was a building, roughly five by five and a half metres, horseshoe-shaped, with an open gable facing away from the graves, walls of man-high stones packed with smaller stone and plank, and no hearth. On its floor lay the sherds of twenty-eight fine pots — the funnel beakers and pedestalled bowls that give the culture its name — apparently standing there when the house was burnt down and abandoned.

It was the first kulthus ever identified. The usual reading is that the dead were housed here, and the ceremonies prepared, before final burial in the chamber a hundred metres away. Tustrup remains one of the few places in Denmark where you can stand inside a complete Neolithic ritual landscape rather than beside a single surviving stone.

07 / NARRATIVEFlint, amber and the first metal

Danish terms in this section

flintmine
Flint mine: shafts and galleries dug to reach unweathered flint in the chalk.
offerfund
Votive deposit — objects placed in a bog or lake as an offering.
tyndnakket økse
Thin-butted axe, the early Funnel Beaker form; later replaced by the thick-butted type.

Chapter 1 ended by noting that flint was the one thing this landscape had in quantity. In this chapter that becomes an industry. Funnel Beaker people did not simply pick flint off the fields; they mined it, sinking shafts into the chalk to reach unweathered nodules, and worked it into axes that were then ground and polished to a gloss the working edge does not require. Polishing an axe takes many hours. Some of the finest were never used.

The mines are real mines. At sites in northern Jutland and on the eastern chalk, shafts were sunk several metres into the chalk and galleries driven off them at the base, worked with picks of red deer antler, to reach flint that had never been exposed to frost. Surface flint is full of invisible cracks; mined flint is not, and a flintsmith making a metre-long ceremonial axe or, later, a dagger needs stone that will not betray him at the last blow. The distribution of the resulting axes shows them travelling well beyond the districts that produced them.

Instead they were given away — to bogs and lakes. Hoards of polished axes, sometimes many at a time, are a standing feature of the Danish Neolithic record, and the practice of placing valuable things in wet ground begins here and runs, astonishingly, for the next four thousand years. It will still be going on when the Gundestrup cauldron goes into a bog in chapter 5, and arguably when weapons go into Illerup Ådal in chapter 6.

Amber, which chapter 2 saw carved into small animals, is now strung in quantity and also deposited. And at the very end of the Funnel Beaker period the first metal appears in Denmark: copper, imported from central Europe. It changes nothing immediately. There is no ore here, and no smelting. What it does is establish a taste.

08 / NARRATIVECollapse

Around 2,800 BCE the Funnel Beaker world ends. The enclosures had already closed; the megaliths had already stopped going up. Now the settlement pattern breaks down, the pottery tradition fragments, and the pollen records over much of central and northern Europe show forest returning to land that had been cleared.

The 2024 Danish study puts a number against this. Its authors note the reforestation after about 3,000 BCE alongside other indicators of population decline, and suggest that the farming populations of central and northern Europe may already have been severely reduced before the newcomers with steppe ancestry arrived. Plague is a live candidate: Yersinia pestis has been recovered from Neolithic skeletons in Scandinavia from around this window.

The plague evidence is the part that has moved fastest. Yersinia pestis has now been recovered from Neolithic skeletons in Scandinavia dating to broadly this window, and recent work on a megalith-building population in southern Sweden has argued for repeated epidemic episodes running through several generations of the same communities — not one catastrophe but a series. If that holds, the Funnel Beaker world was being hollowed out from inside over a century or more.

It is worth being clear about what "collapse" does and does not mean, because the word carries more drama than the evidence. Nobody is describing an event. What the record shows is a set of indicators moving in the same direction over a long generation or two: the gathering enclosures going out of use around 3,200–3,100 BCE, the megalith building stopping at the same moment, the settlement pattern breaking up, the pottery losing its regional coherence, and pollen cores across northern and central Europe recording woodland reclaiming cleared ground.

Any one of those could be explained locally. Together they describe a farming society that had been expanding for eight hundred years and stopped — and then, for two or three centuries, went backwards.

Why is genuinely open. Soil exhaustion after generations of cropping the same ground is a standing candidate; so is the climate, which was cooling; so is the possibility that the social machinery which had made forty thousand tombs possible simply ran out of whatever it ran on. Plague is the newest candidate and the most testable, because it leaves DNA.

What the sequence rules out is the comfortable version, in which a vigorous new people arrives and overwhelms a functioning one. The reforestation is already under way when the newcomers appear. It is not a conquest of a thriving society. It is a society that collapsed first, and a replacement that moved into the space.

09 / NARRATIVEThe second turnover: the Single Grave people

Danish terms in this section

enkeltgravskulturen
The Single Grave culture, c. 2,850–2,350 BCE — the Danish branch of the Corded Ware complex.
stridsøkse
Battle-axe: a shaft-hole stone axe, the signature male grave good.
snorekeramik
Corded ware — pottery decorated by pressing twisted cord into wet clay.
israndslinjen
The ice margin line — the same boundary as chapter 1's hovedopholdslinje.

What arrives is archaeologically unmistakable, and geographically very odd.

The dead are now buried one to a grave, under a small round barrow, lying on the side with the knees drawn up and the face turned south. Men lie on the right with the head west, often with a polished stone stridsøkse; women lie on the left with the head east. The pottery is beaker-shaped and decorated with impressed cord. There are thousands of these barrows, and Denmark's version of the phenomenon has more in common with north Germany and Poland than with the Swedish and Norwegian battle-axe cultures.

Here is the odd part. They are concentrated in central and western Jutland — and, as the standard reference puts it, most of them lie in the area west of the ice margin line.

The reading usually given is that the light western soils suited a herding economy that the heavy eastern clays did not, and that the newcomers took up land which the Funnel Beaker farmers had used lightly or abandoned. Something else supports the idea of two distinct groups: the Single Grave people appear not to have used the good flint sources the Funnel Beaker people had mined, and the Funnel Beaker people do not seem to have received amber from them. Two populations in one small country, not trading the two things the country was best supplied with.

Eastern Denmark and the islands are a different story, and a gentler one. There the old Funnel Beaker traditions persist for centuries alongside the new, the megalithic tombs go on being used for burials, and the material culture becomes a mixture. Archaeologists have generally read west Jutland as replacement and the islands as accommodation.

Five skeletons in a stone cist

For generations Danish archaeologists disagreed, in print and at length, about whether the Single Grave culture represented local people changing their habits or incomers arriving. The material evidence could be read either way, and both readings had distinguished defenders. The debate had the unpleasant additional weight that migration explanations in northern European prehistory had been thoroughly abused politically in the first half of the twentieth century, and a generation of scholars had good reason to distrust them.

At Gjerrild on Djursland there is a stone cist containing the largest and best-preserved collection of Single Grave human bone known in Denmark — five people, buried over about three hundred years between roughly 2,600 and 2,200 BCE. In 2021 a team sampled them.

The DNA preservation was poor, but sufficient. The Gjerrild people carried steppe ancestry. A century-old argument, which no amount of comparing pot decoration had been able to settle, was answered by five skeletons in a box of stones on a Jutland headland.

10 / NARRATIVEDagger time

Danish terms in this section

dolktid
'Dagger time' — the Late Neolithic, c. 2,350–1,700 BCE.
fladehugget flint
Pressure-flaked flint, worked in fine flat scars across the whole surface.
klokkebæger
Bell Beaker — the European pottery style whose influence reaches Jutland.
langhus
Longhouse: the two-aisled post-built farmhouse of the period.

The last six centuries of the Danish Stone Age are named after an object, and it is a strange one. The flintdolk is a dagger of flint, worked flat across both faces by pressure-flaking, sometimes with a carefully finished handle complete with a stitched-looking seam down the grip.

It is an imitation of a metal dagger. Copper and bronze daggers were circulating in Europe; a handful reached Denmark; and Danish flintsmiths — heirs to five thousand years of working the best flint in northern Europe — responded by making the same object out of stone, including the details that only make sense in metal. They are among the most technically accomplished flint objects ever made anywhere.

Read one way this is provincial: the periphery copying the centre in an inferior material. Read another way it is a display of virtuosity that metal could not match, in a country whose one competitive advantage was flint and whose craftsmen knew it. Both readings are probably true.

The rest of dagger time points forward. Bell Beaker influence arrives, strongest in northern Jutland, where settlements like Myrhøj and Bejsebakken produce beaker pottery and where richly equipped male graves with big daggers and concentrations of arrowheads suggest a warrior elite. Cereal farming intensifies again. Substantial post-built longhouses appear, some of them very large. And by the end of the period, metalworking finally takes root in southern Scandinavia.

At which point the Stone Age ends — not because the stone ran out, but because a society that had been importing metal for eight hundred years finally started making things from it. The Bronze Age (4) begins there.

Meanwhile in the world

While Danish farmers were raising dolmens, Egypt was being unified under a single king, writing was being invented independently in Mesopotamia and Egypt, and the cities of Uruk and later of the Indus valley were growing to tens of thousands of people. The first Danish written word is more than four thousand years away.

Stonehenge's main sarsen phase goes up around 2,500 BCE, near the start of Danish dagger time. Ötzi, the man frozen in an Alpine glacier with a copper axe, died around 3,300 BCE — in Danish terms during the megalith boom, and carrying the metal that Denmark was only just beginning to import.

11 / NARRATIVEWhat it cost

Farming is usually narrated as progress. The skeletal record from this period does not cooperate.

Compared with the Ertebølle people of chapter 2, Neolithic Danes were shorter, their bones less robust, and their teeth — now meeting cereals and gritty stone-ground flour — carried caries and heavy wear. Diet narrowed. Living close to livestock in permanent settlements is an efficient way to acquire and share disease, and the plague evidence from the end of the Funnel Beaker period is a direct expression of that.

Against this: far more people. That is the whole trade. Agriculture supports a population several times larger on the same ground, at a lower standard of health for the average individual. Every society in this series from now on is built on that bargain, and none of them get to unmake it.

Something else arrives with the second turnover that is not visible in the graves. The steppe-derived populations brought lactase persistence into the European gene pool — the ability to digest milk as an adult — though it took thousands of years to become common. They very probably also brought Indo-European speech: the ancestor of the language this page is not written in, and of Danish, which it is about. Danish archaeologists and linguists have pointed to exactly this window, around 2,800–2,600 BCE, when Funnel Beaker and Single Grave communities lived side by side in Denmark, as the kind of contact situation where words for local plants and animals could pass from an older language into the incoming one.

12 / NARRATIVEWhat we inherited

Three things survive this chapter into the present.

The first is the landscape. The forest never came back. What chapter 2 called urskov becomes, from here on, farmland with woods in it — and the division between the heavy clay east and the sandy west, already visible in Figure 3, structures Danish settlement and agriculture from now until the twentieth century.

The second is the monuments. Two and a half thousand dolmens and passage graves still stand, which makes them by a wide margin the oldest human structures in Denmark and among the oldest standing architecture anywhere in Europe. They were built before the pyramids. They were already ancient ruins when the Jelling stones were carved, and the Vikings of chapter 10 sometimes buried their own dead in them.

The monuments are the part you can still put your hand on. There are around two and a half thousand of them left, which means that in most of eastern Denmark you are rarely more than a few kilometres from one. Many stand in the corner of a working field, fenced off, with a plough furrow running to within a metre of the kerbstones — five thousand years old, and still slightly in the way.

Go inside one of the passage graves on Møn with a torch and the experience is not what the outside prepares you for. The passage is low enough to crawl, and then the chamber opens out and you can stand. The capstone above you weighs several tonnes and was lifted there by people using earth, rope and time. The dry-stone packing between the uprights is still doing its job. The chamber was built to be opened and re-entered, and it was, for centuries, by people bringing their dead to join the ones already there.

Nobody knows what was said in that room. But somebody swept it, and rearranged the bones to make space, and came back.

The third is the population. The genetic profile that arrives around 2,850 BCE is broadly the one Danes carry today. On the current evidence, if you want to know when the ancestors of modern Danes arrived in Denmark, the answer is not the Ice Age, and not the Vikings. It is here.

Myth-check

"Danes are descended from the Stone Age hunter-gatherers."
The population of what is now Denmark was substantially replaced twice within about eleven hundred years. The Ertebølle contribution to modern Danish ancestry is small.
"Farming spread as an idea; pots are not people."
A necessary corrective in its day, and wrong here. In Denmark both major cultural transitions of this period coincide with genetic turnover. This does not mean every prehistoric change was a migration — it means the question has to be asked case by case, and now can be.
"Population replacement means massacre."
It means an ancestry shift in the skeletons we can sample. Differential population growth, disease, and absorption at low rates can all produce the same signal. The evidence for collapse before the second turnover makes a simple conquest story less likely, not more.
"Jættestuer were built by giants — or by Vikings."
The Danish word means 'giant's room', which is what people assumed when nobody could imagine the labour. Vikings are four thousand years too late, though they did occasionally reuse the chambers for their own burials.
"Farming made people healthier."
It made more of them. Neolithic Danes were shorter than the foragers they replaced, with worse teeth, narrower diets and new epidemic disease.

Summary

IF YOU REMEMBER FIVE THINGSThe page in five

  1. Farming arrived around 3,950 BCE, abruptly, after a thousand-year refusal — and ancient DNA shows it arrived with people, not just with ideas.

  2. From about 3,500 BCE the Funnel Beaker culture built on the order of forty thousand megalithic tombs in a few centuries, plus more than twenty great gathering enclosures. Around 2,400–2,500 tombs still stand.

  3. Around 2,800 BCE that world collapsed — with evidence of depopulation and plague — and was replaced by people carrying steppe ancestry: the Single Grave culture, whose barrows cluster west of the old ice margin line.

  4. The Late Neolithic 'dagger time' produced flint imitations of metal daggers that are among the finest stone objects ever made, immediately before metalworking actually arrived.

  5. Modern Danish ancestry, the deforested landscape, and probably the Indo-European roots of the Danish language all date from this chapter rather than from anything earlier.

Questions & discussion

FOUR KINDSWork the material

Recall

Did the facts land?

  • What is the difference between a dysse and a jættestue, and what does the difference imply about how the dead were treated?
  • What is landnam, and how is it visible in a pollen core?
  • Roughly when do the two population turnovers happen, and where does each incoming group come from?
  • Where in Denmark are Single Grave barrows concentrated, and why is that location significant?
  • What is a flint dagger imitating?

Causal

Why did that follow from this?

  • Why would a scattered farming population invest so much labour in tombs — and why would they stop within a few generations?
  • Why does the megalith building stop at the same moment the gathering enclosures fall out of use? What does that suggest they were both for?
  • Why does a glacial boundary from 22,000 years ago end up separating two Neolithic cultures?
  • Why does agriculture make individuals less healthy and societies more powerful?

Counterfactual

Pull one thread and see what moves.

  • Suppose the Funnel Beaker world had not collapsed before 2,850 BCE. Does the steppe expansion still reach Denmark, and does it still replace the population?
  • Suppose Denmark had possessed copper ore. How much of chapters 3 and 4 changes?

Contested

Where archaeologists actually disagree.

  • Ancient DNA has restored migration to the centre of European prehistory after two generations in which it was treated with suspicion. Given how badly migration narratives were abused in the twentieth century, what obligations does that place on how the results are written up and reported?
  • "Population replacement" is a statement about ancestry proportions. What would it take to distinguish violence, displacement, disease and differential fertility in the archaeological record — and does the distinction matter morally, or only historically?
  • Was the elm fall caused by farmers or by disease, and how would you tell?
  • Denmark converted to farming at almost the same moment as Britain and Ireland after the same long pause. What common cause could produce that, and is the coincidence real or an artefact of how we date things?
