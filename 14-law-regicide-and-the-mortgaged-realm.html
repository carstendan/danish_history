Law, regicide and the mortgaged realm

1241 – 1340 · Jyske Lov · Abel · Jakob Erlandsen · Erik Klipping · Grev Gert

A century that opens with the most famous sentence in Danish law and ends with no king at all and the country in the hands of its creditors. Between the two, a king kills his brother, an archbishop is put in a fool's cap, and another king is stabbed fifty-six times in a barn.

Introduction

WHAT THIS PAGE ANSWERSFive questions

  1. Every Dane can quote the first line of Jyske Lov. What does it actually say, and what was it for?
  2. Denmark had slaves for a thousand years. Where and when did they stop — and was anyone freed?
  3. What did the charter of 1282 concede, and why is it in every Danish constitutional history?
  4. How does a kingdom end up pawned, piece by piece, to German counts — and was that a catastrophe or ordinary medieval finance?
  5. Niels Ebbesen killed a man in Randers in 1340 and became a national hero. Which of those two facts is the historical one?

01 / NARRATIVEVordingborg, March 1241

Danish terms in this section

Jyske Lov
The Law of Jutland, given 1241. The only one of the Danish provincial laws issued by a king, which makes it the only one that is legislation in the formal sense.
landskabslov
Provincial law. Denmark had several, not one: Jutland, Zealand and Skåne each had their own, and they stayed separate until 1683.
landsting
The assembly of a land, which was also its highest court.
fortale
Preamble. The famous sentence is the first line of one.

In March 1241, at Vordingborg, the old and dying Valdemar Sejr gave the Jutlanders a law. It was carried afterwards to the assembly at Viborg and adopted there. He was dead within weeks.

It opens: Mæt logh scal land byggæs — with law shall a land be built. The line is cut over the door of the old courthouse on Nytorv in Copenhagen and it is the one piece of medieval Danish that everybody knows.

Vignette · a preamble, read out

Imagine it being read aloud, because that is how it worked. Most of the men at the Viborg assembly could not read, and the law reached them as sound.

What they heard was not a list of rules. The preamble is an argument, and a rather beautiful one: that law exists so that the honest may live in peace and the dishonest be restrained; that the law must be honourable, just, tolerable, according to the custom of the country, suitable, and useful, and clear, so that all may know what it says; that the law is not made for any man's private advantage but for the good of all who live under it. It says that the king and the chieftains are the guardians of the law and must judge as the law directs. And it says that if there is any dispute about the law, the king shall decide it — with the best men of the land, and at the assembly.

Then, a hundred and sixty chapters later, it turns out to be mostly about boundary disputes, inheritance, cattle, and how many oath-helpers you need.

Gunner, bishop of Viborg, to whom the preamble is traditionally credited · the Viborg landsting, 1241

Two corrections to the usual telling, and both matter.

The first is that this is Jutland's law. Skåne had its own, written down between about 1202 and 1216 — one manuscript of it, the Codex Runicus of around 1300, is written entirely in runes. Zealand had two law-books. These others are formally private compilations rather than legislation, which is precisely why Jyske Lov is special: its preamble names the king who gave it. There was no single Danish law until Danske Lov in 1683. What did happen is that from the 1500s Jyske Lov acquired an authority beyond Jutland and came to be used as a reference by the highest courts — which is how a provincial code ended up carved over a Copenhagen doorway as though it had always been national.

The second is what the famous sentence is doing. It is the opening of a legal preamble, and its purpose is to say what law is for before setting out what it says. Read as a constitutional principle — the state bound by law, the king under the law — it is being asked to carry rather more than it was built to carry, and the same sentiment turns up in the Norwegian Frostating law, so it is not even uniquely Danish. Read as what it is, it is still remarkable: a thirteenth-century king publishing an argument that law must be clear enough for the people bound by it to know what it says.

02 / NARRATIVEThe last thralls

Danish terms in this section

træl
Thrall, slave. From Old Norse þræll. An owned person, and in law a piece of property.
landbo
A tenant farmer, holding land from an owner against an annual rent. The thirteenth-century forerunner of the fæstebonde.
landgilde
The annual rent a tenant paid, in produce or in money.
gårdsæde
A cottager with a house and little or no land, living by working for others. Where a good many freed thralls ended up.

Chapter 9 promised that this is where thralldom leaves Danish law, and it is — but not in the way the phrase suggests. Nobody abolished it. There is no decree, no proclamation, no date.

Jyske Lov, in 1241, still contains rules about thralls. It is among the last Danish texts that does. By 1300 the institution has effectively gone, and the sources simply stop mentioning it.

How many people this concerned is genuinely uncertain, and the estimates in the literature range from about a tenth of the population to a quarter — a spread wide enough that it is really an admission of ignorance. Nor do historians agree on what thralls mostly did: some read them as central to Danish agriculture, others as a smaller domestic and sexual servitude.

What is reasonably clear is why it ended, and the reasons are not flattering. The church had long disapproved of Christians owning Christians, and Absalon freed his thralls in his will in 1201. But the decisive pressures were economic. The supply dried up when the raiding stopped — chapter 9's slave trade was the source, and it had closed. Water mills took over work that human muscle had done. And, above all, the arithmetic changed: a growing population meant land was scarce and labour was not, so it became cheaper to let a farm to a tenant who fed and housed himself than to own a man and have to feed him whether or not there was work.

So the thralls became landboer — tenants, paying an annual rent for a farm they did not own. And here is the part that should be said plainly: their legal status improved and their lives did not. A tenant was free in law, could not be sold, could go to the assembly. He also owed rent every year, held his farm on a short lease at first, and had no land of his own. For the person at the bottom of Danish society, the end of slavery was a change of category rather than a change of condition.

Two thoughts to carry. The first is that this is what most historical change looks like — not a proclamation but a slow shift in what was profitable, invisible to the people living through it. The second is uglier. Denmark stopped holding slaves at home in the thirteenth century for reasons that had little to do with principle, and began buying and shipping them across the Atlantic in the seventeenth for reasons that had nothing to do with principle either. The Atlantic slave trade (27) is that story, and it is worth arriving there remembering this page.

03 / NARRATIVESlien, August 1250

Danish terms in this section

Plovpenning
'Plough-penny' — the tax on ploughs that Erik 4. imposed, and the nickname it earned him.
hertugdømmet Sønderjylland
The duchy of Schleswig, held from the crown by a branch of the royal family. From here on, a permanent problem.

Valdemar Sejr left three sons who each became king in turn, and the arrangement went wrong almost immediately. The eldest, Erik, got the crown. The second, Abel, got the duchy of Schleswig. The third, Christoffer, got neither and waited.

Erik taxed. His plough-tax gave him a nickname that has outlasted everything else about him, and it was resented in the way new taxes always were, because it was assessed on the instrument of production rather than on the yield. He and Abel fought, on and off, for nine years.

In August 1250 Erik was Abel's guest at Schleswig. He was taken from the house at night, rowed out onto the Slien, and beheaded in the boat; the body went into the water and was recovered later by fishermen. Abel swore an oath of innocence with twenty-four compurgators — the standard Danish procedure, in which a man clears himself by producing enough respectable people willing to swear alongside him — and was elected king.

He lasted two years. In 1252 he rode into the marshes to collect taxes from the Frisians and they killed him. Christoffer, the third brother, got his turn at last.

That is the fork. From 1250 the Danish royal house is two houses: the kings, descended from Christoffer, and the dukes of Schleswig, descended from Abel. The dukes hold a border province, marry into Holstein, and can call on German help whenever the king leans on them. Every crisis on this page runs along that seam, and so does 1848 and 1864.

04 / NARRATIVEAn archbishop in a fool's cap

Danish terms in this section

interdikt
Interdict: the suspension of church services across a whole territory. No mass, no marriage, no burial in consecrated ground. A weapon aimed at a ruler and landing on everybody.
bandlysning
Excommunication of a named person.
Danehof
The assembly of the realm's magnates and prelates, meeting with the king. It becomes central in the next section.

Chapter 12 ended with a warning that the church, once it existed as an institution, would turn out to have interests of its own. Chapter 13 showed Absalon as archbishop and royal foster-brother, the two powers in one man. Here they come apart.

Jakob Erlandsen became archbishop of Lund in 1254. He was a canon lawyer, formed by the high papal claims of the thirteenth century, and he held that the church in Denmark was answerable to Rome and not to the king: that clergy could not be tried in royal courts, that church property was not liable to royal taxes, and that the king had no say in who was elected bishop. Christoffer 1. held the opposite on all three.

Vignette · a bishop in a coat of mail, and a hat

The quarrel escalated on both sides. In 1256 the bishops meeting at Vejle agreed a decree: if the archbishop were seized, the whole kingdom would fall under interdict automatically, with no need to ask Rome. Jakob Erlandsen then refused to crown Christoffer's son.

In February 1259 the king had him arrested at Hagenskov on Funen. He was taken away in fetters, and — the detail everybody remembers — with a fool's cap of fox-tails on his head, so that the most senior churchman in Scandinavia should be seen to be ridiculous.

It did not work in the slightest. The interdict came down. The archbishop's ally, Prince Jaromar of Rügen, invaded Zealand. And in May 1259 Christoffer died suddenly at Ribe, after taking communion; the rumour that the wine had been poisoned by an abbot has never been proved and has never gone away. His widow spent years fighting the consequences, and Jakob Erlandsen ended in exile in Rome, where he died in 1274 still archbishop and still unreconciled.

Jakob Erlandsen, archbishop of Lund · Hagenskov, Funen · February 1259

Nobody won, which is the point. The Danish church did not get what Jakob Erlandsen wanted, and the crown did not get to treat bishops as royal officials. What both sides learned is that the other could not be simply overpowered — and the quarrel gave every subsequent opponent of the crown a template and an ally.

It also cost the monarchy something it could not spare, and it left the kingdom to a child.

Christoffer's son Erik Klipping was about ten when he inherited, and for the next seven years Denmark was governed by his mother, Margrete Sambiria — who is one of the more remarkable people in this band and gets roughly a line in most accounts of it. What she took over was a country under interdict, with its archbishop in exile appealing to Rome, an invasion from Rügen on Zealand, a war on the southern border and her son's title disputed. She was beaten in the field at Lohede in 1261 and taken prisoner along with the boy king. She got them both released, went on governing, and handed over a working monarchy in 1266. Afterwards she was granted northern Estonia as her dower and administered it, from Denmark, for the rest of her life. The byname she was given — Sprænghest, burst-horse — is usually taken as a comment on the pace at which she travelled.

The next section is what the magnates did with all this.

05 / NARRATIVENyborg, 1282

Danish terms in this section

håndfæstning
Literally 'hand-fastening' — a charter of conditions a king accepts, in Denmark usually as the price of being elected. The first is 1282; there will be one at almost every accession until 1660.
Danehof
The annual assembly of magnates and prelates that the 1282 charter promised.

In July 1282 at Nyborg, Erik Klipping put his seal to a document that every Danish constitutional history has to stop at.

It promised two things above all. That a Danehof — an assembly of the realm's great men — would meet every year. And that no man would be imprisoned unless he had first been lawfully convicted.

The second of those has an obvious English cousin, and Danish accounts have often reached for it: the 1282 charter as Denmark's Magna Carta, sixty-seven years late. The comparison is fair as far as it goes, and it should not be pushed. What the charter did was give the magnates a permanent forum and a procedural guarantee against exactly the kind of thing Christoffer had done to Jakob Erlandsen. What it did not do was create a right belonging to ordinary Danes, or establish anything resembling representative government. The men it protected were the men who imposed it.

The sharper difference is structural, and it is the one worth carrying forward. Magna Carta was extorted once, in arms, then annulled within months, reissued, and eventually absorbed into statute; no English king ever had to buy his throne again. The Danish charter founded a series. Because the monarchy was elective, the terms had to be agreed afresh at every accession — and each successive håndfæstning was longer than the last, until by the fifteenth century it was the constitution in all but name. The 1282 document is not Denmark's Magna Carta. It is Denmark's first instalment, and chapters 18 to 19 are largely a story about what kings had to sign.

It also gave the opposition a language. From 1282 onwards, resisting a Danish king can be framed as defending the law rather than as treason.

Erik Klipping spent the four years after 1282 trying to get out from under it. He did not manage it.

Meanwhile in Europe

A king signing terms in 1282 was not doing anything unusual. All over Europe in these decades rulers were being pinned down in writing by the people they needed money from. England had Magna Carta in 1215 and the Provisions of Oxford in 1258, and in 1265 Simon de Montfort summoned burgesses as well as barons; the Aragonese Corts and the Castilian Cortes were already meeting; the French Estates General was called for the first time in 1302. The mechanism is the same everywhere: war costs more than a ruler's own lands can produce, so he has to ask — and asking gives the people asked a lever.

Elsewhere the same year Jyske Lov was given, 1241, Mongol armies destroyed Polish and Hungarian forces at Legnica and Mohi and reached the Adriatic before turning back. In 1291 Acre fell and the crusader states in the Holy Land ended. And in 1309 the papacy moved to Avignon, where it stayed for sixty-eight years — which is worth remembering whenever a Danish bishop of the next century appeals to Rome.

06 / NARRATIVEFinderup, 22 November 1286

Danish terms in this section

marsk
Marshal — the realm's senior military officer.
fredløs
Outlawed: placed outside the law's protection, so that killing the outlaw is not a crime. The standard Danish penalty for the unreachable.
folkevise
Ballad. The medieval Danish ballads are the other source for this story, and they are not evidence.

On the night of 22 November 1286, riding back from a hunt, the king of Denmark took shelter in a barn at Finderup near Viborg. Men came in during the night and stabbed him. The wounds were counted afterwards and the number that has come down is fifty-six.

At the Danehof at Nyborg the following Whitsun, nine men were convicted in their absence and outlawed: among them Marsk Stig Andersen, the realm's marshal, and Count Jacob of Halland. They fled to Norway, were given a base, and spent the next decade raiding the Danish coast from the fortress they built on the island of Hjelm — where, incidentally, they minted their own coins, which survive.

This is the point to stop and say what the record for this century actually consists of, because it is why so much of this page is hedged. There is no Saxo after 1200 — nobody wrote a Danish national history again for three hundred years. What survives instead is a scatter of thin annals kept at monasteries, often a line or two a year and frequently written down long afterwards; charters and letters, which tell you what was transacted and never why; papal registers, excellent on church quarrels and silent on everything else; and the ballads, which are marvellous and are evidence about the opinions of the fifteenth century rather than the events of the thirteenth. There was no chronicler in the barn at Finderup and there is no record of the trial that followed.

So when a Danish historian says the killing of Erik Klipping is unresolved, they do not mean the evidence is finely balanced. They mean there is almost none.

Whether they did it is a different question from whether they were convicted of it. The trial was run by the dead king's son and by men with reasons of their own; the accused were not present; the evidence, as far as we can reconstruct it, was thin. Danish historians have argued about this for two centuries without resolving it, and the honest position is that we know a king was killed in a barn, we know who was blamed, and we do not know that those are the same thing.

What made the story immortal was not the trial but the ballads. In the folkeviser Marsk Stig becomes a wronged husband avenging his wife, whom the king had violated while the marshal was away on campaign — a motive that appears nowhere in the documents and everywhere in the songs. It is a superb story and there is no reason to believe it. It also tells you something real, which is that within a generation or two of 1286 there was an audience in Denmark that wanted the killing of that king to have been justified.

07 / NARRATIVEThe most expensive reign

Danish terms in this section

pantsætning
Pledging: handing over land, or a district, or its revenues, to a creditor until the debt is repaid. The creditor governs and collects in the meantime.
pantelen
A district held in pledge. The holder ran it, taxed it, and kept the proceeds.

Erik Menved, son of the murdered king, reigned from 1286 to 1319, and he is the reason the rest of this page happens.

He wanted what his great-grandfather Valdemar Sejr had briefly had: north Germany. He campaigned in Mecklenburg, in Pomerania, in Brandenburg, and against the outlaws on Hjelm; he intervened in Sweden; he held tournaments on a scale that impressed foreigners. He was, by the standards of his peers, a serious European prince.

All of it was borrowed. Denmark's own revenues could not fund campaigns of that size, so Erik Menved borrowed against the only asset the crown had, which was itself. A district would be pledged to a creditor — usually a north German count — who would then govern it and collect its income until the loan was repaid. It never was repaid, so the pledge was extended, or a new loan was raised on top of it, secured on somewhere else.

And the taxes rose. In 1313 the Jutland peasants rose against them, in a rebellion serious enough that it took a campaign to suppress; twenty-five leaders were named afterwards, and every one of them was a farmer, not a nobleman. It is worth setting that beside 1180 in chapter 13. Danish farmers in this period rebel, organise, choose leaders, and are sometimes crushed.

When Erik Menved died in 1319 he had outlived all his children, and the crown's lands were largely in other men's hands. His brother Christoffer 2. was elected in 1320 — and, for the first time, had to sign a charter as the condition of election rather than as a concession afterwards. The document has been called the harshest ever imposed on a Danish king. He signed it, broke it, and was driven out of the country in 1326.

What happened next is the strangest constitutional moment in Danish history. The Holstein count Gerhard 3. — Grev Gert — installed his young cousin, the duke of Schleswig, as King Valdemar 3., and took the duchy for himself as the price. And in 1326 a document was issued in the boy king's name declaring that the duchy of Schleswig should never be united with the kingdom of Denmark under one ruler. The Constitutio Valdemariana was produced to serve a specific piece of family business in 1326. It would be quoted against Danish kings for five hundred years, and it is one of the threads that leads directly to 1848.

08 / NARRATIVETowns, friars and herring

Danish terms in this section

købstad
Market town: a place with a royal charter giving it the exclusive right to trade and craft in its district, in return for taxes.
gråbrødre / sortebrødre
Grey friars and black friars — Franciscans and Dominicans. Named for the colour of the habit.
sild
Herring. For three centuries the single most valuable thing Denmark produced.
fed
The fenced plot at the Skåne market allotted to a particular foreign town's merchants. Lübeck had one, so did a dozen others.

All of the preceding sections are about men with swords, because those are the men the sources are about. Underneath them the thirteenth century was doing something more consequential, and it can be put in one sentence: Denmark acquired towns.

By 1300 there were somewhere in the order of eighty places in Denmark with the standing of a købstad. Most were tiny by any modern measure — a few hundred people, a church, a market square, a wooden quay. But the charter mattered: it gave the town a monopoly on trade and craft in its hinterland, its own court and its own law, and it gave the king a taxable thing that had not existed before. Chapter 9's Ribe and Hedeby had been royal enterprises, exceptional and few. This is a network.

Two arrivals tell you the towns had become real. In 1222 the Dominicans reached Lund, and in 1232 the Franciscans reached Ribe. Chapter 12 had the Cistercians deliberately settling on marginal ground away from people; the friars did the exact opposite. They planted themselves in towns, because preaching needs an audience and begging needs a crowd. They preached in Danish, heard confession — which the Fourth Lateran Council of 1215 had made an annual obligation for every Christian — and were, for most townspeople, the face of the church that they actually dealt with. Within a century almost every Danish town of any size had a friary.

And then there was the herring.

Every autumn, vast shoals came through the Sound, and on the sandy point at the south-western corner of Skåne — at Skanør and Falsterbo — a market assembled to catch, gut, salt and barrel them. It ran for a few weeks and it was one of the largest commercial gatherings in northern Europe. Merchants came from Lübeck, from the Wendish towns, from the Netherlands and England; each town's merchants had their own fenced plot, their own booths, and their own jurisdiction over their own people while the market lasted.

The demand behind it was religious. The medieval church prescribed fasting from meat on Fridays, through Lent, and on a long list of other days — something close to a third of the year. Salted herring was the protein that filled the gap, and it kept and travelled. A Catholic calendar in Germany and a shoal of fish in the Sound between them produced the most valuable Danish export of the Middle Ages.

Two consequences run straight into the following chapters. The first is that whoever controlled Skåne in the autumn controlled an enormous income from stall fees, customs and justice — which is part of why Skåne mattered so much, and why the pledge-holders of section 09 were prepared to pay for it.

The second is that the merchants who came were increasingly German, and increasingly organised. The privileges they extracted — to trade freely, to be judged by their own law, to be exempt from Danish tolls — were granted by Danish kings who needed the money now and did not much mind what they were signing away for later. By the time the towns of the Lübeck league had consolidated into what we call the Hanse, they held written rights inside Denmark that no Danish king could revoke without a war. Chapter 15 ends with one of those wars, and Denmark loses it.

09 / NARRATIVEThe country with no king

Christoffer 2. came back in 1329 on terms that left him almost nothing, and died at Nykøbing Falster in 1332. Nobody was elected to replace him. For the next eight years Denmark had no king at all.

It had owners instead. Gerhard 3. of Holstein-Rendsburg held Jutland and Funen. His kinsman Johann 3., called the Mild, held Zealand, Lolland and Falster. And in 1332 the men of Skåne, Halland and Blekinge — offered nothing better — accepted Magnus Eriksson of Sweden as their lord, and he paid off the German pledge-holder to make it stick. The eastern provinces that had been Danish since before chapter 10 were, for a generation, Swedish.

It is worth being careful about what this did and did not mean, because the phrase "Denmark was pawned" invites a picture that is too dramatic in one way and not dramatic enough in another.

Pledging land for money was ordinary. Every European ruler did it; it was the normal instrument of medieval public finance, and a pledge was in principle redeemable. A creditor holding a district had every reason to keep it productive, because he was living off its income. Danish life in the 1330s did not stop. Harvests were got in, cases were heard at the assemblies, churches were built, and the herring fleets still came to the great autumn market at Skanør, where the Hanseatic merchants had their booths and where a substantial part of northern Europe's protein was salted down each year.

What was genuinely extraordinary was the completeness. Not a province pledged but the whole kingdom, simultaneously, to a handful of men from the same family; and no king, for eight years, in a country that had had one continuously for four hundred. There was no institution to be loyal to. The Danehof stopped meeting. The pledge-holders taxed harder than the kings had, because they were recovering capital and had no reason to care what anyone thought of them in ten years' time.

And underneath, the countryside had stopped growing. Chapter 12 described a three-hundred-year expansion — forest cleared, -torp villages founded on marginal ground, the population perhaps doubling. By the early 1300s that was over. The good land was taken and the marginal land was marginal; there are signs of farms being abandoned in poorer districts well before 1349. When chapter 15 arrives with the Black Death, it arrives in a country that had already reached its limit, which is part of why the recovery took so long.

Meanwhile in Europe

Denmark's countryside was not running out of room on its own. Between 1315 and 1317 it rained across northern Europe for the better part of three growing seasons. Crops failed from Ireland to Poland; seed grain was eaten; the price of wheat multiplied several times over. The Great Famine killed perhaps a tenth of the population of northern Europe, and it hit a continent that had spent three centuries growing into every available acre and had nothing left in reserve.

Historians call the whole period the crisis of the fourteenth century, and the point of the phrase is that the Black Death of 1348–50 arrived into a Europe that was already in trouble. Denmark's abandoned farms, its pledged provinces and its missing king belong to that larger picture, not only to the incompetence of two kings. In 1337, meanwhile, the English and French crowns began a quarrel that would run for a hundred and sixteen years.

10 / NARRATIVERanders, 1 April 1340

Danish terms in this section

væbner
Squire: a man of the armed landholding class, below a knight.
Nørreris
Niels Ebbesen's estate, near Randers. He was a landowner of no great standing and no royal blood whatever.

In the spring of 1340 Gerhard 3. brought an army into Jutland to put down resistance and secure his hold. A chronicle puts it at eleven thousand men, which is the kind of number chronicles say — nothing in fourteenth-century Denmark could have fed a force that size — but it was large enough that nobody proposed to meet it in the field. He quartered himself in Randers.

Vignette · a squire makes a decision

Niels Ebbesen of Nørreris was a Jutland squire. He was not a great magnate, he held no office of state, and outside this one night nothing whatever is known about him that would have got him into a history book.

On the night of 1 April 1340 he and a small band — the ballad says forty-seven men, which is the kind of number ballads say — got into Randers, found the count's lodging, and killed him in his bed. Then they got out of the town, cut the bridge behind them, and rode.

The interesting question is not how he did it but why he thought it would work. Killing a creditor does not cancel a debt, and Gerhard's sons inherited both the claim and the army. What the killing actually did was break a spell: it demonstrated that the man who had made himself master of Jutland could be reached, in his own quarters, by a person of no importance. Within months the Jutland rising had spread far enough that the Holsteiners were negotiating.

Niels Ebbesen of Nørreris · Randers · the night of 1 April 1340

What followed was not liberation but a deal. Later in 1340 the Holsteiners agreed to accept Christoffer 2.'s youngest son, Valdemar, as king, marry him to the sister of the king of Sweden, and hand back one quarter of Jutland as a start. Everything else stayed pledged, and had to be bought or fought back over the following thirty-five years by a young man with almost no resources.

It is worth asking why there was anything left to hand back to. In 1332 Denmark had no king, no royal revenue, no army of its own and a foreign count in every province. It did not dissolve. Nobody in Skåne proposed becoming permanently Swedish; nobody in Jutland proposed becoming a German county. The assemblies went on meeting and hearing cases, the parishes went on baptising and burying, and Jyske Lov went on being the law of Jutland — because none of those things had ever needed a king in order to work. That is what chapters 12 and 13 built, and the 1330s are the decade that tested it.

Niels Ebbesen got no part of any of it. He was killed at Skanderborg in November 1340, seven months after Randers, fighting the dead count's sons. The pledges were still in place. Jutland was still occupied. The Holsteiners were still there, and would be for years. He had seven months, and the kingdom whose door he had opened was put back together by somebody else.

Myth-check

"'Med lov skal land bygges' is the founding principle of Danish democracy."
It is the first line of the preamble to a provincial law code for Jutland, and it introduces an argument about what makes a law usable — that it should be clear, tolerable and in accordance with the custom of the country. It is not a statement about popular sovereignty, and a close variant appears in the Norwegian Frostating law. It is a fine sentence doing a smaller job than it is usually asked to do.
"Jyske Lov was Denmark's law."
It was Jutland's. Skåne and Zealand had their own codes, and Denmark had no single law until 1683. Jyske Lov's national status is a later promotion, from the 1500s, when the high courts began treating it as a reference work.
"Marsk Stig murdered Erik Klipping to avenge his wife."
That motive comes entirely from the ballads. Nine men were outlawed for the killing at a trial they did not attend, run by the dead king's party, on evidence we cannot now assess. Whether they did it has been argued for two hundred years. The ballad is evidence about the fourteenth century's opinions, not about 1286.
"Denmark was sold to the Germans."
It was pledged as security for loans, which was the standard instrument of public finance everywhere in Europe and was in principle redeemable — as it duly was, over thirty-five years. What was extraordinary was not the mechanism but the scale: the entire kingdom at once, and no king for eight years.
"Niels Ebbesen drove the Germans out of Denmark."
He killed one man in one night and was dead himself seven months later. The pledges were redeemed by Valdemar Atterdag over decades of purchase, marriage and war. Niels Ebbesen's stature is largely a nineteenth-century construction, sharpened again between 1940 and 1945 for reasons that are not hard to see — which does not make him unimportant, but does mean the thing being celebrated is mostly the celebrating.
"Slavery in Denmark was abolished in the thirteenth century."
Nothing was abolished. Thralldom faded out because it stopped paying, and the people concerned became tenants who owed rent instead of persons who were owned. Better in law; not obviously better in life.

Summary

IF YOU REMEMBER FIVE THINGSThe page in five

  1. Jyske Lov, given at Vordingborg in 1241, is the only Danish provincial law issued by a king. Its preamble opens "with law shall a land be built" — an argument about what makes law usable, for Jutland, not a constitution for Denmark.

  2. Thralldom ended in Denmark during the thirteenth century with no decree of any kind. It stopped paying: the supply had dried up and letting land to tenants was cheaper than owning people. The freed became tenants owing annual rent.

  3. Abel had his brother Erik Plovpenning killed on the Slien in 1250 and swore himself innocent. From then on the royal house is two houses — kings, and the dukes of Schleswig — and the seam runs to 1864.

  4. The charter of 1282 gave Denmark an annual Danehof and the rule that no man be imprisoned without lawful conviction. Every king to 1660 signed something like it. In 1286 Erik Klipping was killed in a barn at Finderup; who did it is still argued.

  5. Erik Menved's borrowing pledged the kingdom piece by piece to Holstein counts. From 1332 to 1340 Denmark had no king at all, and Skåne was Swedish. Niels Ebbesen killed Gerhard III at Randers in 1340 and was dead by November.

Questions & discussion

FOUR KINDSWork the material

Recall

Did the facts land?

  • Where and when was Jyske Lov given, and which part of Denmark did it apply to?
  • What replaced thralldom, and what did the former thrall now owe?
  • How did Erik Plovpenning die, and how did his brother clear himself of it?
  • Name the two main promises of the 1282 charter.
  • Who held Jutland, Zealand and Skåne in 1332?
  • What did Niels Ebbesen do, and how long did he outlive it?

Causal

Why did that follow from this?

  • Why did a growing population make slavery unprofitable rather than more valuable?
  • Why did putting a fool's cap on Jakob Erlandsen strengthen his position rather than weaken it?
  • Why does giving the duchy of Schleswig to a royal brother in 1250 create a problem that lasts six hundred years?
  • Why did pledging districts to creditors raise the tax burden on Danish farmers rather than lower it?
  • Why did Denmark not simply dissolve during the eight years it had no king?

Counterfactual

Pull one thread and see what moves.

  • Suppose Abel had not killed his brother in 1250, and the duchy had stayed in the king's hands. Does the southern border become an ordinary frontier?
  • Suppose Erik Menved had stayed out of north Germany. Does Denmark avoid the pledge, and does it then meet the Black Death as a functioning kingdom rather than a recovering one?
  • Suppose no claimant had appeared in 1340. How long could a Denmark with no king have gone on before it stopped being a country — and what exactly would have had to fail first?

Contested

Where historians actually disagree.

  • Who killed Erik Klipping? The convictions of 1287 were obtained in absence by an interested court, and the alternatives proposed have included the Norwegian king, the duke of Schleswig, and a faction around the dowager queen. The argument has run since the nineteenth century and the evidence has not improved.
  • How far was Jyske Lov actually applied? A code can be promulgated and largely ignored, and the gap between what the law says and what happened at a herredsting is exactly the gap the sources cannot show us.
  • What did the 1282 charter really concede? Read one way it is a constitutional landmark; read another it is a bargain between a weak king and a magnate faction, with no implications for anybody outside that class.
  • Was the mortgaging of the realm a catastrophe or ordinary medieval finance carried out at unusual scale? The answer determines whether Valdemar Atterdag in chapter 15 is rescuing a wreck or completing a normal redemption.
  • How many thralls were there, and what did they do? Estimates run from a tenth of the population to a quarter, and readings of their economic role differ just as widely. On a question this basic, that spread is worth noticing.

Sources

LIGHT SOURCINGWhat this is built on

  • Jyske Lov, 1241 The oldest surviving manuscript is from the 1270s. The preamble is the most quoted piece of medieval Danish prose; the other 160-odd chapters are the actual evidence.
  • Skånske Lov & Codex Runicus Skåne's own code, written down c. 1202–16, and the manuscript of about 1300 that records it in runes.
  • Håndfæstningen af 1282 The first Danish charter of conditions, and the model for every one down to 1660.
  • The Danish ballads (folkeviser) The source for everything vivid about Marsk Stig and Niels Ebbesen, and evidence only for what later generations wanted to believe.
  • danmarkshistorien.dk Aarhus University. The provincial laws, Jakob Erlandsen, Finderup, the pledging of the realm, and the end of thralldom.
  • Annals and papal registers Thin, and mostly written elsewhere. This is the worst-documented Danish century since chapter 7, which is why so much of it is contested.

STILL THEREPlaces you can visit

  • Danmarks Borgcenter, Vordingborg Where Jyske Lov was given in 1241, on the castle site that staged the southern campaigns. The Goose Tower is the surviving fragment.
  • Domhuset, Nytorv, Copenhagen "Med lov skal land bygges" cut over the portico, 1815, on a courthouse — the sentence in the role it was later given.

  • Hjelm The small island off Djursland where the outlaws of 1287 built their fortress and struck their own coins. Landing is possible in summer.
  • Randers A statue of Niels Ebbesen on horseback outside the town hall, put up in 1882 and given a second life as a national symbol during the occupation.
  • Skanör & Falsterbo, Skåne The sandy point where the great autumn herring market was held. The castle mound and the churches remain; the herring, and the money, are long gone.
