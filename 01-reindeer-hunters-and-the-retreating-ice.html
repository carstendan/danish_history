Reindeer hunters and the retreating ice

c. 13,000 – 9,000 BCE · Late Palaeolithic · senpalæolitikum

Everything later in this series happens on a landscape built in these four thousand years — and on a soil boundary drawn by a wall of ice that no human being ever saw.

01 / NARRATIVEThe ice that made the country

Danish terms in this section

Weichsel-istiden
The Weichselian, the last glaciation, named after the Vistula.
hovedopholdslinjen
The main stationary line: where the glacier's advance and its melting cancelled out.
moræne
Till — an unsorted mash of clay, sand, gravel and boulders dropped by ice.
tunneldal
Tunnel valley, cut by meltwater under pressure beneath the glacier.
dødishul
Dead-ice hole: a hollow left where a buried block of stranded ice melted out.
hedeslette
Outwash plain, literally 'heath plain' — flat sand and gravel washed out from the ice front.
bakkeø
'Hill island' — a worn remnant of older moraine standing above the outwash plain.
Hærvejen
'The army road', the ancient overland route along the Jutland ridge.

Denmark has almost no bedrock at the surface. Chalk shows through at Stevns and Møn, granite on Bornholm, and that is close to the whole list. Everything else you can stand on — every hill, every field, every gravel pit — is debris dropped by ice or washed out from underneath it. The country is not carved; it is dumped.

The last glaciation, Weichsel-istiden, began around 115,000 years ago. It was not one continuous sheet but a series of advances and retreats from different directions, which is why Danish geologists argue about the details to this day. The decisive advance came from central Sweden around 22,000 years ago and stopped roughly down the middle of Jutland. That limit is hovedopholdslinjen. Everything north and east of it went under ice. Western Jutland did not — it stayed an ice-free, permafrosted tundra in front of the glacier, its surface still the worn-down moraine of the previous glaciation.

This is the most consequential line ever drawn across Danish soil, and nobody drew it. East of it the ice left moræne, shaped into rolling hills, gouged by deep tunneldale, and pitted with dødishuller. West of it, meltwater poured out from the glacier front carrying its load westwards and sorting it by weight as the current slowed: boulders near the ice, then gravel, then sand, then silt far out. The result is the hedesletter, vast flat aprons of sand, with bakkeøer standing above them like the islands they are named for.

Clay holds water and nutrients. Sand does not. So this line, drawn by a glacier twenty-two thousand years ago, decided in advance where Denmark's good farmland would lie and where the heath would be — the heath that had to be reclaimed with enormous state and private effort in the nineteenth century, and that made the farming economies of east and west Jutland behave differently right into living memory. It is also, near enough, the watershed: rivers east of it run east, rivers west of it run west. The Hærvejen follows the high dry ground along it, which is why the road that carried medieval cattle drives, pilgrim traffic and nineteenth-century armies is, in the end, a product of the ice.

Melting back from the line began around 20,000 years ago and went in stages, with readvances that left further ridges across the landscape. By roughly 13,000 BCE the ice front had pulled well north and east of present Denmark. What it uncovered was raw: gravel, braided meltwater channels, permafrost, and a thin tundra of lichen, moss, dwarf shrubs and grasses. There were no trees.

02 / NARRATIVEA country that was not islands

Danish terms in this section

Den Baltiske Issø
The Baltic Ice Lake — the freshwater lake that occupied the Baltic basin, dammed by the retreating ice margin.

With so much of the world's water locked up as ice, global sea level stood roughly 120 metres lower than today. The North Sea was not a sea. Between Britain and Jutland lay a wide, low, habitable plain that archaeologists now call Doggerland, with river systems, lakes, marshes and coastline of its own. It still announces itself occasionally: North Sea trawlers bring up bones of mammoth and aurochs, and in 1931 a vessel working the Leman and Ower banks dredged up a worked antler point — the Colinda harpoon — from a landscape that had been dry ground when it was made.

Britain was walkable from Denmark. To the east, the Baltic was not the Baltic either: it was Den Baltiske Issø, a vast body of fresh meltwater held in by the ice margin, its level controlled by whichever outlet the ice happened to be blocking. Bornholm was attached to land in the south.

There was, in other words, no Denmark. There was the northern edge of a single continental plain running from Britain to Poland — and the northern edge is exactly what it behaved like: seasonally productive, climatically unreliable, reachable only from the south, and a very long way from anywhere warm. That geography is worth holding on to, because it recurs. This page is the first instance of a pattern that runs through the whole series: what happens here is largely decided by what is happening further south.

03 / NARRATIVEThe first arrivals

Danish terms in this section

rensdyrjægere
Reindeer hunters — the standard Danish name for the people of this whole period.
boplads
Settlement or camp site; the basic unit of Danish prehistoric archaeology.
zinken
A curved, beaked flint borer; the type-fossil of the Hamburg culture.
Hamburgkulturen
The Hamburg culture, named after Meiendorf near Hamburg.

The schoolteacher

In 1968 a schoolteacher named Jørn Fynbo was walking along the southern shore of Jels Oversø in southern Jutland when he picked up an odd piece of flint. It had a curved, beaked point at one end, and it puzzled him enough that he went away and read about it.

What he had was a zinken — the tool that served these hunters as a kind of pocket-knife for working hide and antler, and the single most diagnostic artefact of the Hamburg culture. Danish archaeologists had been hoping for one for half a century, ever since Alfred Rust's excavations north of Hamburg in the 1930s had shown what a reindeer-hunter site looked like. Nobody had found the culture on Danish soil.

Fynbo took his flint to Haderslev Museum. Then nothing happened for thirteen years. When excavation finally began in 1981, the scatter he had walked over turned out to be Jels 1 — a camp of the first people known to have stood in Denmark.

Jørn Fynbo, amateur archaeologist · Jels Oversø, 1968

The Hamburg culture is named for the Ahrensburg tunnel valley just north of Hamburg, where Alfred Rust excavated Meiendorf and Stellmoor in the 1930s and found thousands of animal bones preserved in waterlogged deposits. Danish archaeologists had wanted their own Hamburg site for half a century. The Jels excavations, run by Jørgen Holm and Flemming Rieck between 1981 and 1984, finally delivered one: two small camps about thirty metres apart on flat sand terraces above a tunnel valley with steep sides. One looks like a base camp occupied for some time; the other like a few days.

Then, between 1989 and 2001, Holm excavated four more late-glacial sites a few kilometres away at Slotseng, near Vojens — two of the Hamburg culture and two of the slightly later Federmesser group. Together with finds at Sølbjerg on Lolland, these are the oldest secure traces of human beings anywhere in Denmark. Radiocarbon puts them at roughly 12,100 BCE, about 14,100 years ago.

What one autumn looked like

What makes Slotseng exceptional is a dødishul — one of those dead-ice hollows, here holding a small pond. Bone does not normally survive in Danish soil from this period, but in oxygen-poor mud five metres down it does. One autumn, hunters killed eleven adult reindeer at the water's edge, butchered them there, and threw the waste into the water. Among several hundred bones recovered was a reindeer vertebra with the broken tip of a flint point still embedded in it, and a shoulder blade with a snapped point lodged in the same way.

Twenty-one May 2001

Jørgen Holm has written his own account of the day. He was getting out of the car in a car park in Middelfart when the call came. At the Slotseng excavation, five metres down in an oxygen-poor, olive-green layer of clay gyttja, someone had lifted out a reindeer vertebra — one of several hundred bones from the deposit, and at first glance unremarkable. Then they saw what was in it: the splintered stump of a flint point, snapped off inside the animal on a hunt in the late ice age. Shouts went up from the bottom of the pit.

The other half of the story is slower and more characteristic of how archaeology actually works. Some years earlier a museum storeman had come to Holm holding a box of flint and asked whether it too belonged to the reindeer hunters. It did. Tracing the accession numbers and the parish records, they found it had been surface-collected in 1962 by a since-deceased museum employee, from a low hill between two villages a few kilometres from Jels — and then sat on a shelf, unrecognised, for thirty years. That hill was Slotseng.

Jørgen Holm, excavator of Jels and Slotseng, 1981–2001

Reindeer antler grows and is shed on a strict annual schedule that differs between males and females, so the antler fragments in the deposit date the kill not merely to a year but to a season: roughly mid-October to early December. That is a quietly important result. The long-standing assumption had been that these hunters came north only in summer and wintered somewhere warmer. Slotseng says they were here in late autumn, and makes year-round presence plausible.

The camps themselves are unimpressive to look at: thin scatters of struck flint on high ground, sometimes only a few square metres. But their placement is not random. They sit above narrow points in tunnel valleys, where migrating reindeer would be funnelled together and slowed, and they are set high with a wide view so that an approaching herd could be seen and prepared for. Experiments suggest a thrown spear needed the hunter within about fifteen metres to be effective, which tells you what kind of ambush this was. Over ninety per cent of the identifiable bone from these camps is reindeer.

Meanwhile in Europe

The Hamburg culture is a northern offshoot of the Magdalenian, the culture of the great painted caves. By the time anyone reached Jels, Lascaux was already about four thousand years old and Altamira older still. Southern Europe had a long-established artistic and symbolic tradition; the far north had a handful of hunting camps and some decorated antler.

Britain, connected by land, was being occupied and abandoned on its own rhythm. The Creswellian sites there are broadly contemporary, and Gough's Cave in Somerset has produced human remains of this general period — which Denmark, as we will see, conspicuously has not.

04 / NARRATIVEFlint: the only wealth this landscape had

Danish terms in this section

flint
Flint — nodules of silica formed in chalk; Denmark's one abundant high-quality raw material.
flække
Blade: a long, parallel-sided flake struck from a prepared core, the blank for most tools.
skraber
Scraper, for working hide.
stikkel
Burin: a chisel-edged tool for grooving antler and bone.
kærvspids
The Hamburg culture's projectile tip, with a notched, narrowed base for hafting.
rentak
Reindeer antler — the period's second great raw material.

Denmark is archaeologically rich for an unglamorous reason: it has excellent flint and a great deal of it. Flint forms as nodules in chalk, and Denmark's Cretaceous chalk is close to the surface in the east and was ground up and scattered as erratics across the moraine everywhere else. You can pick usable flint off a ploughed field. It is also nearly indestructible, which means that in a period where wood, hide, sinew, fur and gut have all rotted away, the flint survives in its thousands and is essentially the entire evidence base.

The technology is more sophisticated than the word 'stone age' suggests. A knapper prepared a core and struck from it a series of long, parallel-sided flækker — blades — which served as blanks. From the blanks came a small standard toolkit: the kærvspids to tip a spear, the zinken to bore and scrape, the skraber to work hides, the stikkel to cut grooves in antler so that splinters could be levered out and made into points and needles. At Trollesgave, the debris is so completely preserved that the excavators could map the concentration of flakes and identify the exact spot beside a large stone where one person sat and worked.

Almost everything else is missing. The flint tips were mounted on wooden shafts with resin and sinew; none of that survives. The tents were probably hide over a wooden frame; we have no certain example. What we do occasionally get is the second raw material, rentak. A heavy antler implement dredged from the Odense canal basin, roughly thirteen thousand years old, is one of the very few surviving objects of the Bromme culture that is not flint. The so-called Lyngby axe — a reindeer antler with the tines removed — is another, and archaeologists still disagree about whether it was a mattock, a club, or something else entirely.

Two things follow. First, when you read a confident account of how these people lived, remember it is reconstructed from the one material class that happens to survive. Second, flint is the beginning of a very long Danish story. This is a country with no metal ores, no building stone worth the name, and one superb export commodity lying in the ground. In four thousand years, people here will be mining flint industrially and trading it across northern Europe. When bronze arrives, the absence of everything else will define the country's foreign relations. The pattern starts on this page.

05 / NARRATIVEA climate that would not settle

Danish terms in this section

senglacial
Late glacial — the unstable centuries between the ice maximum and the present interglacial.
Bøllingtid
The Bølling interstadial, a warm phase named after Bølling Sø in Jutland.
Allerødtid
The Allerød interstadial, named after a clay pit in north Zealand.
Ældre / Yngre Dryas
Older and Younger Dryas: cold relapses, named for the arctic flower Dryas octopetala.

The single most important thing to understand about this period is that the ice age did not end once. It ended, came back, ended again, came back harder, and only then actually ended.

There is a pleasing reflexivity in how we name these swings. In June 1897 the geologist Vilhelm Milthers and the botanist Nikolaj Hartz examined a clay pit belonging to the brickworks at Allerød in north Zealand. Deep in the section, where the layer sequence was most complete, they found a thin brown band of organic mud — a few centimetres to a few decimetres thick — containing traces of elk, reindeer, pike and willow. Those species need warmth, and therefore an ice-free landscape. They had identified a warm interval inside the last glaciation. In 1942 Johannes Iversen described an earlier warm phase from Lake Bølling in Jutland, extending the scheme backwards.

Both names went global. Bølling and Allerød are now standard terms in the climate stratigraphy of the entire Northern Hemisphere, used every day by scientists who will never visit either place. Denmark's first contribution to world science was, fittingly, the chronology of its own thaw — and it will not be the last time a small country's local peculiarity turns into an international standard.

06 / NARRATIVEHow we know any of this

Danish terms in this section

kulstof-14-datering
Radiocarbon dating.
pollenanalyse
Pollen analysis — reconstructing past vegetation, and so past climate, from pollen grains in lake mud.
gytje
Gyttja: soft organic lake mud. Oxygen-poor, and therefore the reason anything organic survives at all.

It is worth pausing on method, because it applies to the whole of the next three chapters and because the confidence of a museum label rarely matches the confidence of the underlying evidence.

Radiocarbon is the backbone. Living things take up carbon-14; when they die the clock starts. But the amount of carbon-14 in the atmosphere has varied over time, so a raw measurement has to be calibrated against independently dated material — tree rings, corals, annually layered lake sediments. This is why you see two kinds of number in the literature, and why they differ by thousands of years: the Trollesgave settlement dates to about 10,826 radiocarbon years before present, which calibrates to roughly 12,871–12,590 calendar years before present. Worse, the calibration curve for exactly this period contains plateaus, stretches where several real calendar dates produce the same radiocarbon result. Some of the vagueness in this page is not carelessness; it is the instrument.

Pollen analysis supplies the environment. Pollen grains are near-indestructible and species-specific, and they rain steadily into lakes and bogs where they are buried in gytje in orderly layers. Count them through a core and you have a running record of what was growing, and by inference how cold it was. The technique was largely developed in Scandinavia, and Iversen's work at Bølling is a founding example of it. It is how Allerød was identified before radiocarbon existed.

Volcanic ash gives an instant. The Laacher See eruption spread a chemically identifiable ash layer across much of Europe. Wherever it appears in a sequence, it marks the same moment, which makes it a superb correlation tool between sites hundreds of kilometres apart. A 2021 study radiocarbon-dated trees killed by the eruption's own pyroclastic flows and placed it at 13,006 ± 9 years before present — more than a century earlier than the previously accepted figure. A 2025 study then found the eruption's sulphur signature in a German cave formation and used it to lock the European and Greenland records together, confirming 13,008 ± 8. That is a startling level of precision for something fifteen thousand years old.

And it is disputed. In 2023 another team argued in Nature that the tree-ring date is about 130 years too old, because volcanoes outgas radiocarbon-free carbon dioxide which the surrounding vegetation absorbs — so trees growing on the flanks of a volcano can look older than they are. If that objection holds, the older chronology returns and so does the possibility of a causal link between the eruption and the cold snap. If it does not, the two events are separated by something like a century and a half.

Which is a good place to notice what this section is actually about. The precision of "13,008 ± 8" is real: it is what the method delivers. Whether the method has a systematic bias is a different question, and it is open. A number with an error bar of eight years can still be wrong by a hundred and thirty.

Greenland ice cores supply the climate curve itself, at annual resolution, from oxygen isotope ratios in the ice. This is another field with deep Danish roots: Willi Dansgaard at the Niels Bohr Institute pioneered the isotope method, and Danish-led drilling projects produced the reference records still in use. So Denmark named the warm phases and built the instrument that measures them.

Which brings a necessary complication. The ice-core climate zones and the pollen-based biozones do not line up exactly. The Trollesgave study demonstrated precisely this: on the pollen evidence the settlement belongs to the end of the Allerød, while the calibrated radiocarbon dates place it inside the cold ice-core interval that supposedly corresponds to the Younger Dryas. Greenland got cold before Zealand did. When you see a date in this period, it matters which clock it was read from.

07 / NARRATIVEFedermesser, Bromme, and an argument still running

Danish terms in this section

skafttungespids
Tanged point: a flint point with a symmetrical tang for hafting to a shaft.
Lyngbyspids
The heaviest tanged point type, up to fourteen centimetres.
irsk kæmpehjort
The giant deer, Megaloceros — 'Irish elk' in English, though neither Irish nor an elk.
Brommeproblemet
'The Bromme problem': the live dispute over whether the Bromme culture is a real unit.

After the Hamburg culture came the Federmesser group — German for 'quill knife', after the small curved backed blades that characterise it. These hunters used the bow and arrow. They were in Denmark through the cold snap of the Older Dryas and into the Allerød warming from around 11,800 BCE, when summer temperatures climbed to perhaps 13–15°C and open birch woodland spread across the tundra: birch above all, with rowan, aspen, willow and patches of pine, and an open floor of herbs and grasses. Elk moved in and stayed for the next several thousand years. So did the irsk kæmpehjort, for one last flourish before extinction. Beaver and roe deer appear. The reindeer were still there, but they were no longer the only thing on the menu.

Then, in the later part of the Allerød, something changes. In 1944 Erik Westerby found a settlement at Bromme, north-west of Sorø on Zealand; Therkel Mathiassen published it. The Bromme culture takes its name from that site. Its signature is the skafttungespids, in several sizes, the largest being the Lyngbyspids at up to fourteen centimetres. Even the shortest Bromme points are large — over five centimetres — which distinguishes them sharply from what comes after.

Here is the puzzle. The Bromme toolkit is simpler than what came before. Fewer tool types, less refined blade production, a much tighter geographic range. And the small backed blades disappear: the large tanged points look like tips for spearthrower-propelled darts, not arrows. On the face of it, the bow and arrow was lost.

Technologies do not usually get lost. So why here?

The most striking answer belongs to the Aarhus archaeologist Felix Riede. Around 11,050 BCE — late in the Allerød, and on the current chronology roughly a century and a half before the cold returned — the Laacher See volcano erupted in what is now western Germany, near Koblenz. It was among the largest eruptions in late Pleistocene Europe: an eruption column reaching perhaps 40 km, more than 1,300 square kilometres buried under ash and pumice up to ten metres deep, the Rhine dammed into a lake that then burst catastrophically downstream. Ash fell across much of the continent. Riede argues that the eruption emptied the worst-affected regions — the Thuringian Basin does appear to depopulate — scrambled the exchange and contact networks that Federmesser groups depended on, and left communities on the northern periphery, southern Scandinavia among them, isolated. Small isolated populations lose complex skills, a point anthropologists have made since the 1910s. On this reading, the Bromme culture is what cultural impoverishment looks like in the archaeological record.

The chronology matters here, and it has recently improved in Riede's favour. On the old dating the eruption and the onset of the Younger Dryas were effectively simultaneous, which made the two impossible to disentangle: any change in the archaeological record could be attributed to the cold rather than the volcano. The 2021 redating pulls them apart and leaves a clean window of roughly a century and a half in which social disruption could work before the climate collapsed. Riede's mechanism was never climatic — he is arguing about broken exchange networks and isolated communities, not about ash falling on Jutland — and separating the two events is what allows that argument to be tested at all.

It is an elegant argument and it is not settled. Others have argued that Bromme emerged under unusually favourable conditions rather than adverse ones, and that the simplification is functional — a toolkit adapted to different game in different country — rather than a loss of knowledge. Note also that Denmark lies far outside the ash fallout: there is essentially no Laacher See tephra in Danish sections apart from possible traces on Bornholm, so the mechanism has to be social and demographic rather than direct. Riede himself has since pushed in another direction entirely, questioning whether 'Bromme' is a legitimate taxonomic unit at all rather than a regional facies of Federmesser — the Brommeproblemet. Danish specialists have argued back in print. This is a live disagreement about a real thing, and it is one of the better windows in Danish prehistory onto how archaeology actually reasons.

Meanwhile in the world

While a few hundred people followed reindeer across the Jutland tundra, the Natufians of the Levant were living in the first villages with permanent stone-built structures, burying their dead in them, and harvesting wild cereals with sickles. By the end of this page's span, around 9,500 BCE, the monumental enclosures at Göbekli Tepe in southern Turkey were being erected, and Jericho was being founded.

Across the Atlantic, the same centuries saw the first securely attested peopling of the Americas south of the ice sheets. And in North America, Eurasia and Australia alike, the Pleistocene megafauna collapsed. Mammoth, woolly rhinoceros and giant deer disappear in a window that overlaps almost exactly with this chapter, in a climate-versus-hunting argument that is still unresolved.

The gap is worth sitting with. Denmark's story starts late and starts poor. At the moment the first permanent architecture was going up in the Near East, this was a hunting ground on the edge of an ice sheet with perhaps a few hundred people on it.

08 / NARRATIVEThe cold comes back

Around 10,900 BCE the climate collapsed. The Younger Dryas — Yngre Dryas — dropped summer temperatures by something like 5–8°C in a matter of decades and held them down for well over a millennium. The young birch woodland was killed off. Tundra returned, and with it a more sparse, pioneering vegetation.

The people who lived through this are called the Ahrensburg culture, after Rust's excavations at Stellmoor. Their tanged points are small and light, and the bow and arrow is back — which, if the Bromme argument above is right, means the technology was reintroduced from outside rather than remembered. In Denmark the culture was known for decades only from stray arrowheads at places like Bonderup and Eskebjerg on Zealand and Hjarup Mose in southern Jutland, until excavations at Sølbjerg on western Lolland — a twelve-metre sandbank flagged by an amateur archaeologist in 1989 — produced the first proper Ahrensburg settlement in southern Scandinavia. In 2020 the first traces of the period were finally identified on Bornholm, near the mouth of the Læså.

Reindeer kept moving through, but the herds were now doing something different: the evidence from Sølbjerg and elsewhere suggests animals that summered on the north-west European plain south-west of the Elbe and wintered in Schleswig-Holstein, Denmark and southern Sweden. The hunters were positioned on that circuit.

Then, around 9,700 BCE, the Younger Dryas ended as abruptly as it had begun. Within something like a human lifetime the climate flipped into the warm interglacial we are still in. The reindeer moved north and did not come back — they did not die out, they simply left. Forest came in behind them, first birch and pine, later hazel. The people who stayed had to become something else entirely, and that is where the hunter Stone Age (2) begins.

09 / NARRATIVEWhat we do not have

One fact should govern how much weight you put on everything above: there is not a single human bone from this entire four-thousand-year period in Denmark. Not one. The oldest known burial in the country is a cremation of a young woman at Hammelev in southern Jutland from about 8250 BCE — a thousand years after this page ends, and a different world.

Everything here is inferred from flint, animal bone, pollen and sediment. We have their rubbish and their kill sites and we do not have them. There is no Danish ancient DNA from this period, because there is nothing to extract it from. There are no graves, so no grave goods, so almost nothing about belief, status, kinship or social organisation. No art from Danish soil is securely dated to this period, though an amber elk's head sometimes claimed as the oldest sculpture in Scandinavia may belong to the Allerød — the dating is disputed. When you read that these were 'small mobile bands', that is a reasonable inference from camp size and toolkit, not an observation.

So who were they?

What we know comes from elsewhere in Europe, and it is startling. Ancient genomes now show that late-glacial Europe was not a story of continuity at all. After the glacial maximum, western and central Europe was occupied by people carrying an ancestry cluster geneticists label GoyetQ2, associated with the Magdalenian. Then, around 14,000 years ago — precisely the Bølling warming, precisely the moment the first hunters walk into Jutland — a genetic profile spreading from the south and south-east largely replaced them across western and central Europe. The resulting population, named after the Oberkassel find in Germany, carries roughly three parts of the incoming ancestry to one part of the older, and it is this group that occupies most of Europe for the next six thousand years.

So the people at Jels and Slotseng arrived in the middle of a continent-scale population turnover. Whoever they were, they were not simply the ancestors of anyone: the late-glacial record is full of populations that expanded, contracted, and were replaced. Whether Denmark's specific first arrivals left any descendants in later Danish populations is not something the Danish evidence can currently answer at all — and, as later chapters will show, this will not be the last population turnover on this soil.

10 / NARRATIVENot settlement. Visits.

It is tempting to call this period 'the settlement of Denmark'. It was not. The population at any one moment was probably in the dozens to low hundreds across the whole area, living in bands of perhaps a few families. There are no permanent structures. The camps were occupied for weeks, in some cases days. The country was entered, used, abandoned when the cold returned, and entered again later by people who may have had no connection at all to those before them.

What the period did establish is three things that outlast it, and they are the reason this chapter exists at all rather than being a paragraph of scene-setting.

It built the physical landscape and its soils, including a division of Jutland that still shows up in soil maps, road networks and farm sizes. It fixed Denmark's position as the far northern end of a continental system — reachable from the south, dependent on what happens there, and among the first places to be abandoned when conditions turn. And it established flint as the one thing this place had in quantity, which is the opening move in a very long story about a country with no metals, no good stone, and a permanent need to trade for what it cannot dig up.

It is worth trying to hold the timescale, because it defeats intuition. Four thousand years separate the hunters at Slotseng from the last Ahrensburg camps on Lolland — the same distance that separates us from the building of Stonehenge. Across that span perhaps a hundred and sixty generations were born, hunted, gave birth and died in this landscape. The entire surviving record of all of them would fit into a few museum drawers: some tens of thousands of struck flints, a few hundred animal bones, one flint tip embedded in a reindeer's shoulder blade.

Set against that, the confident sentences in the sections above ought to feel slightly alarming. We do not know what these people believed, what language they spoke, how they were related to each other, what they called this country or whether they thought of it as a country at all. We know where they sat to knap flint, because the chips are still there.

What we can say is what the ambush at Slotseng required. Somebody knew the herd would come down that valley in the second half of October. Somebody knew where the animals would slow. Somebody had carried the flint from a source that could be relied on, hafted it to a shaft with resin and sinew prepared in advance, and waited within fifteen metres of an animal capable of killing them. That is not survival at the edge of the possible. It is a working body of knowledge about a particular landscape, held in people's heads and passed on, and it went out of the world when the last of them stopped coming.

The people are gone and unreachable. The landscape they walked across is still, in its bones, the one out of the window.

Myth-check

"The ice age ended, and then people arrived."
The ice age ended at least twice, with a millennium-long relapse in between. People were here during cold phases, left, and came back. There were at least four distinct pulses of occupation and no particular reason to assume continuity between them.
"The whole of Denmark lay under the ice."
Western Jutland never did. The ice stopped along the main stationary line and stayed there, which is why the two halves of the peninsula have completely different soils — and why almost everything else on this page happens where it happens.
"These were the first Danes."
They were the first people in this place. Whether they were ancestral to anyone here later is unknown, and on the European evidence for repeated population turnover, not to be assumed. The phrase imports about thirteen thousand years of nationhood that had not been invented yet.
"The Laacher See eruption caused the Younger Dryas."
The two were long thought simultaneous. On the current dating the eruption comes roughly a century and a half earlier, and the study that established it explicitly rules out a direct causal link — though that redating is itself contested. Riede's argument about the Bromme culture never depended on the eruption causing the cold; it depends on it breaking social networks.
"Stone Age people lived in caves."
Denmark has essentially no caves. These people lived in the open, almost certainly under hide tents, and moved. The cave-dweller image is imported from French and Spanish prehistory and has never had anything to do with this country.

Summary

IF YOU REMEMBER FIVE THINGSThe page in five

  1. Denmark is glacial debris. The hovedopholdslinje through Jutland marks where the ice stopped, and separates rich clay moraine in the east from poor outwash sand in the west — a division that still shapes Danish farming, settlement and roads.

  2. There was no Denmark. Sea level was 120 m lower, Doggerland joined Jutland to Britain, and the Baltic was a freshwater ice-dammed lake. This was the northern edge of a continental plain.

  3. The first secure human traces are Hamburg-culture reindeer hunters at Jels and Slotseng in southern Jutland, around 12,100 BCE — ambush camps above tunnel valleys, with over 90% of their bone waste from reindeer.

  4. The climate swung violently: Bølling, Older Dryas, Allerød, Younger Dryas, then an abrupt end around 9,700 BCE. Four archaeological cultures — Hamburg, Federmesser, Bromme, Ahrensburg — map onto that curve, and the transitions between them are argued about.

  5. Not a single human bone survives from these four thousand years in Denmark. Everything we say about these people is inferred from flint, animal bone and pollen, and should be held loosely.

Questions & discussion

FOUR KINDSWork the material

Recall

Did the facts land?

  • What is the hovedopholdslinje, and what does the landscape look like on each side of it?
  • Name the four late-glacial cultures in order, and the climate phase each belongs to.
  • What made Slotseng able to tell us the season of a hunt?
  • Roughly how much lower was sea level, and what did that connect Denmark to?
  • Why do Bølling and Allerød appear in scientific papers about Greenland and Patagonia?

Causal

Why did that follow from this?

  • Why does a landscape boundary from 22,000 years ago still predict Danish agricultural geography in 1900?
  • Why are the earliest Danish settlements concentrated in southern Jutland and on Lolland rather than, say, northern Jutland?
  • Why should a small, isolated population be expected to lose complex technologies? What does that imply about how technical knowledge is stored in a society without writing?
  • Why is flint so central to what we can say about this period — and what does that distort?

Counterfactual

Pull one thread and see what moves.

  • Suppose the ice had stopped 100 km further west, leaving all of Jutland under moraine. What changes about Danish history — and how far forward does the change reach?
  • Suppose the Younger Dryas had lasted another two thousand years. Does Denmark get farming later, or does it arrive from a different direction entirely?
  • Suppose one Slotseng-style waterlogged site had preserved a burial. How much of this page would be written differently?

Contested

Where archaeologists actually disagree.

  • Is the Laacher See hypothesis a compelling explanation for the Bromme culture, or a case of reaching for a dramatic single cause? What evidence would settle it?
  • The Brommeproblemet: if 'Bromme' may not be a real cultural unit, what does that say about the other named cultures here — or about archaeological cultures as a concept?
  • Given that we have no human remains at all, how confident should a museum label be allowed to be about these people? Where is the line between reasonable inference and storytelling?
  • If the ice-core clock and the pollen clock disagree about when the Younger Dryas began, what does 'the Younger Dryas' actually name — a climate event, or a local ecological one?
