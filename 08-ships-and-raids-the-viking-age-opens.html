Ships and raids: the Viking Age opens

c. 750 – 900 · vikingetiden begins

For three centuries, people from this small country did more damage and covered more distance than anyone would have predicted from the size of the place. This chapter is about why it started, what it actually was, and who was writing it down.

Introduction

  2. What does the word viking actually mean, and who counts as one?

01 / NARRATIVEThe customs officer at Portland

Danish terms in this section

vikingetiden
The Viking Age; in Denmark conventionally c. 800–1050, though the raids begin earlier.
at fare i viking
'To go raiding' — the phrase is a verb before it is ever a noun for a people.
hird
A lord's household troop: sworn men, fed and paid, and the basic unit of Viking-age power.

A misunderstanding at Portland, c. 789

Three ships put in on the Dorset coast. The king's reeve at Dorchester, a man named Beaduheard, was told about it and rode down with a small party to deal with them in the ordinary way — foreign merchants had to be registered and taken to the king's estate so that the correct dues could be collected.

He spoke to them somewhat sharply, according to the chronicler, and told them to come along. They killed him and everyone with him.

It is the first recorded contact between Scandinavians and the English, and what makes it worth telling is how banal it is. Nobody expected an invasion. A local official went down to the beach to do the paperwork on some visiting traders, and misjudged who he was talking to. The Viking Age begins as an administrative error.

Beaduheard, reeve of Dorchester · Portland, c. 789

Four years later something happened that nobody could mistake for a customs dispute. On 8 June 793, raiders landed at the island monastery of Lindisfarne off the Northumbrian coast — the house of St Cuthbert, one of the holiest sites in the British Isles. They plundered the church, killed some of the monks, drove others into the sea, and took others away as slaves.

The reaction across Christian Europe was shock of a specific kind. Alcuin of York, the leading scholar at Charlemagne's court and himself a Northumbrian, wrote a series of appalled letters home: never before had such a thing been seen in Britain, and it had happened at the very place where the Christian faith in that kingdom had begun. He was in no doubt that God was punishing the English for their sins, which was the standard explanation and, in its way, more comforting than the alternative.

What made Lindisfarne so shocking was not the violence, which was ordinary enough by the standards of early medieval warfare. It was the target. Monasteries were sanctuaries. They were also undefended, coastal, and full of portable precious metal — which is to say that from a purely practical standpoint they were the best-stocked and worst-protected buildings in Europe. Anyone willing to disregard the sanctity was looking at an extraordinary rate of return.

02 / NARRATIVEWhy then?

The question of why the raids began around 790 rather than at any other point has generated a century of argument. Several answers are usually offered, and the honest position is that they are cumulative rather than competing.

The sail. Chapter 7 ended with the arrival of the sail in Scandinavia, sometime around 700. A clinker-built hull with a mast can cross open sea, carry thirty or more men with their gear, land on an open beach without a harbour, and go home again. Nothing else in this list matters without it.

Wealth worth taking. Western Europe in the eighth century was richer and more monetised than it had been for centuries, and much of that wealth sat in coastal monasteries and trading places. Meanwhile silver was flowing into the Baltic from the Islamic world, so northerners knew what silver was for.

Politics at home. A society consolidating into fewer, larger kingdoms produces losers — displaced chieftains, younger sons, men with a following and no land. Raiding abroad is a way of paying a retinue you cannot otherwise afford, and of becoming somebody. It has also been argued that raiding was a route to marriage: a young man with silver could offer a bride-price he could not otherwise raise.

The Franks. This is the most direct cause and the one usually underplayed. Between 772 and 804 Charlemagne conquered Saxony, in a campaign of brutal length involving forced conversion, mass deportation and, at Verden in 782, the reported execution of thousands of prisoners. When it was finished, the Christian empire stood on the Danish border for the first time in history.

A Danish king in 800 could see exactly what had just happened to the neighbours, and exactly what the Frankish empire did to pagan peoples on its frontier. Raiding Frankish and English coasts was not only profitable; it was a way of hitting an enemy who had made his intentions unmistakable.

03 / NARRATIVEWhat "viking" means

It is not an ethnic label. In Old Norse, a víkingr is a person on a raiding voyage, and víking is the voyage itself: you go in viking the way you might go fishing. It is something you do, seasonally, and then you come home and it stops being what you are.

So a Norwegian farmer might spend one summer in ten as a viking; and a Frisian or a Slav might do the same thing without being Scandinavian at all. The vast majority of people in ninth-century Denmark never went on a raid, and lived by farming, exactly as their grandparents had.

English sources of the period usually say Dene — Danes — for Scandinavians in general, including Norwegians. Frankish sources say Nordmanni, northmen. Irish sources distinguish "dark foreigners" and "fair foreigners", which historians have argued about for two hundred years. None of these words map cleanly onto modern nationalities, and it is worth resisting the urge to make them.

Broadly, though, the directions do sort out. Fleets from Norway went north and west — Scotland, the Isles, Ireland, and eventually Iceland and Greenland. Fleets from Sweden went east, up the Russian rivers towards Byzantium and Baghdad, and that is the trade world's story (9). Fleets from Denmark went south-west: Frisia, England, and the rivers of Francia. That is this chapter.

04 / NARRATIVEWhat they believed

Danish terms in this section

blót
Sacrifice — and the feast that followed it. The central religious act, and a meal.
gode
Old Norse goði: a chieftain who also led the cult. The two offices were not separable.
sejd
Seiðr: a form of magic worked mainly by women, and regarded as shameful in men.
vi / lund
A sacred place; a grove. Both survive in Danish place names.
Æser og vaner
The two families of gods — Odin's and Freyr's — reconciled after a war in the myths.

Jelling (10) is about a king abolishing this. It is worth knowing what he abolished, because the series has not said, and because the shape of it explains why he could.

Begin with what was absent. There was no church, no scripture, no creed, no clergy as a separate profession, and no word in Old Norse that means "religion". There was siðr — custom, the way things are done — and the relevant opposite of it was not unbelief but a different custom. That is why the Christian sources keep having to explain that these people would not stop: nobody was being asked to abandon anything, only to add.

The central act was the blót. Animals were killed, the blood was sprinkled on the participants and the walls, and then everybody ate the meat and drank the ale. It happened at the hall of whoever mattered locally, and it was led by him. This is the fact that matters most: religious authority and political authority were the same authority, held by the same man, in the same building. A chieftain who fed his district and sacrificed for its harvest was not a secular lord who also went to church. There was no church for him to go to.

Cult happened in halls, and in groves, springs, bogs and islands — the wet places chapters 3 to 07 watched receiving offerings for four thousand years. Adam of Bremen describes a great temple at Uppsala with human sacrifices hanging in a grove, but he wrote a century later, had never been there, and was making a point; treat it as the medieval equivalent of a hostile documentary.

The gods, and who had which

Odin — Óðinn — is the god of poetry, of frenzy, of the hanged and of kingship. He is also treacherous, and gives victory and then withdraws it. Chapter 7 found a Danish ruler described on gold as Odin's man around the year 400, which is the earliest naming of him anywhere. He was a god for people with power.

Thor was for everyone else. Thunder, storms, protection; the one who kills the things that threaten the enclosure. Hammer amulets are by a wide margin the commonest religious object recovered from Viking-age Denmark, and Thor's name is all over the map — Torslunde, Torsager, Thorsted. If you want to know what ordinary Danes actually did, count the amulets rather than reading the poems.

Freyr and Freyja govern fertility, prosperity, sex and the harvest, and were probably the gods most often addressed about the things that decided whether a household survived the winter.

The place names are the best Danish evidence and they are still in the phone book. Odense is Odin's vi, his sacred place. Viborg is the holy place on the hill. Gudme, in chapter 07, is the gods' home. These are not survivals from an exotic past; they are addresses.

Death, and the source problem

There was no single afterlife and no orthodoxy about it. Some of the dead were burnt and some buried; some went into boats, some under mounds, some into flat graves with a knife and a brooch. Valhalla — a hall where the battle-slain fight all day and feast all night — belongs to poetry about warriors, and would have meant little to most people.

And here is the difficulty that governs everything above. Almost every written account of Norse religion was set down in Iceland in the thirteenth century, two to three hundred years after conversion, by Christians — Snorri Sturluson above all — who were preserving the mythology as literature and as a key to poetry, not as a faith they held. We are reading a religion through the notes of people who had stopped practising it and were interested in the stories.

What the archaeology supplies instead is amulets, deposits, burials and place names: what people did, in aggregate, without commentary. The two bodies of evidence answer different questions and should not be blended, which is a rule this series has needed since chapter 1.

One consequence is worth carrying to Jelling (10). A religion with no centre, no doctrine and no exclusivity cannot be argued with, and cannot be reformed — but it also cannot resist. There is nobody to excommunicate a king who changes his mind, and no institution to defend. Christianity is hierarchical by construction and arrives with an organisation attached. When Harald Bluetooth converts, every local chieftain's inherited claim on the sacred is abolished at a stroke, and the only man left with a line to God is the king.

05 / NARRATIVEGodfred, the first Danish king we can see

Danish terms in this section

Frankiske Rigsannaler
The Royal Frankish Annals — the court chronicle that supplies almost everything we know here.
Ejderen
The river Eider, the border agreed in 811 and the southern limit of the kingdom for a thousand years.
hirdmand
A sworn member of a king's household troop — and, in 810, his killer.

Chapter 7 ended with somebody able to order a canal cut and a rampart built, whose name we do not know. In 804 we get a name.

In that year, according to the Royal Frankish Annals, Godfred, king of the Danes, brought his fleet and the whole cavalry of his kingdom to a place called Sliesthorp on the border between his realm and Saxony. He had promised to come and negotiate with the emperor in person. On his men's advice, he thought better of it and sent envoys instead.

That single sentence contains a great deal. There is a king of the Danes. He has a fleet and a mounted force he can summon. He has a border, and it is understood as a border. He has advisers whose counsel he takes. And he is cautious enough not to put himself in Charlemagne's hands.

808: moving a town

Four years later Godfred attacked the Obodrites, a Slavic people on the Baltic coast who were Frankish allies and had previously paid tribute to the Danes. He beat them, hanged one of their leaders and drove out another, and made two-thirds of them tributary.

Then he did something more interesting than winning a battle. There was a trading place on that coast called Reric, near modern Wismar, which had been paying him well. He destroyed it — and put its merchants on his ships.

He sailed home with them and set them down at Sliesthorp, on the Schlei inlet at the base of the Jutland peninsula. That settlement is Hedeby, and it becomes the largest town in Scandinavia. Godfred did not found a town so much as confiscate one.

And in the same season, the annals say, he ordered his kingdom's frontier towards Saxony fortified with a rampart running from the Baltic along the north bank of the Eider to the western ocean, with a single gate through which wagons and riders could pass. That is the Danevirke — already standing since chapter 7, and now rebuilt and manned.

The Frankish chronicler, who did not like him, called Godfred the mad king.

Godfred, king of the Danes · 808

In 810 Godfred sent a fleet — the annals say two hundred ships — against Frisia, defeated the inhabitants in three engagements and made them pay him a hundred pounds of silver. He was reportedly threatening to come to Aachen and see the emperor there.

Then, in the same year, one of his own household men killed him. The circumstances are murky and the Franks may well have arranged it. His nephew Hemming succeeded, made peace immediately, and in 811 twelve envoys from each side confirmed a treaty fixing the border at the Eider.

Godfred matters out of proportion to the six years he is visible. He is the first ruler of this country about whom we have substantial contemporary evidence, and everything about him — the fleet, the cavalry, the tribute, the forced relocation of an entire merchant community, the frontier work — describes a state with real coercive capacity. It also, awkwardly, all comes from people who considered him an enemy.

06 / NARRATIVEThe ships

Danish terms in this section

langskib
Longship: the warship — long, narrow, shallow, fast.
knarr
The cargo ship: shorter, broader, deeper, relying on sail rather than oars.
klink
Clinker construction — overlapping planks riveted together, flexing with the sea.
kølsvin
The keelson, the timber that takes the mast's thrust down into the keel.

The ship is the technology that makes the period, and it is worth being precise about why.

A Viking-age longship is clinker-built: overlapping planks, riveted, fastened to a light framework inserted afterwards. The result is a hull that flexes visibly in a seaway instead of resisting it, and which is astonishingly light for its length. Shallow draught means it can be run onto a beach or taken far up a river; low freeboard and a symmetrical hull mean it can be reversed without turning round.

Combine that with a single square sail on a mast that can be lowered, and oars for when the wind fails or the water narrows, and you have a vessel that can cross the North Sea, sail up the Seine to Paris, and be dragged overland between rivers if necessary.

Nothing else in Europe could do all of those things at once. A Frankish or English defender who fortified a coast found the ships going up a river; one who blocked a river found them portaging around. The strategic problem the Vikings posed was not that they were unusually ferocious. It was that they could not be caught.

The Danish evidence for these ships is mostly later than this chapter — the Ladby ship burial from around 925, the Skuldelev ships scuttled in Roskilde Fjord in the eleventh century — but the Norwegian Oseberg ship of 834 and the Gokstad ship of around 890 fall squarely inside it, and Gokstad in particular has been copied and sailed across the Atlantic.

07 / NARRATIVEHow a raid actually worked

Danish terms in this section

leding
The naval levy: the obligation on districts to supply ships and men. Formalised later, but its roots are here.
hærtogt
A military expedition, as opposed to a trading voyage — though the same men made both.
vinterlejr
Winter camp: the fortified base that turned a seasonal raid into an occupation.

Strip away the drama and a raid is a logistical exercise with a fairly tight set of constraints.

Numbers. A longship carries perhaps thirty to sixty men with their gear, food and water. So a fleet of ten ships is three to six hundred people. When a chronicler writes of two hundred ships, he may be reporting a genuinely enormous force or simply saying "more than I can count" — and the scholarly consensus has moved steadily downwards over the last half-century. The Great Heathen Army was probably in the low thousands, not the tens of thousands that Victorian historians assumed.

Season. Sail after the spring sowing; be home, or safely camped, before the autumn gales. That rhythm is why early raiding does not interfere with farming: the men who went were away during the slack months. The moment fleets start wintering abroad, that stops being true, and raiding becomes a full-time occupation for a professional class.

Intelligence. The most underrated fact about Viking raiding is that the raiders had usually been there before as customers. Traders and raiders were the same people — that is the whole point of the Portland story, where the reeve took them for merchants because that is exactly what they might have been the previous summer. You knew which monastery was rich, which river was navigable, and where the local lord's army was, because you had done business there.

What was taken. Silver and gold, obviously, and much of it in the form of church plate that was immediately cut up — hacksilver, valued by weight. But the highest-value outcome was usually people: a wealthy captive could be ransomed for far more than the contents of an altar, and an ordinary one could be sold. Booty was divided by share, with the leaders taking more, which is why a successful captain could pay a retinue and an unsuccessful one lost his.

Where they stayed. From the 840s the fortified winter camp appears — a defensible river bend or island, ditched and banked. At Repton in Derbyshire, where the Great Army wintered in 873–874, excavation found the army's camp built against the church.

Two hundred and fifty people in a Mercian mausoleum

Beside the camp at Repton stood a small stone building — a burial vault of the Mercian royal house, already a century or two old. Somebody cleared it out, and filled it.

Inside were the disarticulated bones of around two hundred and fifty people, stacked around the walls, with a single man laid out at the centre. About four-fifths were male, and most were between eighteen and forty-five. Isotope work indicates that many had not grown up in England. Some of the bones show weapon injuries; most do not, which points to disease and hunger doing what a Mercian army could not.

These are the rank and file of the Great Heathen Army: the men chapter 9 will find rowing cargo, the men who had left a farm somewhere in Denmark or Norway for a season and were still away three winters later. Not one of them has a name.

The mound over the vault was finished with a kerb and, apparently, some ceremony. Whoever the man at the centre was, the army that buried him had lost a quarter of itself doing whatever it had spent that winter doing, and stayed to bury the dead properly before moving on in the spring.

The Repton charnel deposit · Derbyshire · winter 873–874

Whatever else the great army was, it was an army — and it was mortal at a rate that no saga mentions.

08 / NARRATIVEFrom raiding to conquest

For the first half-century the pattern is seasonal: come in summer, take what is portable, go home. Then it changes, in three stages.

Wintering. From the 840s, fleets stop going home. They seize an island or a defensible river bend, build a camp, and stay — which converts a raid into an occupation and lets them start the next season already in position.

Tribute. Rather than fight, rulers begin to pay. The Frankish kings did it repeatedly; the English did it on a scale that eventually produced a national tax. Paying a fleet to go away is rational in the short term and catastrophic in the medium term, because it advertises that Frankish and English silver is available to anyone with ships.

Settlement. And then the armies stop leaving at all.

The Great Heathen Army

In 865 a force the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle calls the micel hæþen here — the great heathen army — landed in East Anglia. It was not a raiding party but a coalition of war-bands under several leaders, later tradition naming them as sons of the legendary Ragnar Lothbrok, and it had come to stay.

It took York in 866. It killed King Edmund of East Anglia in 869. It broke Northumbria and Mercia. By the mid-870s parts of the army were dividing land and settling on it, and only Wessex was left.

In January 878 a surprise midwinter attack drove Alfred of Wessex into the marshes at Athelney with a handful of men. That spring he came back, raised the levies of three shires, and beat the Danish leader Guthrum at Edington. The peace that followed required Guthrum to be baptised with Alfred as his sponsor, and divided England: Wessex and English Mercia under Alfred, and the north and east under Danish law.

That territory is the Danelaw. Its effects on England are permanent and mostly invisible to people who live there — hundreds of place names ending in -by and -thorpe, the division of Yorkshire into ridings, and a substantial layer of everyday English vocabulary that came in from Old Norse. The words they, egg, knife, sky, window, law and husband are all in the language because of this.

Meanwhile in Europe

Charlemagne was crowned emperor in Rome on Christmas Day 800, reviving an imperial title in the west after three centuries. His empire did not survive him by much: it was divided among his grandsons at Verdun in 843, and the resulting quarrels between weak successors are a large part of why Frankish coasts and rivers were so poorly defended for the rest of the century.

This is the century in which the western empire Charlemagne assembled stopped being able to defend itself, and in which local lordship began to replace royal administration across most of what becomes France.

09 / NARRATIVEFrancia: up the rivers

England gets the attention in English-language history, but for Danish fleets the richer and more frequently visited target was the Frankish empire, and the instrument was the river.

Charlemagne died in 814. His empire was divided among his grandsons at Verdun in 843, and the resulting three kingdoms spent the rest of the century fighting each other, buying each other's allies and failing to co-ordinate anything. A coastline defended by rival kings who would rather see their brother raided than help him is not defended at all.

The great Frisian emporium of Dorestad, on a branch of the Rhine, was attacked repeatedly from 834 and never really recovered. From 843 fleets were on the Loire, taking Nantes. In 845 a Danish fleet came up the Seine and reached Paris; Charles the Bald bought them off with seven thousand pounds of silver, which was an enormous sum and an even more expensive precedent.

Over the next forty years the pattern repeated across every navigable river in western Francia: the Seine, the Loire, the Garonne, the Somme. Monasteries moved their relics inland. Bridges were built specifically to block rivers. Local counts who could actually organise a defence gained authority at the expense of kings who could not — one of the mechanisms by which Carolingian central power dissolved into the patchwork of territorial lordships that becomes medieval France.

In 885 a very large fleet besieged Paris itself. The city held, under Count Odo, through a siege lasting into 886 — and then the emperor Charles the Fat arrived, declined to fight, paid the besiegers, and gave them leave to go and plunder Burgundy instead, which was in revolt. The Parisians who had just spent a winter on the walls took the point. Charles was deposed within two years, and Odo, who had actually defended the city, was elected king.

By 911 the West Frankish king Charles the Simple had drawn the obvious conclusion: if you cannot keep them out, hire them. He granted the Viking leader Rollo the land around the lower Seine on condition that he accept baptism and defend the coast against other Northmen. It worked completely. The territory became Normandy — the land of the northmen — and within three or four generations its rulers spoke French, endowed monasteries, and had become the most formidable military aristocracy in Europe.

In 1066 Rollo's descendant crossed the Channel and took England, six days after another Scandinavian claimant had been defeated in Yorkshire. The North Sea Empire (11) ends there.

10 / NARRATIVEThe part that is hardest to look at

The Viking economy ran on slaves.

This is not a marginal observation. Raiding produced two kinds of return: portable valuables, and people. Monasteries were attractive because they held both. The English and Irish and Frankish sources record captives being carried off from the very first raids, and the Irish annals in particular describe it relentlessly.

Where did they go? Some were ransomed, which was often the most profitable outcome. Some were kept — Scandinavian society ran on unfree labour, and a træl had no legal standing. And a great many were sold onward, into a market that stretched from Dublin through Hedeby to the Volga and ultimately to the Islamic world, which had silver and wanted people.

That is the mechanism connecting the two halves of the Viking Age. The silver that arrives in Danish towns in chapter 9 — Arabic dirhams by the tonne — is, to a substantial degree, payment. This is the same trade Denmark was supplying to Rome in chapter 6, running now in a different direction and at a much larger scale.

Danish popular history has not always been comfortable with this, and museum presentations tended for a long time to emphasise the craftsmanship, the seamanship and the trade. That has changed considerably in the last twenty years, and it should have.

11 / NARRATIVEWritten by the victims

Now the caveat that ought to sit under everything above.

Every written source for this chapter was produced by people the Vikings attacked, or by their co-religionists. The Anglo-Saxon Chronicle, the Royal Frankish Annals, the Annals of St Bertin, the Irish annals, Alcuin's letters — all are Christian, mostly monastic, and describe pagans who burned churches and killed clergy. Not one word survives written by a Dane about any of this. The runic inscriptions of the period are short and say nothing about policy.

Several distortions follow. Monastic raids are over-reported, because monasteries kept the records and had reason to describe their own losses. Numbers are unreliable and usually inflated; "two hundred ships" is a chronicler's way of saying a great many. Motives are supplied by writers who assumed the raiders were an instrument of divine punishment, which is not an explanation that helps.

The corrective from the other direction has sometimes gone too far. There was a fashion, roughly from the 1960s to the 1990s, for a rehabilitated Viking — trader, craftsman, poet, democrat, a bit misunderstood. That reading takes the archaeology seriously, which is right, and the written sources insufficiently seriously, which is not. Both things are true at once: these were skilled shipwrights and long-distance merchants with an assembly-based legal culture, and they were slavers who sacked churches.

The methodological point generalises well beyond the Vikings, and this series will meet it repeatedly. When all your evidence comes from one side, the right response is neither to believe it nor to invert it.

12 / NARRATIVEMeanwhile, at home

It is easy to lose sight of the fact that Denmark itself was doing something during all this besides launching ships.

Godfred's sons ruled, quarrelled and shared power for four decades. In 826 a claimant named Harald Klak, needing Frankish backing, was baptised at Ingelheim with the emperor Louis the Pious as his sponsor, and returned home accompanied by a monk named Ansgar. Harald lost his throne almost immediately, but Ansgar stayed in the north on and off for decades and eventually obtained permission to build a church at Hedeby around 850 and another at Ribe. He converted almost nobody of consequence. The point, for now, is that Christianity is on the ground in Danish towns from the mid-ninth century, and the kings are already aware that baptism is a diplomatic instrument. Harald Bluetooth's conversion (10) is where that becomes decisive.

Meanwhile the towns grow. Ribe, laid out around 704–710, is a going concern; Hedeby, restocked by Godfred in 808, becomes the largest town in Scandinavia and starts striking coins on a Frankish model within a generation. Both belong properly to the trading towns (9).

And the kingdom holds together, more or less, which is not obvious. A country whose ambitious men are away for the summer with armed followings is a country with a permanent succession problem. Danish royal history in the ninth century is a long sequence of co-kings, exiles, and men killed by their own retinues. What is remarkable is not the instability but that the thing being fought over persisted.

Meanwhile in the world

The Abbasid caliphate ruled from Baghdad, then the largest city on earth and the centre of an economy whose silver reached the Baltic. In China the Tang dynasty was entering its long decline. The Maya cities of the southern lowlands were being abandoned.

And in the North Atlantic, Norwegians settled the Faroes and then Iceland from the 870s — the first permanent human occupation of either. Both will end up under the Danish crown, and both are threads this series follows to Iceland's Act of Union (36) and the realm of 1953 (42).

Myth-check

"Viking was a people."
It was an activity. Víkingr means someone on a raiding voyage; you went in viking and then came home and farmed. Most Danes in this period never went on one.
"The Vikings appeared out of nowhere in 793."
Portland came first, around 789, and the technology, the trade contacts and the political pressure had been assembling for a century. What was new at Lindisfarne was the willingness to attack a monastery — and the fact that a monastery was where the records were kept.
"They came for plunder and glory."
They came for silver, land and slaves, in roughly that order, and the slave trade was central rather than incidental. Danish museums presented a sanitised version of this for a long time.
"The Vikings were savages" / "the Vikings were misunderstood traders."
Both readings are available because the evidence is split: hostile written sources on one side, archaeology of towns and workshops on the other. The sources are biased and the archaeology is silent about violence. Holding both is uncomfortable and correct.
"Alfred defeated the Vikings."
He saved Wessex, which was a considerable achievement in 878. The result was a negotiated partition that left the north and east of England under Danish law for the better part of a century — and a Danish king ruling all of England within a hundred and forty years. That is the North Sea Empire (11).

Summary

IF YOU REMEMBER FIVE THINGSThe page in five

  1. The raids begin with a killed customs official at Portland around 789 and become unmistakable at Lindisfarne in 793. The causes are cumulative: the sail, available wealth, political pressure at home — and Charlemagne's conquest of Saxony bringing a hostile Christian empire to the Danish border.

  2. Viking is an activity, not a people. Danish fleets went south-west to Frisia, England and the Frankish rivers; Norwegians north-west, Swedes east.

  3. Godfred, king 804–810, is the first Danish ruler we can see clearly: a fleet, a cavalry force, a frontier he rebuilt, and a whole merchant town he moved by force to found Hedeby. He was murdered by one of his own men.

  4. Seasonal raiding became wintering, then tribute, then conquest. The Great Heathen Army of 865 broke three English kingdoms; Alfred saved the fourth in 878, and the partition created the Danelaw.

  5. Every written source was produced by the victims. The archaeology and the chronicles pull in opposite directions, and the honest reading holds both: skilled seafarers and long-distance traders who dealt extensively in slaves.

Questions & discussion

FOUR KINDSWork the material

Recall

Did the facts land?

  • What happened at Portland, and why is it a strange way for an era to begin?
  • What did Godfred do in 808, and what three things does it tell us about his power?
  • What features of a longship made it so hard to defend against?
  • What was the Danelaw, and what did it leave behind in English?
  • Where was the border fixed in 811, and how long did it last?

Causal

Why did that follow from this?

  • Why did Charlemagne's conquest of Saxony make Viking raids more likely rather than less?
  • Why does paying tribute to a raiding fleet make the problem worse?
  • Why would a king destroy a profitable trading place and move its merchants rather than simply taxing it where it stood?
  • Why does a society that sends its ambitious men abroad every summer have a chronic succession problem?

Counterfactual

Pull one thread and see what moves.

  • Suppose Alfred had lost at Edington. Does England become a Scandinavian kingdom permanently, and what happens to the English language?
  • Suppose the sail had reached Scandinavia in 400 rather than 700. Does the Viking Age happen three centuries early, or does the absence of rich monasteries mean it does not happen at all?

Contested

Where historians actually disagree.

  • All the writing is by the victims and all the archaeology is silent about violence. Set out how you would weigh the two — and what the "rehabilitated Viking" of late twentieth-century scholarship got right and wrong.
  • How central was the slave trade? Consider why it was underplayed in Danish public history for so long, and what changes when it is put at the centre.
  • Godfred is known only through the writing of his enemies, who called him mad. What can legitimately be inferred about a ruler from hostile sources, and where should inference stop?
  • Denmark celebrates the Viking Age as national heritage in a way it does not celebrate, say, the colonial period of chapter 27. Is that distinction defensible, and on what grounds?
