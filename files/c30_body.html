The Danish Atlantic

1620 – 1803 · Christian Runge · Breffu · Philip Gardelin · Heinrich Carl Schimmelmann · Ernst Schimmelmann

Between 1658 and 1803 Denmark came to run forts on the Gold Coast, islands in the Caribbean and the ships between them. About a hundred and eleven thousand people were carried in Danish bottoms. In 1792 Denmark became the first slave-trading nation to decide to end its trade — with a ten-year delay written into the ordinance, during which Danish ships fetched more people from Africa than ever before.

Introduction

WHAT THIS PAGE ANSWERSFive questions

  1. Danish ships ran the triangular trade from the 1660s to 1803. What were its three legs, and what went on each?
  2. The rising on St Jan in 1733 and the rising on Bornholm in 1658 are told very differently in Danish history. Why?
  3. What did the ordinance of 16 March 1792 actually order, and what happened in the ten years that followed?
  4. Why is the company's grid on St Croix so seldom set beside the Danish land surveys of 1662 and 1681, and what does the comparison show?
  5. Of the slave ships found as wrecks, the Fredensborg is the best documented. What do its papers record, and what do they not?

01 / NARRATIVEBefore the Atlantic — Trankebar 1620, the Gold Coast 1658–61

Danish terms in sections 01–02

oktroj
a royal charter granting a company a monopoly on a trade, with the powers of government that went with it.
Trankebar
the Danish post on the Coromandel coast from 1620 to 1845. Tharangambadi, the place of the singing waves.
Guldkysten
the Gold Coast, in what is now Ghana. Denmark held forts on a narrow coastal strip, never a territory like the islands.

A word before the story, because this chapter does not follow the one before it in time. It runs from 1620 to 1803: chapter 29 stopped at 1788 and chapter 31 will open in 1784. The Danish Atlantic trade was one institution working continuously from the 1650s to 1803, and cutting it into chronological slices would break the thing it has to show. So the clock goes back here, once, and then forward again.

Denmark went east before it went west, and the eastern venture is the one that matters least to this story.

In 1620 Ove Gjedde negotiated a treaty with the ruler of Tanjore for a strip of the Coromandel coast, and the Danish East India Company built a fort at Tranquebar — Trankebar in Danish, Tharangambadi in Tamil, the place of the singing waves. It was Danish from 1620 to 1845, with two spells of British occupation, in 1801–02 and 1808–15, and in 1845 it was sold to Britain. What came home from it was pepper, saltpetre and above all cotton textiles, and it is worth registering that last one now, because the coloured cottons of India turn out to be the thing Danish ships would later carry to Africa to exchange for people.

The move to Africa began as a gold trade, and it began three times over. In January 1658, as part of the war with Sweden, the Danes took the Swedish fort of Carolusborg on the Gold Coast. Late in 1659 they agreed with the king of Fetu to build a fort of their own, Frederiksborg, which had to be given up to the English in 1685. And in 1661 they bought from the Ga ruler of Accra the site at Osu on which they built Christiansborg, which stands in what is now Accra. Around it in time came Fort Fredensborg and the smaller lodges of Augustaborg, Kongensten and Prinsensten.

What Denmark held there was never a territory in the way the islands would be. It was a narrow strip of shoreline with forts on it, and it sat there — as the Dutch and the English, and for a time the Swedes, sat along the same coast — waiting for African merchants to arrive from inland with people to sell.

The Danish presence on the Gold Coast falls into three periods, and the division tells the story without commentary. Until the end of the seventeenth century the principal business was gold and ivory. From about 1700 until the trade ban took effect in 1803 the principal business was people. After that it was chiefly an attempt at plantations, which failed. The forts were sold to Britain in 1850.

02 / NARRATIVESt Thomas, 1672

The Danish West India Company was chartered in 1671. In 1672 it sent a small expedition to the Caribbean, which landed on the largely uninhabited island of St Thomas, put up a flag, and declared it Danish.

St Jan was added in 1718. That is a small sentence covering a real dispute — the British on Tortola considered the island theirs — and the Danish position rested on getting there and staying.

What the islands were for was sugar, and sugar in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries was among the most labour-hungry commodities in the world. Cane has to be cut by hand at the moment it ripens, carried at once to the mill, crushed within a day, and boiled immediately, or the sugar in it is lost. The work is unremitting, dangerous around the rollers and the boiling house, and concentrated into a harvest that cannot wait. A plantation economy of that kind cannot be run with a labour force that can leave.

So from the beginning the colony imported people from Africa. That sentence is the hinge of the whole chapter, and it should be read slowly: the islands were not a settlement that later acquired slavery. They were founded as a system that required it, by men who knew exactly what they were founding.

03 / NARRATIVEThe triangle, in tons and in people

Danish terms in sections 03–04

trekantshandel
the triangular trade: goods to Africa, people to the West Indies, sugar home.
mellempassagen
the middle passage: the second leg, from Africa to the West Indies, with people as the cargo.
kasematter
the casemates: the cells beneath Fort Christiansborg where people were held with the rest of the trade goods until a ship came.

The route is called the triangular trade, and most Danish slave voyages followed it, out from Denmark and home again.

The first leg carried manufactured goods to the Gold Coast. What sold there is listed in the company records with the flatness of any commercial inventory: coloured East Indian cotton textiles above all, then other textiles, firearms, gunpowder and brandy, and small goods — mirrors, coral, hats, tobacco pipes. A captain would often fit out his own cabin as a showroom, so that African merchants could come aboard and inspect samples.

The second leg was the middle passage, west across the Atlantic with a cargo of people.

The third leg came home to Copenhagen with raw sugar.

The scale is documented. Danish ships carried about a hundred and eleven thousand Africans across the Atlantic, on about four hundred and thirty voyages, between the 1660s and the ban of 1803. Europeans as a whole carried about twelve and a half million between 1501 and 1866. The Danish share of the traffic to the West Indies in the Danish period was about 2.3 per cent; of the whole Atlantic trade it was under one per cent. By the number of people carried, Denmark was the smallest of the seven national carriers the SlaveVoyages database distinguishes (it files the Danish figure as "Denmark/Baltic").

A small share, then; but a carrier nation in its own right, with its own forts, its own companies and its own flag in the trade. That is the thing to hold, because the Danish national memory has generally preferred the percentage.

A series of chartered companies ran it — the West India and Guinea Company from 1674, when it took over the Guinea trade, until it was dissolved in 1754; the Guinea Company in the 1760s and 1770s; the Baltic and Guinea Company in the 1780s — with private traders running a shuttle between Africa and the islands alongside them.

The ships carried surgeons, and the effort to keep the captives alive had a plain commercial reason: a person who died at sea could not be sold in the West Indies.

Meanwhile · 1730s — the Atlantic at scale.

While Denmark was buying St Croix and dividing it into lots, Britain and France were running slave economies an order of magnitude larger. Saint-Domingue alone would become the most profitable colony in the world, and by the 1780s it was importing tens of thousands of people a year. Against that scale the Danish share looks small, which is why it is so often produced in Denmark as a defence. §03 gives the share, and the fact that Denmark carried under its own flag; the second is the fairer of the two.

04 / NARRATIVEThe crossing

The middle passage took two to three months on average.

Between one in six and one in five of the people put aboard died on it.

Those two sentences are the whole of what most Danish readers have been told, and they are both true, and they conceal the thing that matters — which is that the mortality was not an accident of the weather or a failure of navigation. It was a known and priced feature of the business. A merchant fitting out a voyage calculated for it. The surgeon's work was a calculation about the same figure from the other end: every percentage point of mortality was a percentage point of the return.

They were held before embarkation in the casemates below Christiansborg — dark cells under the rooms where the governor lived and worked, alongside the rest of the trade goods, because that is what the building was for. Many had already died on the caravan routes from the interior before they ever saw the sea.

On arrival in the West Indies they were sold at auction, to owners on the plantations and in the towns.

Vignette · Christian Runge, aboard the Fredensborg, April to July 1768

He is a Norwegian sailor from Arendal, taken on there when the ship called on her way south, in place of a man who had fallen ill. His name is in the papers because everybody's name is in the papers: the forty men who sailed from Copenhagen; the carpenters, nearly all of whom died on the Gold Coast; the captain, Espen Kønig, who died at Christiansborg at half past eleven on the night of 15 December 1767 and never saw the crossing; the first mate, Johan Frantzen Ferentz, who took the ship over. That December Runge himself was made third mate and steward, which put the ship's food and drink in his accounts. We know what they ate and when the wind changed.

Of all the slave ships found as wrecks, this is the best documented. The Fredensborg left Copenhagen late in June 1767, reached the Gold Coast on 1 October, sailed from there on 22 or 23 April 1768 — the accounts differ by a night — and came into St Croix on 9 July, eleven weeks across. When she was wrecked off Tromøya near Arendal that December, the ship's journal and the assistant's protocol were carried ashore, and they survive. The wreck itself was found by divers in 1974. Between the two, the last voyage can be followed day by day and in places hour by hour.

The people in the hold for those eleven weeks are in the same documents. They are counted. They are entered as numbers, as cargo, and when they die they are entered as losses.

Christian Runge is in this chapter because the archive that keeps him is the archive that does not keep them, and it is the same archive, written by the same hand, on the same page.

The mortality of the enslaved population in the Danish islands was always higher than its birth rate, throughout the whole period. That single fact does more work than any description: the colony could not sustain its own population and never came close to doing so, which is why the ships had to keep sailing, and which is also — as the end of this chapter shows — the argument that finally moved a Danish finance minister.

05 / NARRATIVEThe Akwamu rising on St Jan, 1733–34

Danish terms in sections 05–07

Akwamu
the West African state whose collapse put many of its people, including some of its elite, onto Danish ships.
maron
a person who had escaped and lived beyond the plantations. Hunted by the colony's militia.
Gardelins reglement
the slave code of 5 September 1733, in nineteen paragraphs, issued by the governor Philip Gardelin.

At dawn on 23 November 1733, a party of enslaved men walked up to the little star-shaped redoubt at Fortsberg, above the entrance to Coral Bay on St Jan, carrying bundles of firewood they were required to deliver.

There were cane knives in the bundles. They got through the gate, killed the small garrison, and fired the signal gun.

What followed was one of the longest slave rebellions in the history of the Caribbean, and it is not the story it is usually made into.

The leaders were Akwamu, from the region of present-day Ghana. A number of them had been people of consequence at home — high-ranking members of their society, wealthy merchants, men with authority — before the collapse of the Akwamu kingdom put them on Danish ships. They were not a mass of the anonymous oppressed. They were a displaced elite, and they organised accordingly.

Their plan was not flight. They did not intend to escape into the hills or take boats to Puerto Rico. They intended to capture the island's productive apparatus and keep it running. Europeans were to be held as hostages and traded for freedom and land. Sugar and rum would continue to be made, under Akwamu control, and exchanged for weapons and salt from St Thomas. A free black polity on St Jan could become the model for risings elsewhere in the Danish islands.

And they intended to work the plantations with Africans of other ethnic groups, held as slaves.

That last sentence has to stay in. The rebellion was a bid for freedom by the people who made it, and it was not a bid for the abolition of slavery, and a history that leaves the second part out in order to make the first part cleaner is not doing the dead any favours.

They held most of the island for six months. What broke them was not the colony: it was about two hundred better-armed French troops sent from Martinique in April 1734. The planters had regained control by the end of May. By then many of the rebels, cornered, had killed themselves rather than be taken — eleven at Ram Head in early May, more on the north side of the island later that month — and those deaths are still commemorated on the islands. The militia went on hunting maroons through the summer, and the rising was declared over at the end of August 1734.

Vignette · Breffu, St Jan, November 1733

She is enslaved on a plantation on St Jan, she is Akwamu, and when the historians list the leaders of the rebellion her name is among them: June, Kanta, Bolombo, Aquashie, Breffu.

Nearly everything else about her has to be assembled from other people's documents — from court records and estate lists written by the men she was fighting, in a colony that recorded her as property. An archaeologist who has worked on the landscape of the rising called her a slave, a rebel, a fighter, and a woman almost invisible to history, which is a fair description of the evidence and an unfair description of her.

What can be said is that she was not a follower. She is named among the leaders, in a record kept by men who had no reason to credit an enslaved woman with anything.

06 / NARRATIVESt Croix bought, 1733

In June 1733, before the governor's slave code of September and the rising of November, the company had bought an island.

St Croix lay forty miles south of St Thomas, it was larger and far more fertile than either of the existing possessions, and the French crown was willing to sell. It was also almost uncultivated, which from the company's point of view was the attraction: there was nothing on it to work around.

So it was surveyed, and the survey is the thing to look at. The island was measured and divided into uniform rectangular plantation lots, laid out on a grid, and the lots were distributed to the company's shareholders, who either worked them or sold them on. The printed map of 1754 — respectfully dedicated to the company's president — shows the whole island as a chequerboard, each lot with its windmill or horse mill marked, and the regular street plans of Christiansted and Frederiksted set into the coast.

It is worth putting that map beside two other acts this part has already described. In 1662 the Danish state reduced every farm in Denmark to a figure in barrels of hartkorn. In 1681 it went out and measured every field in the kingdom. From 1734 a chartered company ruled a grid over a Caribbean island and handed out the lots. The same habit of mind in three places between 1662 and 1734, the third in a company's hands (§08 says why that matters), and the third is the only one that has never been celebrated as an achievement of the absolutist state.

07 / NARRATIVEThe law of the plantation

Two months before the rising, on 5 September 1733, the governor Philip Gardelin issued a code of nineteen paragraphs governing the enslaved.

The order matters. The code was not a response to the rebellion. It came first.

Its preamble states that "our Negroes" — the period's term — had been made slaves by God himself, and that they were to be regarded as their owners' money. Both halves of that sentence are doing work: the first makes the arrangement theological, so that no further justification is required, and the second makes it accounting.

The paragraphs set out penalties for running away, for theft, and for planning or joining a conspiracy against the free white population of the colony. At the top of the scale: execution; the cutting off of a leg or an ear; pinching with red-hot tongs. Below that, treated as the milder options: branding on the forehead, and between fifty and a hundred and fifty lashes.

The enslaved were about ninety per cent of the population of the islands. The code is what a tenth of a population wrote down in order to hold the other nine tenths, and it should be read as a document of fear as much as of cruelty. Its procedural provisions say the same thing more quietly: the testimony of a person of European origin outweighed the testimony of an Afro-Caribbean.

There is an epilogue that is worth having. Gardelin left the governorship in February 1736 and sailed home. Later that same year he submitted to the company a proposal for a new and substantially milder code. He now thought it would serve better to give up the most draconian punishments and to treat the enslaved considerably better in general: better clothing and food, a small plot of ground on which to grow vegetables, a duty on owners to teach them and to missionise among them, and provision for the old and those unable to work.

The company shelved it without discussion. Gardelin, who had been a brewer on Christianshavn before he was ever a governor, died in Copenhagen in 1740.

The man who wrote the code changed his mind, put it in writing, and was ignored — by a commercial company answerable to its shareholders.

Meanwhile · 1791 — Saint-Domingue.

In August 1791 the enslaved population of the French colony rose, and the revolution that followed produced the republic of Haiti in 1804. It is the essential context for the Danish ordinance of March 1792, and not because anyone in Copenhagen was frightened into virtue. It is context because it shows what the alternative to reform looked like from a planter's chair, and because the Danish commission was working through the winter of 1791 in a world where the largest sugar colony in the Caribbean was on fire.

08 / NARRATIVEThe Crown takes the islands, 1754–55

Danish terms in sections 08–10

Vestindisk-guineisk Kompagni
the chartered company, founded in 1671, that held the islands until the state bought it out in 1754; the Crown took over the administration in 1755.
fideikommis
an entailed fortune: the capital tied up, the income assigned to a family's maintenance. The Schimmelmann holdings were held this way.
Forordning om Negerhandelen

From 1672 until 1755 the Danish West Indies were not governed by Denmark. They were governed by a chartered company, and the distinction is not a technicality.

The West India and Guinea Company held the islands from 1672, occupied St Jan in 1718 and bought St Croix in 1733. Its supreme authority in the colony was a governor it appointed. Its obligations ran to its shareholders. When its own former governor proposed treating the enslaved better, it filed the proposal.

In 1754 the state bought out the company's shareholders, on terms very favourable to them, and the company was dissolved. From 1755 the islands were administered by the Crown.

The change is easy to underrate and it is the hinge of the second half of this chapter. As long as the islands belonged to a company, what happened on them was commercial conduct, and the responsibility ran to a board. From 1755 the sugar islands were the direct possession of the Danish state, the enslaved were held under the authority of the Danish king, and the code that governed them was enforced in his name.

Everything after this point — the boom that follows, the fortunes made in Copenhagen, and the ordinance of 1792 — happens inside that arrangement. When Denmark eventually acted, it acted as an owner.

09 / NARRATIVEWhat the sugar built in Copenhagen

The charter of February 1734 gave the company a monopoly on importing and refining raw sugar in Denmark and Norway. That is where the money became Danish.

The islands grew the cane and the enslaved cut it. The raw sugar came home in the third leg of the triangle, and it was refined in Copenhagen, under a monopoly, and sold into a protected home market of two kingdoms. Sugar was one of the great commodities of the century — a luxury turning into a staple across Europe — and Denmark had a share of it that was small by Atlantic standards and very large by Danish ones.

The name to follow is Schimmelmann: a family that combined plantations, the refining business and the state finances until the three were difficult to tell apart.

Vignette · Heinrich Carl Schimmelmann, Copenhagen, 1763

He is not yet forty, he is not Danish, and he has been in the king's service since 1761.

He came from Pomerania by way of Dresden, where he made his first fortune supplying the Prussian army, and took part of his payment from the king of Prussia in the form of the Meissen porcelain works and its stock. In 1759 he bought an estate in Holstein, which made him a subject of the Danish crown. Then Copenhagen — which had heard of him as a financial magician — brought him in to advise on the state's finances.

One of his early recommendations was that the state should sell off large parts of its property. Among the items he named were the four great crown plantations in the Danish West Indies.

This year he buys them. He buys them at a notably advantageous price, and Denmark's largest sugar refinery on Christianshavn with them. He becomes, at a stroke, one of the largest slave owners in the Danish realm: La Grange and Grande Princesse, the two biggest estates on St Croix, Thomas on St Thomas, Carolina on St Jan. By the end of his life about a thousand people are held on them.

Before long he acquires the Kronborg gun factory at Hellebæk as well, sweeping aside a rival bid the state had already accepted.

Follow the circle once. Indian cotton and guns from his own factory in north Zealand are shipped to the Gold Coast and exchanged for people. The people are carried to his plantations. The sugar they cut is refined in his works on Christianshavn and sold under monopoly in Denmark and Norway. The state that takes its share of the profit is advised by him, and from 1768 he is the king's treasurer.

He is painted in 1773 sitting between a portrait of his wife, a bust of Christian 7., and a black serving boy, presumably brought from one of the plantations. He died in 1782 the largest taxpayer in the kingdom.

His son Ernst took charge of the plantations and the works, held together as a family fideikommis, became Denmark's finance minister, and wrote the memorandum that abolished the trade.

Heinrich Carl Schimmelmann, financier · Copenhagen · 1763 · [-]

The Berckentin palace on Bredgade, which Schimmelmann bought, is still there. So is the sugar house quarter on Christianshavn, and so are a good many of the merchant houses along the canals that were paid for out of the same trade. Much of Copenhagen's admired eighteenth-century architecture is, to a degree that has only recently been said out loud in Denmark, a Caribbean building programme executed in the Baltic.

10 / NARRATIVEThe ordinance of 16 March 1792

On 16 March 1792 Christian 7. signed the Forordning om Negerhandelen, and Denmark-Norway became the first slave-trading nation in the world to decide to abolish its transatlantic slave trade.

That sentence is true. It is also, on its own, close to a lie, and the chapter has to do both.

What the ordinance says. From the beginning of 1803, all trade in slaves by the king's subjects was to cease on the African coasts and anywhere else outside the Danish West Indian possessions; after that date no person might be bought there by or for a Danish subject, carried in a Danish subject's ship, or imported for sale to the Danish islands, and any trade against the prohibition would be unlawful.

And what it also says. From now until the end of 1802, it shall be permitted for all nations without distinction and under all flags to import men and women from the coast to Our West Indian islands. Further clauses granted an export concession on two thousand pounds of raw sugar for every healthy adult landed during that window and half that for every half-grown child, and waived the head tax on women brought in.

The ten years were not an administrative delay. They were a subsidised stocking-up. The state's reasoning was that the enslaved population of the islands died faster than it reproduced — the fact this chapter set down after the crossing — so that when the ban took effect, the colony would need a population large enough to sustain itself without further imports. The way to achieve that was to bring in as many people as possible first, and to pay planters to do it.

In those years, from 1793 to 1802, the Danes fetched more people from Africa than ever before or after.

Where it came from. A commission on the slave trade was set up by royal resolution on 5 August 1791, largely at Ernst Schimmelmann's instigation, and it worked fast: its report ran to over a hundred folio pages and was delivered on 28 December 1791.

And why. The commission's own arguments were substantially economic. Its calculations set out the trade's costs — in the purchase of people, in the transport, and in lives, not least the lives of the sailors, who died on those voyages as well. There was a political calculation too: the debate in England was running hot, Denmark correctly judged that Britain was moving toward abolition, and a small carrying nation had reason to be first rather than last. And there was a moral argument, which was real. Schimmelmann was probably a sincere opponent of the trade. He had worked through the physician Paul Isert on schemes for plantations in Africa as an alternative to it, and he eased conditions on his own family's estates.

He also owned those estates, and the people on them, throughout.

That is the figure to end on, and he is not a hypocrite in any simple sense — he is what the Danish Enlightenment actually looked like when it had to pay for itself. A man of genuine humane conviction, who abolished the trade in people and kept the people, who reasoned from the Enlightenment and from the balance sheet in the same memorandum, and who would be finance minister in 1813, when the currency reform remembered as the state bankruptcy came, and was dismissed after it.

What was abolished, and what was not. The trade, not slavery. Buying people in Africa and shipping them across the Atlantic became illegal for Danish subjects in 1803. Owning people in the Danish West Indies, buying and selling them within the islands, working them, and punishing them under the code of 1733 remained entirely lawful for another forty-five years. Slavery in the Danish colonies ended in 1848, and it ended because the enslaved of St Croix rose and forced it.

The 1792 ordinance is taught in Denmark as a first. It was a first. It was a first that carried a ten-year exemption designed to increase the number of people held in bondage, taken by a government whose finance minister owned plantations, and it left the institution itself standing for two generations.

Both halves are the record. A history that keeps only one of them is not describing Denmark; it is doing what the column on Vesterbrogade does, one chapter earlier, for somebody else.

Myth-check

Denmark was the first country to abolish slavery.

Three corrections, in order of size.

It was not slavery. The ordinance of 1792 abolished the transatlantic trade. Slavery in the Danish West Indies remained entirely lawful, and people continued to be bought and sold within the islands, until 1848.

It was not immediate. The ban took effect from the beginning of 1803, and the decade before it was not a wind-down but, as §10 sets out, a licensed and subsidised stocking-up of the islands.

And it did not end because Denmark decided. Emancipation in 1848 was forced from below, by a rising.

What is true. Denmark-Norway was the first slave-trading nation in the world to resolve to end its transatlantic trade, and it did so fifteen years before Britain. The decision was made by a commission whose arguments were substantially economic, at the urging of a minister whose family's plantations held about a thousand people. All of that is the same fact, and a Danish history that reports the first sentence without the rest is not describing what happened.

Summary

IF YOU REMEMBER FIVE THINGSThe page in five

  1. Denmark chartered a West India company in 1671, occupied St Thomas in 1672, added St Jan in 1718, and bought St Croix from France in 1733. On the African coast it held forts — Christiansborg from 1661 and others — on a narrow strip of shoreline, where the business was gold until about 1700 and people thereafter.

  2. Danish ships carried about a hundred and eleven thousand Africans across the Atlantic on about four hundred and thirty voyages, and between one in six and one in five died on the crossing. Denmark's share of the whole trade was small, but it was a carrier nation in its own right.

  3. In September 1733 the governor Philip Gardelin issued a code of nineteen paragraphs declaring the enslaved to have been made so by God and to be their owners' money, with execution, amputation and red-hot tongs among the penalties. In November the Akwamu of St Jan took the fort at Coral Bay, held most of the island for six months, and intended to run the plantations themselves. They were defeated by French troops from Martinique.

  4. The state bought out the company in 1754, and from 1755 the islands were the direct possession of the Danish state. The fortunes came home: Heinrich Carl Schimmelmann advised the crown to sell its plantations, bought them himself in 1763 with the country's largest sugar refinery, and died in 1782 the realm's biggest taxpayer.

  5. On 16 March 1792 an ordinance his son Ernst had pressed for abolished the trade from 1803 — the first such decision by any slave-trading nation — which licensed unrestricted importation until the end of 1802 and granted a sugar-export concession for each adult landed.

Why did it happen that way?

  • Explain why the mortality of the enslaved population exceeding its birth rate made the trade both necessary to the colony and, eventually, arguable against.
  • Why did the Crown's buy-out of the company in 1754 change the moral position of the Danish state, whatever it changed in practice?
  • Why did the ordinance of 1792 include a ten-year delay, and what did the government expect it to achieve?

Counterfactual

What if it had gone otherwise?

  • Suppose the Akwamu had held St Jan. They intended to run the plantations with enslaved people of other African origins. What would that island have been, and how should a history describe it?
  • Suppose Britain had abolished its trade in 1788 rather than 1807. Would Denmark have moved earlier, later, or not at all?

Contested

Where do historians disagree?

  • Was Ernst Schimmelmann a hypocrite? Set out the case that he was, the case that he was a man doing what was possible from inside a system he profited by, and say what evidence would distinguish them.
  • Denmark's share of the whole Atlantic trade was under one per cent, and Denmark was a carrier nation in its own right, with its own forts, companies and flag. Which of those two true statements belongs in a school textbook, and why?
  • The St Jan rising was a bid for freedom by people who intended to enslave others. How should that be told — and does the answer change depending on who is telling it?

Go and look

STILL THEREPlaces you can visit

  • Christiansborg, Accra. Osu Castle, on the Ghanaian coast, now a memorial and a museum. The casemates are under the rooms where the governor lived.
  • Christiansted and Frederiksted, St Croix. The two towns of the 1734 grid, with the street plans as they were surveyed. Fort Frederik at Frederiksted is where emancipation was declared in 1848, after the rising.
  • Fortsberg, Coral Bay, St John. The small star-shaped redoubt taken at dawn in November 1733. It is a short walk and a very small fort.
  • KUBEN, Arendal. The Fredensborg exhibition, with the objects raised from the wreck and the transcribed journals of the last voyage.
  • Odd Fellow Palæet, Bredgade, Copenhagen. The Berckentin palace, bought by Heinrich Carl Schimmelmann. Then walk to Christianshavn and look at the warehouses along the canal. The sugar came here.
  • Sølyst, Klampenborg. Where Ernst Schimmelmann wrote the letter of 18 June 1791 that began the process, in a house paid for by the plantations.
