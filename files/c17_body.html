The union at work, and the end of Margrete

1397 – 1412 · Margrete 1. · Erik af Pommern · Peder Jensen Lodehat · Abraham Brodersen

The union is signed. Now it has to be run — by appointments, not by documents. In fifteen years Norway slides from partner to province, a colony in Greenland stops existing without anyone noticing, and a third of the farms in some Danish districts stand empty.

Introduction

WHAT THIS PAGE ANSWERSFive questions

  1. What did the union consist of, day to day, once the documents were signed?
  2. Norway entered as an equal partner in 1380. How was it a province by 1412, when nobody decided that it should be?
  3. A European colony in the Atlantic ceased to exist during these years. Why did nobody in Copenhagen notice for three centuries?
  4. Behind the largest state in Europe, a third of the farms in some districts were standing empty. How do those two facts belong to the same country?
  5. Margrete died with the union intact and no crisis. What did she leave that held, and what did she leave that did not?

01 / NARRATIVEWhat the union actually was

Danish terms in this section

rigsråd
The council of the realm: the bishops and great nobles who advised, elected and, increasingly, bargained. Each kingdom kept its own.
fællesskab
Community, in the sense the union letter uses it — three kingdoms acting as one abroad while remaining three at home.

Day to day, the union was a set of appointments. Castles and districts in Sweden and Norway went increasingly to men she trusted, and a growing share of those were Danish or Danish-connected. In Norway the effect was structural: with a thinned aristocracy and no resident king, the kingdom slid within a generation from partner to province in everything but name.

What it did deliver was the thing it was built for. Between 1389 and 1412 the three kingdoms did not fight each other, which none of the preceding three generations could say, and abroad they acted as one bloc against Mecklenburg, the Teutonic Order and — carefully, without ever quite forcing a confrontation — the Hanseatic towns. That is her foreign policy in a sentence: she never fought a war she could buy her way out of, and she bought a great deal.

Vignette · the man from Graudenz

In 1402 a poor and sick man turns up in the town of Graudenz in Prussia, in the territory of the Teutonic Order. Merchants notice that he looks remarkably like the late King Oluf, dead these fifteen years. He is taken to Danzig. He says that he is Oluf; that he did not die at Falsterbo; that he was taken away and has been living obscurely ever since. People begin to honour him as a king.

The chronicler who records this is Johann von Posilge, one of the main sources for the Order's history, and the Order's own correspondence survives too: in June 1402 thirteen Danish knights and squires write to the Grand Master asking that the man be handed over, and in July the Grand Master writes to Margrete about it. He was given up. There was a public trial in Denmark, he was condemned, and he was burned at the market at Falsterbo — the same place Oluf had died — in front of merchants from across northern Europe, which was the point of choosing it.

He has no name in this page because he had none of his own to give: the only one he offered was somebody else's. What his case shows is the cost of the manner of Oluf's death. A young king dies without warning; his mother's power is transformed by it within a week; and fifteen years later a sick stranger in Prussia can be believed, by enough people to matter, because the story had a shape that fitted.

An unnamed man of Graudenz, called 'the false Oluf' · the market at Falsterbo, Skåne · 1402

Meanwhile in Europe

The whole of Margrete's career falls inside the Great Schism. From 1378 there were two popes, at Rome and Avignon, each with a full apparatus of cardinals, courts and revenue, each excommunicating the other's supporters; from 1409 there were briefly three. The Nordic kingdoms adhered to Rome throughout, which put them on the same side as England, the Empire and Hungary, and on the other side from France, Scotland and Castile.

For a ruler this was less a spiritual crisis than an opportunity: a pope with a rival is a pope who needs friends, and appointments to bishoprics — the richest offices in any kingdom — became much easier to influence. The Schism was closed at the Council of Constance in 1417, five years after her death, by a council that also burned Jan Hus.

02 / NARRATIVENorway, from partner to province

Danish terms in this section

skattland
Tributary land: a territory under the Norwegian crown that was not part of the kingdom proper. Iceland, the Faroes, Greenland, Orkney and Shetland were all skattlande.
lensmand
The holder of a castle and its district. Who got these in Norway, and where he came from, is the whole of this section.
Bergenhus
The castle at Bergen, and with it the Norwegian west coast, the Atlantic trade and the German quarter. The most important appointment in Norway.

In 1380 Norway and Denmark entered a personal union of equals, and chapter 16 said that within thirty years it was not one. This is where that happens, and it is worth watching closely, because nobody decided it.

Start with the arithmetic of who was available. Norway had been hit harder by the plague than Denmark — the estimates for mortality run higher and the recovery slower — and the effect on its aristocracy was disproportionate, because a noble family that fails in the male line in one generation is gone. By 1400 the Norwegian council of the realm was a much smaller body than it had been in 1350, drawn from fewer families, and several of the great lineages had simply ended. A kingdom that cannot staff its own government from its own families will be staffed from somewhere else.

Then the appointments. Margrete governed Norway as she governed everywhere, through castles and the men who held them, and the men she trusted were increasingly Danish or Danish-connected. Akershus, Bergenhus, Tunsberghus: the pattern over her lifetime is a slow substitution, not a purge. Norwegian families kept local office and lost the great commands. And because the king was not resident — Erik was in Denmark, and after 1397 the government of three kingdoms ran from wherever Margrete was — the Norwegian council met less, and mattered less each time it met.

Then the money. Norwegian trade was in Hanseatic hands, run through the German quarter at Bergen, which meant that the most valuable economic activity in the kingdom generated revenue for merchants in Lübeck and customs for whoever held Bergenhus, and very little else for anybody in Norway. The archbishop at Nidaros remained a real power, and the church is the one Norwegian institution that does not decline in this period. Everything secular does.

None of this was policy. There is no document in which anyone proposes to reduce Norway, and Margrete's instruction to Erik about Norwegian government, drawn up in her last years, reads like the advice of a ruler who took the kingdom seriously. What there is instead is a hundred small decisions each of which made sense at the time, taken by a government whose centre of gravity was in Denmark, in a kingdom that had lost the people who might have objected.

The formal recognition comes much later and belongs to chapter 20: the recess of 1536 declares that Norway shall be under the Danish crown like any other Danish province. But the thing that sentence describes had been true in practice for well over a century by the time anyone wrote it down.

What came with Norway is on the map, and it is the part of the Danish realm that Danish history habitually forgets. Iceland had its own law and its own assembly and saw a royal official rarely; its trade was supposed to run through Bergen, and increasingly did not, because English fishermen had discovered the Icelandic banks and would spend the fifteenth century there in defiance of everybody. The Faroes were smaller and quieter and stayed. Orkney and Shetland were still Norwegian and would be until 1468, when chapter 19 pledges them away for a dowry.

03 / NARRATIVEThe last ship to Greenland

Danish terms in this section

Østerbygden
The Eastern Settlement, in the south-west of Greenland around present-day Qaqortoq — the larger of the two Norse colonies, with a cathedral at Garðar and something like a dozen parish churches.
Vesterbygden
The Western Settlement, further north near present-day Nuuk. Abandoned some time around the middle of the fourteenth century.
Grønlandsfarere
The Greenland ships. By this period one or two a year in a good decade, sailing from Bergen, and by the 1400s not even that.

Somewhere in these years a European colony that had existed for four hundred years stopped existing, and nobody in Copenhagen noticed for three hundred more.

The Norse settlements in Greenland were founded around 985, converted about 1000, and had a bishop at Garðar from 1126. At their height perhaps four or five thousand people lived in them, farming sheep and cattle on the fjord meadows, hunting seal and caribou, and paying for imported iron and timber with walrus ivory and hides. They were the westernmost part of the Norwegian crown and, from 1380, the westernmost part of a realm ruled from Denmark.

By 1400 they were in serious trouble, for reasons that are still argued about: a cooling climate shortening the growing season, the collapse of the European ivory market once elephant ivory returned through Portuguese trade, the near-cessation of shipping from Bergen, and possibly conflict with the Thule Inuit who had moved down the coast. The Western Settlement had already gone — Ivar Bárðarson, sent from Garðar to investigate some time around the middle of the century, reported finding livestock running wild and no people at all.

Vignette · a wedding at Hvalsey, 16 September 1408

Two Icelanders, Thorstein Olafsson and Sigrid Björnsdóttir, are married in the church at Hvalsey in the Eastern Settlement. They had not meant to be in Greenland at all; they were sailing from Norway to Iceland and were driven off course, and they have been there some years.

The church is stone, about sixteen metres by eight, with gabled ends and a doorway that would not look out of place in Orkney. It still stands, roofless, and it is the best-preserved Norse building in Greenland.

We know about the wedding because of paperwork. Two priests, Eindridi Andresson and Pall Halvardsson, read the banns on three consecutive Sundays; nobody objected; and on 16 April of the following year the bishop's official at Garðar issued a certificate saying so. The couple got back to Iceland, and the documents that survive are the copies they had made there afterwards — the kind of paperwork you keep because it proves a marriage and therefore an inheritance.

That certificate is the last written record of the Norse in Greenland. Not the last event: people went on living there, and the archaeology suggests the Eastern Settlement lasted into the second half of the century, perhaps the 1450s. But after 1408 the written record simply stops, because the ships stopped, and a society with no ships generates no documents that anyone else will keep.

When Hans Egede landed in 1721 looking for the descendants of these people — chapter 27 — he found ruins, and Inuit, and no Norse at all. He also found the church at Hvalsey standing much as it stands now.

Thorstein Olafsson and Sigrid Björnsdóttir · Hvalsey church, the Eastern Settlement, Greenland · 16 September 1408

There is no moment of decision here and no policy to criticise. Margrete's government did not abandon Greenland; it simply never thought about it, because a place that produces no revenue and sends no complaints does not appear on anyone's agenda. The Greenland trade had been a royal monopoly since 1294, which meant that when the crown lost interest there was no one else legally able to sail there.

The colony's disappearance is the largest thing that happens in the Danish realm during Erik's minority, and it happens entirely off the page — which is why the map in the previous section is worth looking at again. Something like three thousand kilometres of ocean, one or two ships a year, and then none.

04 / NARRATIVEThe land behind the union

Danish terms in this section

ødegård
A deserted farm. Chapter 15's word, and still the central fact of the Danish countryside — the problem peaks not in the plague years but around 1400.
landgilde
The annual rent a tenant paid his landlord, mostly in produce.
fæste
Tenancy: the holding of a farm from a landlord against rent and services. From this period it is the normal way a Danish peasant holds land, and it stays that way until the 1780s.
hovedgård
A manor: the lord's own farm, worked separately, with tenant farms attached to it.

All of this was happening on top of a countryside in the worst condition it had been in for three hundred years, and a chapter about three crowns that does not say so has told half a story.

Chapter 15 left the land emptying, and it went on emptying. Deeds and mortgage letters — which distinguish carefully between farms in use, farms partly in use and farms lying waste — show the ødegård problem climbing from the 1360s to a peak in the twenty years after 1400. The most alarming surviving figure is from the Brøndum estate in north-western Himmerland: of forty-eight farms, thirty-four were waste in 1400 or 1401. That is one estate in one poor district and must not be made a national average — but it is what landlords were looking at.

Rents collapsed. Where we can trace landgilde across the period — on the confiscated Fiskbæk property, and from an altar endowment of 1416 — the level reached shortly after 1400 is roughly the level still being paid at the Reformation, and indeed down to the great agrarian reforms at the end of the eighteenth century. A Danish landlord in 1400 was taking a cut in income that his family would never recover.

What the landlords did about it reshaped the Danish village. The old distinctions — the bryde who managed a large farm for his lord, the landbo who rented one, the gårdsæde with his cottage and few acres — blurred into a single figure, the fæstebonde, holding a farm of standard size on standard terms. Holdings were merged and equalised so each tenant in a village owed roughly what his neighbour owed, small manors were absorbed into larger ones, and because a waste farm produced nothing it became normal to let a new tenant in rent-free for a few years to get it going.

Who gained? In the short run the peasants, as in chapter 15: land was abundant, people were scarce, and a man who could work could get a farm on terms his grandfather would not have believed. Taking a fæste was cheaper than inheriting freehold, too, since there were no co-heirs to buy out. In the long run the winners were the noble families who used two generations of cheap land to assemble the estates that dominate the Danish countryside from the sixteenth century on — and whose descendants sit in the council that elects Christian 1. in 1448 and dictates terms in 1460. The union's political class was being built out of deserted farms.

It is worth holding the two pictures together, because they are the same country in the same decade: the largest state in Europe, and a district in Himmerland where two farms in three had nobody on them.

05 / NARRATIVEBirgitta, and what piety bought

Danish terms in this section

sjælegave
A soul-gift: property given to the church so that masses would be said for the giver's soul. The largest single transfer of land in late medieval Denmark ran through this.
birgittinerorden
The Bridgettine order, founded on Birgitta of Sweden's rule, with its mother house at Vadstena — double houses of nuns and monks under an abbess.

Margrete was conventionally, expensively and usefully pious, and it is a mistake to sort those three adjectives into sincere and cynical. They were the same activity.

In 1391 Birgitta of Sweden was canonised at Rome. She had died in 1373, her daughter Katarina had pushed the process, and Vadstena — the house Margrete's own upbringing connected her to through Merete Ulvsdatter — became the mother house of a new order and the most important religious centre in Scandinavia. A Swedish noblewoman canonised by the Roman pope in the middle of a schism, in the years when a Danish-born ruler was assembling a Nordic union: none of these caused each other, but they ran together, and she was attentive to all of them.

So did many other houses. She gave land and money on a scale that shows plainly in the records, endowed masses, and — a detail preserved at Roskilde — she and her women embroidered altar cloths for the cathedral themselves. The flow of property from lay into ecclesiastical hands through sjælegave was one of the great economic movements of the late Danish Middle Ages, and by 1500 the church held something like a third of the land in the kingdom. That accumulation is what makes 1536 worth doing: the Reformation's fiscal logic is laid down here, gift by gift, by the most pious rulers in Danish history.

For the ordinary parish these were prosperous decades, oddly enough: fewer people, but more land and more surplus each, and the fifteenth century is when Danish village churches get their vaults, towers and wall paintings. A parish that could not have afforded a tower in 1340 built one in 1440.

And underneath the piety, the government. Bishoprics were the great offices of state; the chancellor of Denmark in this period was Peder Jensen Lodehat, bishop of Roskilde, who was Margrete's man in the way that mattered — her closest administrative ally, the executor of her will, and, in 1413, the man who removed her body from the abbey she had chosen and took it to his own cathedral. Chapter 12 described a church being built as an institution with its own interests, its own income and its own courts. Here it is at its height, and thoroughly harnessed.

06 / NARRATIVEThe alabaster and the gown

Danish terms in this section

landefred
The land-peace: the king's peace, breach of which was a capital matter. The charge on which Abraham Brodersen died.
gravmæle
A funerary monument. Margrete's, at Roskilde, is the finest surviving object of the Danish Middle Ages.

The last years went on the south. Gerhard 6. of Holstein, enfeoffed with Schleswig in 1386, was killed in 1404 fighting the peasant republic of Ditmarsken — which does the same to a Danish royal army in 1500, in chapter 19. His widow Elisabeth was left guardian of young sons and permanently short of money, and Margrete did what she always did: she bought. District by district, she redeemed or purchased pieces of Sønderjylland back towards the crown, with Erik installed as guardian of the ducal heirs. It was slow, legal, expensive and very nearly successful. The Holsteiners attacked before it was finished, the war was going Denmark's way when she died, and everything unresolved was handed to Erik, who lost it. That is chapter 18.

Vignette · outside Sønderborg, 27 August 1410

Abraham Brodersen Baad is one of the most successful men in the union and nobody knows who his parents were. He appears in the 1370s doing business with the crown; he is rich in his own right, with lands in Halland, Västergötland and Småland; and he does the thing that made careers under Margrete — he lends her money and takes castles and districts in pledge against it. He commands at the sieges of Stockholm in the 1390s. He leads the failed attempt on Gotland in 1403. He is exactly the unionsadel the union was built for.

In the summer of 1410 he is with King Erik's army in Sønderjylland, besieging Als. There he is accused. The charge is breach of the landefred. What it consisted of moves depending on which source you read: first that he was not pressing the campaign hard enough, then that he had raped a girl, then tyranny and taking the law into his own hands. One later source lets slip what may be the real complaint — that he kept more armed men about him than the king did. On 27 August he is beheaded outside Sønderborg.

Who ordered it is not known. Margrete was in Sweden. Erik was there and was twenty-eight and had been kept a junior partner for thirteen years. His estates were seized; and before his heirs were allowed to have them back, they had to state in writing that he had been lawfully put to death according to the law of the land.

That last requirement is the government of this chapter in one sentence. It was not enough to kill a great man and take his property. The family had to sign.

Abraham Brodersen Baad, knight and councillor · outside Sønderborg on Als · 27 August 1410

Margrete died on 28 October 1412 at Flensburg, by the usual account on board her ship in the harbour. She was fifty-nine. Plague is the common explanation and it is not certain. Erik succeeded without a crisis, without a negotiation and without an interregnum, which had not happened in living memory and would not happen again for a long time.

She had asked to be buried at Sorø, beside her father, and she was — for a few months. Then the bishop of Roskilde, Peder Jensen Lodehat, her own chancellor, had the body removed to his cathedral, on the stated ground that Roskilde was the proper burial church for the rulers of a union and Sorø too small and too Danish. The abbey's monks called it what it was.

What he built over her, and what Erik paid for in 1423, is still there. A sarcophagus of dark limestone with alabaster reliefs, and on top of it a recumbent figure of a woman. Only the coffin and most of the effigy are medieval; everything else was reconstructed between 1862 and 1912 from surviving fragments. The work is generally attributed to Johannes Junge, a sculptor known in Lübeck between 1406 and 1428, on the strength of style rather than documents; a small alabaster bust in Lübeck, perhaps a failed study for the figure, is the nearest thing to evidence. The face is that of a young woman. She was nearly sixty when she died. On the hem of her gown, carved into the stone, are little bells, which were fashionable in the 1400s.

Beside the tomb there used to hang a dress: gold brocade, gilded silver thread on purple silk, probably woven at Lucca. Swedish troops took it in 1659 along with the rest of the loose Margrete relics, and it is in Uppsala cathedral now; Roskilde has a copy. For centuries it was shown as the wedding dress of the ten-year-old bride of 1363. A radiocarbon date puts the cloth somewhere between 1403 and 1439, and the dress historians agree with the laboratory. So it is not the wedding dress. It may well have been hers, and it may have lain on the sarcophagus.

The sarcophagus has never been opened. One Danish account notes, drily, that the question could be settled by looking inside and seeing whether she would have fitted the dress.

Meanwhile in Europe

The years of Margrete's last decade were the ones in which two long arguments in Europe came to a head. On 15 July 1410 the Teutonic Order — the power that had taken Gotland from her in 1398 and sold it back in 1408 — was destroyed as a military force at Grunwald, or Tannenberg, by a Polish and Lithuanian army. It never recovered its old position in the Baltic, which removed one of the three threats the union had been built against.

And in 1409 an attempt to end the papal schism by deposing both popes produced a third one instead. That absurdity is what finally forced the Council of Constance in 1414, which closed the schism, burned Jan Hus, and established for a generation the principle that a council could discipline a pope — the argument that will be running again, in a very different form, when chapter 19 opens.

Myth-check

"The golden gown at Uppsala is Margrete's wedding dress."
Carbon-14 dates the fabric to roughly 1403–1439 and stylistic analysis agrees, so it cannot be from the wedding of 1363. It may still have been hers — she died in 1412 — and it was kept beside her tomb until Swedish troops removed it in 1659. Not everything taken as war booty was invented, and not every relic is what the label says.
"Oluf did not really die at Falsterbo."
This was a live claim in 1402, and the man who made it was burned for it. It is worth taking seriously as evidence of something other than survival: a young king had died suddenly, his mother's power had been transformed by it within a week, and fifteen years later enough people found that story suspicious for a sick stranger in Prussia to be believed.
"Denmark abandoned Norse Greenland."
Abandonment implies a decision, and there was none. The Greenland trade had been a royal monopoly since 1294, so when the crown lost interest nobody else could legally sail there; the ships thinned to one or two a year and then stopped. The last written record is a wedding certificate from 1408. Nobody in Copenhagen appears to have asked what had happened until Hans Egede went to look in 1721.
"Margrete ruled through Erik as a puppet."
Broadly true and worth stating precisely. He was crowned in 1397 at about fifteen and did not govern until she died in 1412, when he was thirty. But the union letter guarantees her holdings for life, not his, and the coronation document discharges her of any liability for everything she had done. The arrangement was written down, by people who knew exactly what they were writing.

Summary

IF YOU REMEMBER FIVE THINGSThe page in five

  1. Day to day the union was a set of appointments. Castles and districts went to men Margrete trusted, a growing share of them Danish, and a ruler who controls the castles does not often need to ask the council what it thinks.

  2. Norway slid from partner to province inside a generation without anyone deciding it: an aristocracy thinned by plague, no resident king, trade in Hanseatic hands, and the great commands going to Danes.

  3. The Norse settlements in Greenland stopped generating records after a wedding at Hvalsey on 16 September 1408. There was no abandonment and no decision — the trade was a royal monopoly, the crown lost interest, and nobody else could legally sail there.

  4. The Danish countryside was at its emptiest: deserted farms peaked in the twenty years after 1400, rents fell to a level that held for four centuries, and the tenancy system and the great noble estates of early modern Denmark were assembled out of the wreckage.

  5. Margrete died at Flensburg on 28 October 1412 and Erik succeeded without a crisis. She was buried at Sorø by her own wish, and the bishop of Roskilde — her own chancellor — had the body taken to his cathedral, where the alabaster tomb still is.

Counterfactual

What if it had gone otherwise?

  • If the Bergen ships had kept sailing, does Norse Greenland survive — or was the climate going to end it either way?
  • If Margrete had lived another ten years, would Erik have governed any better for the practice?
  • If Norway had kept its aristocracy through the plague, is the union a partnership of three rather than a Danish one?

Contested

Where the argument still is

  • Why did Norse Greenland end? Cooling climate, the collapse of the walrus-ivory market, the failure of shipping, conflict with the Thule Inuit, or a society too specialised to adapt. All are argued; the evidence does not settle it.
  • Who accused Abraham Brodersen in 1410, and of what? The charge shifts between sources, the queen was in Sweden, and the heirs had to certify the execution as lawful before recovering the estates.
  • How much did Danish dominance of the union owe to design and how much to demography?
  • Was Margrete's church patronage piety, statecraft, or a distinction the period would not have recognised?
