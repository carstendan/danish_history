Kingdom and church take shape

1050 – 1157 · Svend Estridsen · Knud den Hellige · Asser · Niels · Valdemar

A century in which Denmark stopped being paid for by other countries and had to be paid for by Danes. The instrument that made that possible was the church — and once it existed, it turned out to have interests of its own.

01 / NARRATIVEThe king Adam of Bremen came to see

Danish terms in this section

Estridsen
'Son of Estrid'. Danish kings are normally named for their fathers; that Svend is named for his mother tells you where the claim came from.
leding
The naval levy: the obligation on coastal districts to supply ships, crews and provisions. The kingdom's one reliable instrument of force.

Chapter 11 ended with the North Sea Empire gone and the throne passing to Svend Estridsen — not a descendant of Cnut but a nephew, the son of Cnut's sister Estrid and of the Ulf Jarl whom Cnut had had killed in a church at Roskilde. Every Danish monarch since, including the present one, descends from him.

He held the throne for roughly twenty-seven years, from about 1047 to 1074, and for the first seventeen of them he was at war with Norway — first with Magnus the Good, then with Harald Hardrada — and lost most of the battles. Peace came in 1064. He never took England, though he had a claim as good as anyone's and sent a fleet to support the English rebellion against William in 1069. It achieved nothing except a payoff.

By the standards of the North Sea Empire (11) this is a poor record. Measured differently it is the most consequential reign of the century, because Svend was the first Danish king who had to make the country pay for itself. The English tribute was over. The eastern silver had failed two generations earlier. What was left was land, and the people on it, and the tolls of a few small towns — and none of that could be extracted without machinery the kingdom did not yet have.

What Svend understood, and what makes him the hinge of this page, is that the church was the only available machinery. It could write. It could hold land in perpetuity without an heir to divide it. It had a claim on income that was independent of whether the king had won his last battle. And it answered, in the last instance, to somebody a very long way away.

Vignette · a visitor takes notes

Sometime around 1070 a canon from Bremen arrives at the Danish court. He has come to collect material for a history of his own archbishopric, and what he wants from Denmark is information: who was converted, by whom, and on whose authority.

He gets more than he bargained for. The king turns out to be a talker — a man who can recite the descent of the Danish kings, the campaigns of his uncle, the geography of the Baltic and the customs of the Swedes, and who is happy to do so at length. Adam of Bremen names him as a source repeatedly and with something close to affection, and much of what we know about Denmark before 1050 we know because Svend Estridsen told it to a German cleric who wrote it down.

The awkwardness is that both men had reasons to shape it. Adam was building a case that the North belonged to Hamburg-Bremen. Svend was building a case that Denmark was a proper Christian kingdom that deserved an archbishop of its own. They were, in the most polite possible way, negotiating — and the result is our principal source.

02 / NARRATIVEEight dioceses

Danish terms in this section

stift
A diocese: a bishop's territory, and the basic administrative unit of the medieval Danish church.
domkirke
Cathedral — literally the church that holds the bishop's seat.

Around 1060, Denmark was divided into eight dioceses: Slesvig, Ribe, Århus, Viborg and Vendsyssel west of the Belts; Odense, Roskilde and Lund east of them. A ninth, at Dalby in Skåne, lasted about six years before being folded back into Lund.

This is the single most durable thing anyone did in this century. The boundaries drawn around 1060 survive, with modifications, as the Danish diocesan map today. Look at the figure below: Copenhagen is not on it, because Copenhagen does not exist yet. Viborg and Ribe and Odense are, and they are still cathedral cities nine hundred and sixty years later.

Two things are worth noticing about the arrangement. The first is that it is a grid. It covers the whole kingdom, it has no gaps, and every square kilometre of Denmark now falls inside somebody's jurisdiction. Nothing like that had existed before. Chapter 10's ring fortresses were an attempt at the same thing by other means, and they lasted perhaps twenty years; this lasted a millennium.

The second is that it was not the king's idea alone. Adam reports that the division was agreed at a meeting between Svend and Archbishop Adalbert of Hamburg-Bremen in 1053, and Adalbert had his own project: a great northern patriarchate, run from Bremen, covering Scandinavia and the Slav lands. The eight Danish sees were meant to be his suffragans. Svend went along with it and then spent the rest of his life trying to get out from under it. He failed. His son's son succeeded.

03 / NARRATIVELearning to pay for a kingdom

Danish terms in this section

kongelev
Crown land: estates attached to the office of king, passing with the throne rather than to heirs.
patrimonium
The king's private inherited land, as distinct from kongelev. The distinction matters enormously, and was frequently ignored.
bryde
The manager of an estate farm on another man's land — free, but not a landowner. The most common kind of substantial Danish farmer in this period.

Chapter 11 promised that Svend Estridsen's sons would spend their reigns learning to fund a kingdom from home rather than abroad, and would mostly fail. Here is the failure in detail.

A Danish king around 1075 had four sources of income. He had his own land, which was substantial but not enormous. He had the leding, which produced ships and men but not cash, and which could be commuted into a payment only if the men agreed. He had fines from the assemblies — the ting — of which he took a share. And he had a set of claims that sound minor and turned out to be explosive: the right to goods from shipwrecks, to the property of people who died without heirs, to the estates of the convicted, and to a portion of everything found.

What he did not have was a tax. There was no annual levy on land or produce that the crown could count on. This is the whole problem of the twelfth century in one sentence, and the whole reason the church matters: the tithe, when it finally arrived, was the first regular, universal, annual obligation ever laid on Danish farmers. The crown did not collect it. But somebody had shown that it could be done.

Svend had, by the chroniclers' reckoning, around twenty sons, none of them born of a marriage the church would recognise. Five of them became king, one after another, over sixty years: Harald Hen, Knud, Oluf, Erik and Niels. It is worth pausing on how unusual that is. Five brothers, five orderly successions, no civil war. Compared with what follows it looks like a golden age, and compared with England in the same decades it looks like competence.

Harald Hen (1074–80) got a nickname meaning something like 'whetstone' or 'soft', and a reputation for mildness and law-making that rests on very little evidence. Then came his brother Knud, and the mildness stopped.

04 / NARRATIVEOdense, 10 July 1086

Danish terms in this section

Knud den Hellige
Canute the Holy, king 1080–86. Not to be confused with Knud den Store (Cnut the Great, chapter 11) or Knud Lavard (section 11 below). Danish history has too many Knuds.
vrag
Wreck. The king's claim to wrecked ships and their cargo — a small right that made him a great many enemies on a coastline this long.

Knud den Hellige ruled for six years and did three things that mattered. He pressed the royal claims — wreck, ownerless property, fines — harder than anyone had before. He gave lavishly to the church, including a gift charter to St Laurence's in Lund in 1085 that survives and is the oldest original document preserved in Scandinavia. And he tried to invade England.

The invasion is the hinge. In 1085 Knud assembled a fleet in the Limfjord, in alliance with Olaf of Norway and his own father-in-law Robert of Flanders. It was a serious attempt — William the Conqueror took it seriously enough to bring mercenaries across the Channel and lay waste the coast. Then Knud delayed. He was in Schleswig, dealing with the southern border; the fleet sat waiting through the season; and eventually the levy went home.

And then the king fined them for leaving.

This is the moment the whole thing turns. A leding summons was an obligation, but it was an obligation understood as reciprocal — the men came, the campaign happened, there was plunder. To be summoned, to wait, to be sent nothing, and then to be fined for going home was a breach of the bargain. In the winter of 1085–86 Vendsyssel rose.

Vignette · the last hour in St Alban's

The king has been moving south ahead of the rising for weeks — out of Vendsyssel, through Jutland, across to Fyn. On 10 July 1086 he is in the wooden church of St Alban in Odense, with his brother Benedikt and a small band of followers, and the men from Jutland have caught up with him.

What we are told is that they threw stones and spears through the windows; that Benedikt held the door; that Knud confessed, took communion, and was killed in front of the altar by a spear through the side; and that seventeen others died with him. He was thirty-three or so.

Every detail of that comes from Ælnoth of Canterbury, an English monk living at Odense, who wrote it down more than thirty years later, in Latin, for the express purpose of establishing that a saint had died there. The spear in the side is doing theological work. The confession and communion are doing theological work. What is almost certainly true is the bare shape: the king of Denmark was hunted across his own kingdom by his own subjects and killed in a church on Fyn, and nobody intervened.

Knud den Hellige, in Ælnoth's telling · St Alban's church, Odense · 10 July 1086

His brother Oluf succeeded, and his reign coincided with a run of bad harvests severe enough to earn him the name Hunger. The chroniclers — who by then had an interest in the matter — explained the famine as divine punishment for the killing of Knud.

05 / NARRATIVEMaking a martyr useful

Fifteen years after Knud was killed as a tyrant, he was a saint.

The mechanics are worth following, because they show a kingdom learning to use an institution. His brother Erik Ejegod — 'ever-good' — came to the throne in 1095 and began the work immediately: gifts to Odense, cultivation of the monks there, reports of miracles at the tomb, and then a journey. In 1098 Erik went to Bari in southern Italy and put the case to Pope Urban II in person. Approval came. On 19 April 1101 Knud's bones were lifted from the ground and moved to the high altar of the church at Odense that now bears his name.

A translation of relics is not a coronation, but it does something a coronation cannot. It makes the royal family holy. The man the Jutlanders killed for over-taxing them was now a martyr, which meant that killing him had been not merely a crime but a sin, which meant that the dynasty he belonged to had a claim on obedience that no assembly could vote down.

Denmark had, in other words, acquired its first saint, and it was a king, and he had been murdered by Danes. Every subsequent Danish royal saint follows the same template — Knud Lavard, canonised in 1170, most of all. The Valdemar age (13) will show his son making a state out of it.

Meanwhile in Europe

The timing of all this is not Danish. In 1075 Pope Gregory VII asserted that the pope could depose emperors; in January 1077 the Emperor Henry IV stood in the snow at Canossa waiting to be forgiven. The Investiture Contest — over who appoints bishops, the pope or the ruler — dominated European politics for fifty years, and it is the reason a small northern kingdom could get what it wanted.

Rome needed counterweights to the German emperor. Hamburg-Bremen was an imperial archbishopric. Cutting Scandinavia out of it and attaching it directly to the papacy served Rome's interests exactly, and the Danish kings knew it. Erik Ejegod's journey to Bari in 1098 took place while the First Crusade was on the road; Jerusalem fell in July 1099. A papacy that had just discovered it could move armies across a continent was a papacy worth asking for a favour.

06 / NARRATIVELund, 1103

Danish terms in this section

ærkesæde
Archiepiscopal see: the seat of an archbishop, who has ordinary bishops under him.
kirkeprovins
Church province — the territory of an archbishop. From 1103/04 the Nordic province is run from Lund.
pallium
The woollen band sent from Rome that makes an archbishop an archbishop. Without it the title is empty.

Erik Ejegod asked for two things at Bari and got both. The second was an archbishop of his own.

The date is usually given as 1103 or 1104, and the doubling is not sloppiness: Paschal II appears to have granted it in 1103, and Asser, bishop of Lund, received the pallium and was fully installed by 1104. The Danish church — and with it Norway, Sweden, Iceland, the Faroes and Greenland — left the province of Hamburg-Bremen after nearly three hundred years.

Two things about this are usually left out of the schoolbook version.

The first is the price. Rome did not give away a province for nothing. The counter-demand was Peter's Pence paid directly to the papacy, and the tithe — the levy of a tenth on agricultural produce, which was standard across western Europe and which Denmark did not yet have. Erik agreed to it in 1103. It cannot be documented as actually being collected before 1135. Thirty years elapsed between the promise and the payment, and those thirty years are not empty: they are the period in which somebody had to go out to every district in Denmark and explain to farmers that they now owed a tenth of their grain, every year, forever. We will come back to what that felt like.

The second is that it nearly did not stick. Hamburg-Bremen never accepted the loss. In 1133 Archbishop Adalbert of Hamburg-Bremen obtained a set of papal bulls placing Lund and every other Nordic see back under Bremen, exactly as before 1104. The reversal was undone only because a canon of Lund named Herman — a Rhinelander, a failed abbot, a man who had drifted north after his ambitions collapsed in Germany — was sent to Rome and argued the case back. Denmark's ecclesiastical independence, the thing every Danish account treats as the natural outcome of 1103, survived on the performance of one cleric on one embassy.

Erik Ejegod did not live to see the pallium arrive. He had set off on pilgrimage to Jerusalem, and died on Cyprus in July 1103. His queen Bodil reached the Holy Land and died on the Mount of Olives.

07 / NARRATIVEWhat a tithe cost

Danish terms in this section

tiende
Tithe: a tenth of the year's grain, owed annually. In Denmark it was divided three ways — bishop, priest, and the fabric of the church building.
tiendelade
Tithe barn: the building the parish's grain went into. Some survive.
kirkeværge
Churchwarden — the layman responsible for the building's third of the tithe, and for the fabric.

Here is the sentence this page exists to earn: for an ordinary Danish farming household, the arrival of Christianity as an institution meant a permanent ten per cent cut in the harvest.

Not a donation. Not an offering at the great feasts. An annual, calculable, enforceable tenth of the grain, assessed in the field before it was cut, and owed whether the year was good or bad. In a subsistence agriculture where the gap between a sufficient year and a hungry one might be fifteen or twenty per cent of the crop, a tenth is not a nuisance. It is the difference between a household that gets through the winter and one that does not.

The Danish tithe had one unusual feature, and it needs stating precisely, because the loose version of it is wrong. Canon law prescribed a division into four, with a quarter going to the poor. That prescription was widely ignored — England, among others, commonly divided in three. What is distinctive about Denmark is not that it departed from European practice but that the threefold division was simply the rule: bishop, parish priest, and the fabric of the church building, with no share for the poor written in at all. The quarter that canon law assigned to almsgiving went, in Denmark, into masonry. Sections 09 and 10 are about what that bought.

Resistance was continuous and it is under-recorded, because the people who wrote things down were the people receiving the tithe. What we can see is the shape of it: the thirty-year gap between Erik's promise in 1103 and the first documentary trace of collection in the 1130s; repeated concessions on how the tenth was to be assessed; and, in Skåne, a series of risings against the archbishop's demands that run through the rest of the century and boil over in the 1180s, which is chapter 13's problem.

It is worth holding both halves of this in mind at once. The tithe is what made the parish church, the parish priest, the parish school of a sort, and eventually the parish register — the infrastructure of ordinary Danish life for the next eight hundred years. It was also, from the farmer's side, a new and very large annual bill that had not existed in his grandfather's time, and which he had no way to refuse.

08 / NARRATIVEThe parish and the priest

Danish terms in this section

sogn
Parish: the territory served by one church, and the basic unit of Danish local life from this period until the nineteenth century.
sognepræst
Parish priest.
egenkirke
'Own-church' — a church built by a landowner on his own land, whose income and appointment he controlled. Most early Danish parish churches began this way.

By around 1250 Denmark had something in the order of two thousand parishes. In 1050 it had none.

A parish is a deceptively large idea. It means that every person in the kingdom belongs to a named territory with a named church and a named priest; that they are baptised, married and buried in one place; that they owe their tenth to that place; and that somebody, somewhere, knows they exist. It is the first system in Danish history that touches everybody.

Where did the parishes come from? Mostly, in the first instance, from landowners. A magnate with an estate built a church on it — of wood at first — appointed a priest, took the income, and in return got a burial place, prayers for his soul, and standing. This is the egenkirke, the proprietary church, and it was normal across northern Europe. The parish boundary was, very often, simply the boundary of somebody's property.

The whole subsequent history of the medieval Danish church is the slow prising of these buildings out of private hands and into the bishop's. That is a fight, and it is one of the reasons the crown and the church ended up on opposite sides in the twelfth century, because the king was the largest proprietor of churches in the kingdom.

As for the priest: he was, in this period, a local man. He was very often married, whatever Rome said — clerical celibacy was being pressed hard by the reform papacy and resisted successfully in Scandinavia for another century and more. He farmed. His Latin was, on the evidence of what survives, frequently minimal. He was closer in condition to a substantial farmer than to a scholar, and the distance between him and the bishop in Lund, who read canon law and corresponded with Rome, was greater than the distance between him and the people in his pews.

09 / NARRATIVETwo thousand churches, and the stones that stopped

Danish terms in this section

frådsten
Calcareous tufa — a soft, porous freshwater limestone that can be sawn. The first Danish building stone, quarried around Roskilde.
granitkvadre
Dressed granite ashlar blocks, the characteristic material of Jutland's churches. Cut from glacial erratics with a pointed hammer.
tegl
Brick. Arrives in Denmark in the second half of the 1100s, first in southern Jutland, and changes everything about what can be built.
runesten
Runestone. The Danish corpus runs to something over two hundred, peaks around 950–1050, and then stops.

Between roughly 1100 and 1250, more than two thousand stone churches were built in Denmark. Nearly all of them are still standing. If you have driven anywhere in the Danish countryside you have seen several without registering it: a small white-washed box with a lower, narrower chancel at the east end and a tower added four centuries later.

The number invites a wrong picture, so here is the corrective, and it comes from the standard Danish account rather than from me: two thousand churches over a hundred and fifty years is an average of about fifteen a year. In a country with several hundred thousand people, fifteen small buildings a year is not a national mobilisation. It is a steady, unspectacular, multi-generational habit — a couple of parishes in each diocese starting work in any given year, most of them taking a decade or two over it.

What the churches were built of depended entirely on what the ground offered, and this produces the clearest regional map in Danish material culture. Around Roskilde, where springs deposit calcareous tufa in the Elverdam valley, the early churches are frådsten — soft, yellowish, easily worked. In Jutland, where the glaciers left granite boulders everywhere, they are squared granite ashlar, and the labour of splitting and dressing those blocks is the reason Jutland's churches took so long. In the south-west, where there is neither, they imported volcanic tuff from the Rhineland by the shipload, which tells you the trade routes of chapter 9 were still running. And from the later 1100s, brick — cheap, uniform, and the material that will build the whole Danish Middle Ages after this page ends.

Vignette · the only date

Somewhere near Herning, in Jutland, in the year 1140, a mason is finishing a semicircular granite slab that will sit above the south door of the church at Gjellerup. He has cut a Latin inscription into it: in the 1140th year of the Lord's incarnation, this house was founded to the glory of God.

He is doing something nobody else in Denmark is recorded as having done. Of the two thousand medieval churches in the country, Gjellerup's is the only one that carries a date. Everything else we know about when these buildings went up comes from dendrochronology, from stylistic comparison, or from guesswork.

We do not know his name. We know a great deal about his hands: the same pointed hammer, the same confident lion, the same treatment of a doorway recurs from church to church across Jutland, and the workshops can be traced even though not one of the men in them can be. They were the most widely distributed artists Denmark has ever had, and they are all anonymous.

An unnamed stonemason · Gjellerup, near Herning · 1140

Two revisions to the schoolbook picture are worth flagging. The first is dating: dendrochronology has pulled a great many of these buildings earlier than the old estimates, putting churches like Gundsømagle near Roskilde around 1100 rather than a generation or two later. The second is the wall paintings. Ulla Haastrup's work established that the great Danish Romanesque frescoes were not, as had long been assumed, products of the Valdemar heyday after 1157, but were being painted before the middle of the 1100s — that is, during the civil wars, not after them. Denmark was producing painting of European quality while its kings were murdering each other.

And here is the thing nobody has explained. At almost exactly the moment the stone churches begin, the runestones stop.

The Danish runestone corpus peaks around 950–1050 and then simply ends. There is no decree, no prohibition, no recorded controversy. The obvious explanations are all partial: that Christian burial moved commemoration from the roadside into the churchyard; that the stones had done legal work about inheritance which written charters now did better; that elite display shifted from a stone by a road to a stone building with your family buried in it. Each of these is plausible and none is demonstrated. A tradition that had run for centuries and produced the single most characteristic Danish monument type went out, within about two generations, for reasons we do not know.

10 / NARRATIVEThe land fills up

Danish terms in this section

-torp / -rup / -trup / -strup
'Outlying settlement'. A village name ending in any of these marks a daughter settlement carved out of older land — the fingerprint of medieval colonisation.
-tved
'Clearing'. Another colonisation name, and a blunter one: it means somebody cut down a wood.
hjulplov
The wheeled, mouldboard plough — heavy, ox-drawn, and capable of turning the wet clay soils that the older scratch-plough could not.

Underneath the kings and the bishops, the thing actually happening in Denmark between 1050 and 1300 is that there are steadily more people, and they are spreading out.

The evidence is in the map of village names, and it is unusually direct. Denmark's oldest settlement names — the -lev, -inge and -sted names — sit on the best soils, the ones people took first. Ringed around them, on poorer and more marginal ground, sit hundreds of villages whose names end in -torp or its contractions -rup, -trup, -strup. The word means an outlying settlement, a daughter farm, a place founded out of somewhere older. Open a Danish road atlas: Kirkerup, Vindstrup, Ustrup, Hastrup, Torup. Every one of them is a piece of medieval land clearance.

Alongside them the -tved names say the same thing without metaphor: a clearing, somewhere a wood used to be. The pollen record agrees. Woodland cover falls; cereal pollen rises; grazing indicators spread. Denmark is being converted from a patchwork of settlement in forest into something much closer to the open agricultural landscape of today.

The technology behind it is the heavy wheeled plough with a mouldboard, which turns a furrow rather than scratching it and which can therefore work the heavy, wet, fertile clays of eastern Denmark that the older plough could not. That plough needs a team of oxen, which is more than most single households own, which pushes farming towards cooperation between neighbours, which is one of the roots of the tightly organised Danish village with its shared fields and its collective decisions. The agrarian reforms of the 1780s, in Part G, will find that arrangement still in place seven hundred years later, and dismantle it.

How many more people? The honest answer is that we are estimating from settlement counts and church counts rather than from anything resembling a census, but the general view is that the Danish population perhaps doubled between 1000 and 1300. That is the demographic engine under everything on this page: more farms means more tithe, more tithe means more churches, more people means more men for the levy, and a larger surplus means there is something for kings and bishops to fight over.

It also means Denmark is running towards a wall. A population that has doubled by filling in the marginal land has no more marginal land left. Chapter 15 arrives in 1349 with the plague, and finds a country that had already stopped growing.

Meanwhile in Europe

None of this is Danish either. The same centuries see the largest expansion of farmland in European history: forest cleared across Germany and France, fen and marsh drained in the Low Countries and eastern England, and a steady eastward movement of German-speaking settlers into Slav lands that historians call the Ostsiedlung. Denmark's -torp villages are the local expression of a continental phenomenon.

Carrying it, everywhere, are the monks. The Cistercians were founded at Cîteaux in 1098 and under Bernard of Clairvaux became the most effective land-clearing organisation in Europe, deliberately settling on marginal ground and making it productive. Archbishop Eskil brought them to Denmark in 1144 — to Herrevad in Skåne, the first Cistercian house in Scandinavia — in the middle of the civil war. Esrum, Sorø and Vitskøl follow.

And in 1147 a second front of the Second Crusade was directed not at Jerusalem but at the pagan Wends across the Baltic, with Danish forces taking part. It achieved little. It established a principle that the Valdemar age (13) will run on for eighty years: that campaigning eastward against the Baltic Slavs was not war but holy war, with everything that licensed.

11 / NARRATIVEHaraldsted, 7 January 1131

Danish terms in this section

Knud Lavard
'Lavard' is from Old English hlāford, 'lord'. Duke of Schleswig, and the third major Knud of this period.
hertug
Duke. In Schleswig the title is a border office: it means holding the frontier against, and dealing with, the Germans.
gilde
A guild — a sworn association of townsmen, with religious and mutual-aid functions. The Schleswig guild is about to take a hand in national politics.

Niels, the last of Svend Estridsen's five reigning sons, held the throne for thirty years from 1104. The reign is thinly recorded and looks, from outside, uneventful. Underneath it a problem was assembling itself, and the problem had a name: Knud Lavard.

Knud Lavard was Erik Ejegod's son — that is, the king's nephew — and Niels had made him duke of Schleswig, which meant putting him in charge of the southern frontier. He was extremely good at it. He pacified the border, suppressed the Wendish raiding that had made the coasts unsafe, and rebuilt Schleswig's trade. He was also, by the standards of the Danish court, alarmingly continental: raised partly in Saxony, at ease in German, married to a Kievan princess, and moving in the Empire's orbit.

In 1129 the Emperor Lothar invested him as prince of the Abodrites, the Slav people across the border. A Danish royal prince had taken a title from the German emperor. Whatever Knud intended by it, what Niels's son Magnus saw was a cousin with a foreign army behind him, a power base on the border, and a better claim to the throne.

On 7 January 1131, in the forest at Haraldsted near Ringsted, Magnus met Knud Lavard for a talk and killed him.

Everything on the rest of this page follows from that morning. A week later Knud Lavard's widow gave birth to a son and named him Valdemar, after her Kievan grandfather. Knud's half-brother Erik Emune raised a rebellion, and the country split.

The decisive battle came at Fodevig in Skåne on 4 June 1134. Erik Emune had spent his exile in Germany usefully: he brought back mounted, armoured knights fighting in the continental manner, and used them against a Danish army that fought on foot in the old way. Magnus was killed. So were five bishops, on Niels's side — a detail that says more about the position of the Danish church in 1134 than any charter does. Niels fled south to Schleswig, where the guild of the town, which had taken Knud Lavard as its patron, killed him three weeks later.

Erik Emune reigned for three years and was killed at an assembly by a man called Sorte Plov — Black Plough — who is said to have walked up to him in front of everybody and speared him. His successor Erik Lam abdicated to a monastery in 1146, which by the standards of this period counts as a happy ending.

12 / NARRATIVEGrathe Hede

From 1146 there were three claimants and no king. Svend Grathe, son of Erik Emune. Knud Magnussen, son of the Magnus who had killed Knud Lavard. And Valdemar, the posthumous son, now grown, holding Schleswig as his father had.

Eleven years of war followed, in which every combination of alliance was tried. The church, under Archbishop Eskil of Lund, took sides, changed sides, and went into exile; Eskil is the figure in whom the second half of this page's argument becomes visible, because his loyalty was not to any of the three but to the institution, and he was prepared to fight the crown for it. He brought the Cistercians to Denmark in 1144 while the war was on.

In the summer of 1157 the three men agreed to divide the kingdom: Svend took Skåne, Knud took Zealand, Valdemar took Jutland. They met at Roskilde in August to seal it with a feast.

What happened there is known in Danish as blodgildet i Roskilde, the blood-feast. Partway through the evening the candles went out and Svend's men came in with weapons. Knud Magnussen was killed on the spot. Valdemar, wounded in the thigh, got out of the building and out of the town, and rode for Jutland.

Eleven weeks later, on 23 October 1157, the two survivors met on Grathe Hede, a stretch of heath south of Viborg. Svend's army broke. He was caught in a bog trying to escape, recognised, and killed by peasants with an axe. Valdemar was twenty-six years old, and king of a whole country for the first time in twenty-six years.

It is tempting to end there, on the heath, because that is where Danish schoolbooks end this period and because the reign that follows is one of the great ones. But the more interesting thing to carry out of this page is quieter and has nothing to do with Grathe Hede.

In 1050 Denmark was a kingdom held together by a fleet, a levy, and the personal authority of whoever could command both. Everything depended on the king being alive and successful. By 1157 there was a second structure lying across the country — eight dioceses, a couple of thousand parishes, an archbishop answering to Rome, a permanent annual tax on grain, and a spreading pattern of stone buildings that would still be standing when everything else had gone. It had been built, mostly, in the decades when the monarchy was at its weakest, and it kept working straight through twenty-six years of civil war without noticing them very much.

That is the durable achievement of this century, and it was not really anybody's plan. Svend Estridsen wanted an archbishop to escape the Germans. Knud wanted revenue. Erik wanted a saint in the family. What they built between them was the first Danish institution capable of outliving the man who ran it — and the first one, as Eskil had already begun to demonstrate and as the quarrel with Jakob Erlandsen (14) will show at length, capable of telling a king no.

Myth-check

"Denmark became Christian in 965, when Harald Bluetooth put it on the Jelling stone."
Harald announced a decision. What this page describes — dioceses, parishes, priests, a tax, and two thousand buildings — is the thing that actually made Denmark a Christian country, and it took from roughly 1050 to 1250. Chapter 10's stone is the press release; this is the delivery, two hundred years late.
"Knud den Hellige was killed because he was too devout for a half-pagan people."
That is Ælnoth's framing, and Ælnoth was writing to make him a saint. The rising began over fines imposed on men who had been summoned to an invasion that never sailed, and over a king pressing his claims to wrecks, windfalls and the property of the dead harder than his predecessors. It was a tax revolt with a martyrdom at the end of it.
"The peasants built the village churches themselves, out of faith."
Most early parish churches were put up by landowners on their own land, as private churches whose income and whose priest the owner controlled. The parish paid for them afterwards, through the third of the tithe assigned to the fabric. Faith is in the story; so is property.
"The civil wars of 1131–57 were a straightforward fight over who was the rightful king."
Succession was the trigger, but two other things are running underneath. One is the southern border and how close a Danish prince should stand to the German emperor — the charge against Knud Lavard. The other is the church: this is exactly the period in which the tithe is being enforced and the reform papacy is pressing its claims, and an archbishop like Eskil had his own reasons to want a king who owed him something.
"Saxo tells us what happened."
Saxo wrote around 1200, for Absalon, at the court of Knud Lavard's son and grandson. For him the murder at Haraldsted is the founding crime of the age and Valdemar's victory is providence. He is magnificent and he is a house historian. The same caution applies to Adam of Bremen, who had an archdiocese to defend, and to Ælnoth, who had a saint to establish.

Summary

IF YOU REMEMBER FIVE THINGSThe page in five

  1. Svend Estridsen, from whom every later Danish monarch descends, had to fund a kingdom without foreign tribute for the first time. His answer was the church, and around 1060 Denmark was divided into eight dioceses whose boundaries are still visible on the map today.

  2. Knud den Hellige was hunted down and killed in a church at Odense on 10 July 1086, after fining the levy for going home from an invasion of England that never sailed. Fifteen years later his brother had him made a saint, and the dynasty acquired holiness.

  3. In 1103/04 Lund became the archbishopric for all Scandinavia and Denmark left Hamburg-Bremen. The price was the tithe — which cannot be shown to have been collected until the 1130s, and which took a permanent tenth of every farmer's grain.

  4. Two thousand stone churches went up between 1100 and 1250, about fifteen a year, built of whatever the local ground offered. In the same decades the runestone tradition stopped, for reasons nobody has established.

  5. The murder of Knud Lavard at Haraldsted in January 1131 opened twenty-six years of civil war, ending with the blood-feast at Roskilde and the battle of Grathe Hede in 1157. The church structure built during those decades carried on regardless — which is the point.

Questions & discussion

FOUR KINDSWork the material

Recall

Did the facts land?

  • Name the eight dioceses of c. 1060, and the ninth that lasted six years.
  • What did Knud den Hellige do in 1085, and what did he do afterwards that caused the rising?
  • What did Rome demand in exchange for the archbishopric at Lund, and when can it first be shown to have been paid?
  • How was the Danish tithe divided, and how did that differ from the rest of Europe?
  • What does a village name ending in -torp or -strup tell you about when it was founded?
  • Who killed Knud Lavard, where, and what was born a week later?

Causal

Why did that follow from this?

  • Why did the end of English tribute make a Danish king more likely to quarrel with his own farmers?
  • Why did the Investiture Contest in Germany help a small northern kingdom get its own archbishop?
  • Why did making Knud a saint strengthen the dynasty in a way that winning a battle could not?
  • Why does the heavy wheeled plough push Danish farmers towards collective village organisation rather than away from it?
  • Why were five bishops fighting and dying on the king's side at Fodevig in 1134, and what does that tell you about the Danish church at that date?

Counterfactual

Pull one thread and see what moves.

  • Suppose Herman's embassy of 1133 had failed and Lund had gone back under Hamburg-Bremen. What happens to Danish independence from the Empire over the next four centuries — and to Norway and Sweden, who were in the same province?
  • Suppose Knud den Hellige's fleet had sailed in 1085 and the invasion of England had gone ahead. Does Denmark get drawn permanently west again, and does any of the church-building on this page happen?
  • Suppose Magnus had not met Knud Lavard at Haraldsted. Does the Danish twelfth century simply continue as the peaceable sixty years that preceded it?

Contested

Where historians actually disagree.

  • Was the rising of 1086 a popular revolt of free farmers against royal exactions, or a magnates' coup using popular grievance as cover? The sources are hagiographic and the question has been argued in Danish scholarship for a century without settling.
  • Why did the runestones stop? Christian burial, written charters, and a shift of elite display into church-building are all offered. None is demonstrated, and the coincidence of timing with the stone churches may be causal or may be two effects of one cause.
  • Who actually built the parish churches — the proprietor, or the parish? The private-church model and the community model imply very different pictures of early Danish rural society, and the archaeology has been read both ways.
  • How much did the tithe really cost, and how much was actually collected? A nominal tenth and an enforced tenth are not the same thing, and Denmark's evidence for the twelfth century is thin enough that estimates of the real burden vary widely.
  • Are the civil wars of 1131–57 best read as a dynastic feud, as a proxy conflict over Denmark's relationship with the Empire, or as the Danish episode of the European church-versus-crown struggle? Each reading is defensible and each makes a different thing central.

  • Nationalmuseet The church survey, the Romanesque stone sculpture, and the dendrochronology that redated much of it.

Go and look

STILL THEREPlaces you can visit

  • Sankt Knuds Kirke, Odense The bones of Knud den Hellige and his brother Benedikt are in the crypt, on display, a few hundred metres from where they were killed in 1086. Very few European countries can show you the actual eleventh-century king.
  • Lunds domkyrka, Sweden The archbishop's church from 1103/04, and the largest Romanesque building in the North. The crypt is the part to see; it is essentially unaltered.
  • Gjellerup Kirke, near Herning A granite ashlar church with the only dated inscription on a Danish medieval church: 1140, cut over the now-blocked south door.
  • Sankt Jørgensbjerg & Sankt Ibs, Roskilde Denmark's oldest stone church remains, and a well-preserved frådsten church of about 1100 — the soft yellow tufa is unmistakable once you have seen it.
  • Ringsted and Haraldsted The wood where Knud Lavard was killed in 1131, and the church at Ringsted where his son buried him and had him canonised in 1170.
  • Grathe Hede, south of Viborg Heath, a memorial stone, and not much else. That is rather the point: the most consequential battlefield of the Danish twelfth century is an unremarkable stretch of Jutland.
