Christian 4.: the wars that broke him

1625 – 1648 · Ellen Marsvin · Kirsten Munk · Corfitz Ulfeldt · Vibeke Kruse

Sixty years of building are undone in twenty-three. Two foreign armies occupy Jutland; the first territory is lost; the Sound toll's principle is broken; and the family the king made outside his marriage takes over the state the week he dies.

Introduction

WHAT THIS PAGE ANSWERSFive questions

  1. Why did the king of Denmark join a German civil war his own council had refused to enter?
  2. Foreign armies held Jutland for two years and Denmark lost no territory at all. Why were the terms so mild?
  3. Who were the sons-in-law, and how did a morganatic marriage of 1615 end up running the country in 1648?
  4. What did Denmark actually give up at Brømsebro in 1645 — and which clause mattered most?
  5. He is the most popular king in Danish memory and he lost everything he attempted. How do both of those stay true?

01 / NARRATIVEWhy a Danish king went to Germany

Danish terms in this section

Den Nedersaksiske Kreds
The Lower Saxon Circle: one of the administrative districts of the Holy Roman Empire. Christian 4. belonged to it as duke of Holstein, and in 1625 it elected him its colonel — its military commander.
subsidier
Subsidies: cash promised by allies to pay another prince's army. England and the Dutch Republic promised them. Very little arrived.

By 1625 the war that began in Bohemia in 1618 had worked north. The Protestant cause in Germany had been beaten in the field, and the emperor's armies were operating far beyond anything the princes of the north had expected. Two men had reason to intervene: Christian 4. of Denmark and Gustav Adolf of Sweden. Both offered themselves. The German princes chose Christian, because his terms were cheaper and because Sweden frightened them more.

He did not go in as king of Denmark. He went in as duke of Holstein, elected colonel of the Lower Saxon Circle — the same device chapter 22 described him using in 1611, and the council of the realm again declined to commit Denmark to the war. Its reasoning was the reasoning it had given for thirty years: the realm's income could pay for the realm's defence and could not pay for the ambitions of a German prince.

What he was counting on was other people's money. England and the Dutch Republic promised subsidies. Only a fraction ever came, and it came late. What he actually had was his own credit, the Sound toll, the revenues of Holstein, and an army of mercenaries hired on the expectation of payments that did not arrive.

It is worth being clear about the motive, because Danish accounts have sometimes made it grander than it was. Religion was real: he was a Lutheran prince and the Lutheran north was being overrun. But he was also trying to secure the north German bishoprics for his sons, to keep Sweden out of a theatre Sweden wanted, and to establish himself as the leading Protestant prince of northern Europe. The first of those was dynastic, the second strategic, the third personal, and all three had to be paid for.

02 / NARRATIVELutter am Barenberge, 27 August 1626

Danish terms in this section

hvervet hær
A hired army. Christian 4.'s German force was mercenary almost throughout, which made it expensive, mobile, and prone to evaporating when not paid.

The campaign of 1625 went nowhere; he fell from his horse at Hameln and was out of action for weeks, which did not help. In 1626 the imperial commander Johann Tserclaes, Count of Tilly, brought him to battle at Lutter am Barenberge in Brunswick on 27 August.

It is worth saying what the army was, because the answer explains the defeat and the twenty years after it. Perhaps a fifth of it was Danish or Norwegian, raised by levy. The rest was hired: German regiments, and Scottish and English ones recruited on the promise of English subsidies that had not arrived. It had been in the field for a year without a general engagement, it had been paid irregularly, and it was commanded by a king who had never fought a pitched battle. Tilly's force was roughly the same size and had been fighting continuously since 1618.

Christian was withdrawing north when Tilly caught him and made him stand. The Danish infantry held the first attacks and then gave way; the cavalry left the field; the guns and the whole baggage train were taken. Estimates of the loss vary and the low ones are as unreliable as the high, but somewhere between a third and a half of the army was killed or captured in an afternoon, and the artillery — which a state buys once in a generation — was gone entirely. Christian's horse was shot under him and he got away in the confusion.

What he had left was not an army in any usable sense. Mercenaries who survive a defeat can be re-engaged, but only for money, and the money was the thing that had not been arriving before the battle. Denmark had lost, in one afternoon, both the force it had and the means of replacing it.

Lutter is the hinge of the reign and it takes one paragraph, which is roughly the right proportion. A single afternoon in Lower Saxony converted a solvent, ambitious, thirty-year-old monarchy into a state with no field army, an occupied peninsula ahead of it, and debts it would still be servicing in 1648. Nothing Christian 4. did afterwards was done from strength.

03 / NARRATIVEThe occupation, 1627–29

Danish terms in this section

kontribution
Contribution: the systematic levy an occupying army imposed on a district, in cash and in kind, as an alternative to plundering it. In practice districts got both.
ødegård
A deserted farm — the same word chapter 17 used for the fifteenth century. The occupation put it back into the tax records in quantity.
Kejserkrigen
'The Emperor's War': the Danish name for 1625–29, which locates the enemy correctly and the responsibility vaguely.

In the autumn of 1627 Tilly's and Wallenstein's armies came north over the Elbe and into Jutland. They met almost nothing. There was no Danish field army, the fortresses were few, and the peninsula was open from the Kongeå to Skagen within weeks. They stayed until the spring of 1629.

What an army of that period did to a district it occupied was not incidental to warfare, it was the warfare: soldiers were quartered on farms and fed by them, contributions were assessed on every parish and collected under threat of burning, and what was not levied was taken. Add the movement of troops through a countryside with no sanitation, and add plague. Jutland's population fell, farms went out of cultivation, and cattle — the export chapter 21 followed down the ox road — were driven off in numbers that broke the trade for a generation. Tax assessments in the 1630s show districts that could not pay anything at all.

Two things did not fall. Glückstadt, the town chapter 22 said had failed at its commercial purpose, turned out to be a very good fortress and held out through a long siege — the one foundation of his that repaid its cost in the way he had least expected. And the islands were never touched, because the fleet held the water. Zealand, Funen and Scania went through the whole war unoccupied. That is the strategic fact of Danish history in a sentence: a country of islands with a navy is very hard to conquer, and its mainland is very easy to overrun.

The division mattered politically as well as militarily. Jutland's nobles and peasants paid for a war the island nobility had voted against and did not experience. When the reckoning came in the 1640s and again in 1660, the resentment was still there.

04 / NARRATIVEThe Peace of Lübeck, 1629

Danish terms in this section

Freden i Lübeck
The Peace of Lübeck, May 1629. Denmark gave up nothing and got everything back, on one condition: no further intervention in the Empire.

By any reading of the military situation Denmark should have been dismembered. Instead the terms of May 1629 restored every occupied district, took no territory, imposed no indemnity, and required only that Christian 4. abandon the German bishoprics and stay out of imperial affairs.

The reason is that Wallenstein had a project. He wanted an imperial Baltic fleet and a Baltic policy, and for that he wanted Denmark as a client rather than a corpse — a defeated Denmark that kept its navy and owed him its survival was worth more than a partitioned one that handed the Sound to Sweden or to Poland. The emperor, for his part, had a war in the south to finish and no appetite for a long amphibious campaign against islands.

So Denmark came out of it with its map unchanged and its position destroyed. Gustav Adolf landed in Germany the following year and became the Protestant champion Christian had wanted to be. The subsidies, the alliances and the standing all went to Stockholm. Denmark's twenty-three remaining years under Christian 4. are conducted in the shadow of a Sweden that is now the stronger state — and everyone in Copenhagen knew it.

Meanwhile in Europe

The war Denmark left in 1629 ran for another nineteen years. Gustav Adolf landed in Pomerania in 1630, won at Breitenfeld in 1631 and died at Lützen in 1632; Wallenstein was murdered by his own officers in 1634. France entered openly in 1635 and turned it into a Habsburg–Bourbon war, which is what it stayed until the settlements of 1648. By then a third of the German population in some regions was gone. Denmark's two years of occupation, terrible as they were locally, are a small entry in that ledger — which is worth knowing, because Danish accounts sometimes write 1627–29 as though it were the worst thing that happened to anyone.

05 / NARRATIVEKirsten Munk, Ellen Marsvin, and the sons-in-law

Danish terms in this section

ægteskab til venstre hånd
Morganatic marriage — literally 'marriage to the left hand'. A legal marriage in which the wife does not become queen and the children have no claim to the throne.
svigersønnepartiet
The party of the sons-in-law: the group of great noblemen who married Christian 4.'s daughters by Kirsten Munk and effectively governed Denmark from 1648 to 1651.
rigshofmester
Steward of the realm, the office Peder Oxe held in chapter 21. Corfitz Ulfeldt held it from 1643, and he had married the king's daughter.

In December 1615 the widowed king married Kirsten Munk, aged eighteen, of a wealthy but untitled noble family. Her mother had negotiated the terms, and the terms are the interesting part: not a mistress but a wife, married legally if morganatically, with estates settled in her own name. Ellen Marsvin did not intend her daughter to be disposable.

They had twelve children. Then, on campaign in Germany in 1627 or 1628, Kirsten began an affair with one of her husband's own commanders, the Rhinegrave Otto Ludwig. In November 1628 she shut her bedroom door to the king; when a daughter was born the following September he refused to acknowledge her. In 1630 he repudiated the marriage, and Kirsten spent the next eighteen years under house arrest on her Jutland estates.

Vignette · Ellen Marsvin, 1629

She is fifty-seven, twice widowed, the greatest landowner on Funen and one of the richest people in Denmark of either sex. She has run Dalum since 1620. She buys estates, sells them, builds them up, and is respected by the king specifically as a businesswoman, which in 1629 is not a compliment anyone was obliged to pay a woman.

Her daughter's position is collapsing and her grandchildren's prospects with it. What she does about it is the thing every account of her has to stop at: she introduces the king to a new mistress. Vibeke Kruse had been Kirsten's own chambermaid and was then in Ellen Marsvin's household. From 1629 she was the king's, and remained so for nineteen years.

Historians have read this two ways and the sources do not settle it. Either she was buying her daughter's disgrace down — a king with a mistress of his own has less standing to complain about a wife with a lover — or she was protecting her own access to a man who had made her family and could unmake it. Both are consistent with everything else she did. What is certain is that she raised the royal grandchildren herself, fought for years to have the unacknowledged daughter recognised, failed, and kept the family's estates intact through all of it.

Ellen Marsvin, of Dalum and Rosenvold · 1629 · born 1572, died 1649 · mother of Kirsten Munk, grandmother of Leonora Christina

The consequence is not domestic, it is constitutional. Five of the daughters married into the greatest noble houses in Denmark — Ulfeldt, Sehested, Lindenov, Pentz, Rantzau — and those marriages made a faction. Corfitz Ulfeldt, who married Leonora Christina in 1636, was steward of the realm from 1643. When the king died these men were the government, and they spent three years running it in their own interest before the reaction came. A morganatic marriage contracted in 1615 to keep a king's children out of the succession put the king's sons-in-law in charge of the state instead.

06 / NARRATIVEBuilding on a raised toll

Danish terms in this section

Rundetårn
The Round Tower, 1637–42: an observatory on top of a church on top of a university library, with a spiral ramp instead of a stair. Tycho Brahe's instruments were meant for it.
Nyboder
The rows of yellow terraced houses built from 1631 for the navy's crews and their families — Denmark's first purpose-built workers' housing.
møntforringelse
Debasement: reducing the silver in the coin so that the same bullion pays more bills. It works once, and it works against you afterwards.

He did not stop building. Nyboder from 1631, the Round Tower and Trinitatis church from 1637, work continuing at Rosenborg and on the fortifications. What changed is how it was paid for.

The Sound toll went up, repeatedly and steeply. Rates were raised, exemptions were narrowed, and the crown began treating the strait less as a customs post than as a source of whatever sum was needed. This is the point at which the toll stops being a charge that foreign merchants grumbled about and becomes a grievance that foreign governments were prepared to act on — the Dutch above all, who carried most of what passed and by the 1640s had both a fleet and a motive.

Beyond the toll he debased the coinage, borrowed from his mother while she lived, borrowed from Hamburg, mortgaged crown land, and ran up personal debts that were increasingly hard to distinguish from the realm's. The council watched this with an alarm that reads clearly in the record and could do very little about it, because the toll was still not theirs to control.

The Round Tower is a fair emblem of the decade. It is a genuinely original building, it served the university for two centuries, and it went up while the state that paid for it was sliding toward insolvency and a war it could not fight. Both halves are true, and neither is the whole story.

07 / NARRATIVETorstensson's war, 1643–45

Danish terms in this section

Torstenssonkrigen
The Torstensson War, 1643–45, named for the Swedish commander who began it. The third of the eleven Danish–Swedish wars and the first Denmark loses territory in.
Kolberger Heide
The naval action off Kiel on 1 July 1644 in which Christian 4. was wounded on his own quarterdeck. Tactically indecisive; symbolically the single most reproduced scene in Danish history.

In December 1643 a Swedish army under Lennart Torstensson turned north out of Germany without declaring war and marched into Jutland. Within weeks the peninsula was occupied for the second time in sixteen years, by a different enemy, for the same reason: there was nothing there to stop anyone.

The Swedish calculation was cold and correct. Denmark's toll policy had made enemies of the Dutch, who joined in. Sweden's own war in Germany was going well enough to release an army. And Denmark had spent fifteen years unable to rebuild what Lutter destroyed. Torstensson's orders were not to conquer Denmark but to take enough of it to dictate terms — which is exactly what happened.

At sea Denmark did better, and this is where the story everyone knows sits.

Vignette · Kolberger Heide, 1 July 1644

He is sixty-seven. The Danish and Swedish fleets meet in the bay off Kiel and fight most of the day at close range. Christian 4. is on the deck of the Trefoldigheden when a Swedish shot strikes a gun beside him and throws splinters and metal across the quarterdeck. He is knocked down with wounds to his face and body, and he loses the sight of his right eye.

He got up and stayed on deck, and the fleet kept fighting. That is the part that is true, and it is attested by people who were there. He kept the splinters afterwards and had them set into earrings for Vibeke Kruse, which is either the most human detail in the reign or the most vain, and possibly both.

The battle settled nothing. What settled the naval war was three months later off Femern, where a combined Swedish and Dutch fleet destroyed the Danish fleet almost entirely — the worst defeat in Danish naval history to that date, and the reason there was no answer to Torstensson on land. The song schoolchildren sing about the man at the high mast was written by Johannes Ewald in 1779, a hundred and thirty-five years later, and it puts Christian 4. in the same verse as two admirals from later wars. It is a fine poem and it is not evidence.

Christian 4., aged 67 · aboard Trefoldigheden, Kolberger Heide off Kiel · 1 July 1644 · the fleet was destroyed at Femern three months later

08 / NARRATIVEHannibal Sehested's Norway

Danish terms in this section

statholder
Governor of Norway, the office chapter 21 followed from 1572. Under Hannibal Sehested from 1642 it becomes something much closer to a government.
Hannibalfejden
'Hannibal's feud': the Norwegian campaign of 1644–45, fought by Norwegian troops under Norwegian command against Swedish territory. The only part of the war Denmark-Norway was not losing.
udskrivning
Conscription. Christian 4. had raised a peasant militia in Denmark in 1614; it was Sehested who made the Norwegian version actually work.

One son-in-law was doing something other than manoeuvring. Hannibal Sehested married Christiane, one of Kirsten Munk's daughters, in 1642 and was made governor of Norway the same year, aged thirty-three. What he did there over the following nine years is the most competent administration anybody performed in this reign, and it is largely absent from Danish accounts because it happened in the other kingdom.

He took a province that paid for itself badly and made it pay for itself well: he reorganised the revenue, took the crown's Norwegian income out of the hands of Danish fief-holders who were keeping most of it, and — the decisive step — built a Norwegian military establishment funded from Norwegian taxes, with Norwegian officers and conscripted Norwegian farmers in the ranks.

When Torstensson came north in 1643 that establishment was the only usable land force Denmark-Norway had. Sehested took it across the border into Swedish Jämtland and Värmland, held the Norwegian frontier, and conducted the one campaign of the war that went Denmark's way. It is called Hannibal's feud, and Norwegians have generally thought better of it than Danes.

It did him no good and did Norway a great deal. At Brømsebro the two provinces given up were Jämtland and Härjedalen, both Norwegian, both taken from a kingdom that had just successfully defended itself — traded away in a settlement negotiated in Copenhagen to protect Danish interests elsewhere. And in 1651, in the reaction against the sons-in-law, Sehested was accused of enriching himself, stripped of his offices and estates, and dismissed. The Norwegian institutions he built outlasted him, and by 1660 Norway was the part of the realm that could still raise soldiers and money. Chapter 24 depends on that.

09 / NARRATIVEBrømsebro, 13 August 1645

Danish terms in this section

Brømsebrofreden
The Peace of Brømsebro, 13 August 1645. The first treaty in which Denmark cedes territory to Sweden, and the treaty that breaches the Sound toll.
pantsat i 30 år
Pledged for thirty years: Halland's status under the treaty, as security for the rest. Denmark never got it back.

The terms, negotiated with French mediation and signed at a bridge on the Blekinge border: Denmark ceded Jämtland and Härjedalen from Norway, the islands of Gotland and Ösel outright, and pledged Halland to Sweden for thirty years. Sweden's ships were exempted from the Sound toll.

Every item on that list is a first. It is the first Danish cession to Sweden in the whole of the eleven wars; chapter 21's map of 1600 is the last on which the realm is undiminished. Gotland had been Danish since the fifteenth century and Ösel since 1559 — both appear on that map. Jämtland and Härjedalen had been Norwegian for six hundred years. And Halland, "pledged for thirty years", was still Swedish when the thirty years expired, because by then the treaty of 1658 had made the question moot.

The clause that mattered most was the smallest. Exempting Swedish ships from the toll conceded that the Sound was not simply Denmark's to charge for — that the right could be negotiated away under pressure. The toll survived until 1857, but from 1645 it was a privilege other states could demand a share of rather than a fact of geography. The fiscal foundation chapter 21 traced from 1429 through Peder Oxe's reform of 1567 was not destroyed here. It was made negotiable, which turned out to be enough.

10 / NARRATIVE1648

Danish terms in this section

håndfæstningen 1648
Frederik 3.'s accession charter: the most restrictive in Danish history, and the last. Twelve years later the whole arrangement is abolished.
valgrige
Elective kingdom — the constitutional fact underneath all of this. There was no automatic succession, so every new reign began with the council naming its price.

He spent his last three years trying to hold the pieces together and failing at it in public. The council was openly hostile, the sons-in-law were manoeuvring, his own designated heir had died in 1647 and the succession was unsettled. He died at Rosenborg on 28 February 1648, aged seventy, having ruled for fifty-two years.

What the council did next tells you what it thought of the reign. It kept the throne empty for months while it negotiated, and the charter it finally required of his son Frederik 3. was the hardest any Danish king had signed: no war without consent, no alliances, no appointments of consequence, no new taxes, and a council entitled to hold the king to account. The lesson the nobility drew from Christian 4. was that a king with independent money is a danger, and it wrote that lesson into the constitution as thoroughly as it knew how.

It was the last accession charter in Danish history. Twelve years later, after a war that went far worse than anything in this chapter, the whole structure was swept away in a fortnight and replaced by an absolutism more complete than anywhere else in Europe. Chapters 24 and 25 are that story. The council's caution was vindicated by events and destroyed by them at the same time.

Meanwhile in the world

The year Christian 4. died is one of the busiest in European history. The Peace of Westphalia ends the Thirty Years' War in October 1648 and settles the shape of Germany for a hundred and fifty years, with Sweden as a guarantor power holding territory on the Baltic coast. England is in the middle of a civil war that will execute its king in January 1649 — the first European monarch put on trial by his own subjects, eleven years before Denmark's nobility hands its king absolute power. The Dutch Republic wins formal independence from Spain in the same settlement and enters its richest decades. And in 1644 the Ming dynasty falls in China, which nobody in Copenhagen noticed and which moved more people than all of it.

Vignette · Vibeke Kruse, Copenhagen, spring 1648

She had been Kirsten Munk's chambermaid, then Ellen Marsvin's servant, then for nineteen years the king's companion and the mother of two of his children. She had no title and no family behind her that counted. Everything she had came from one man, and on 28 February 1648 he died.

What happened next took weeks. Corfitz Ulfeldt and Kirsten Munk — the discarded wife and the son-in-law who was steward of the realm — had her estates sealed, turned her and her daughter out of the palace, and demanded she be tried. The council refused: whatever it thought of her, an extrajudicial proceeding against a dead king's mistress was not something it would put its name to. She was ill by then. She died before the end of April, and was buried without ceremony by people who made sure it stayed that way.

Her son Ulrik Christian Gyldenløve became one of the defenders of Copenhagen in 1659 and died at twenty-eight. Her daughter died at twenty-one. There is a good deal of Danish history in the fact that we know the exact dates of everything Christian 4. built and cannot establish the day his companion of nineteen years died.

Myth-check

"Kong Christian stod ved højen mast" — the song is about Kolberger Heide.
Partly. Johannes Ewald wrote it in 1779 for a play, a hundred and thirty-five years after the battle, and its verses move from Christian 4. to Niels Juel to Tordenskjold — three different men in three different wars. The king was wounded on his quarterdeck and did stay on deck; that much is contemporary. The rest is a late eighteenth-century poem about Danish naval glory, written when there was not much of it left to point at.
"Denmark was crushed in 1629."
Denmark was occupied and then handed everything back. The Peace of Lübeck cost no territory and no money, because Wallenstein wanted a surviving Danish client with a fleet rather than a carcass that would hand the Sound to somebody worse. The loss in 1629 was of standing, allies and the leadership of Protestant Germany — all of which went to Sweden, and all of which cost Denmark more over the next thirty years than an indemnity would have.
"He lost the wars but at least he built the country."
The building is real and mostly belongs to chapter 22, before the wars. What this chapter adds is that the later building was paid for by raising the Sound toll to a level that helped turn the Dutch into Sweden's allies in 1643. The Round Tower and the war are not separate stories with separate budgets.
"Kirsten Munk was disgraced and disappeared."
She spent eighteen years under house arrest and then, within weeks of the king's death, was back in Copenhagen with her son-in-law running the realm, having her marriage and all her children declared legitimate and pursuing her successor through the courts. Her daughters had married the five most powerful men in Denmark. The disgrace was real; the powerlessness was not.
"Halland was pledged for thirty years, so Denmark expected to get it back."
Denmark did expect that, and the expectation is why the clause was accepted. But a pledge is only as good as the ability to enforce redemption, and by 1675 the question had been settled twice over by the treaty of 1658 and the war that followed it. The thirty-year pledge is a good example of a treaty term that looks temporary in the document and is permanent in practice.

Summary

IF YOU REMEMBER FIVE THINGSThe page in five

  1. Christian 4. entered the German war in 1625 as duke of Holstein, not as king, because the council refused to commit Denmark. He was counting on English and Dutch subsidies that largely never came.

  2. Tilly destroyed his army at Lutter am Barenberge on 27 August 1626. Imperial forces then occupied Jutland from 1627 to 1629, living off contributions and plunder; the islands, protected by the fleet, were never touched.

  3. The Peace of Lübeck in 1629 took no territory, because Wallenstein wanted Denmark alive as a naval client. What Denmark lost was its standing, and Gustav Adolf inherited it.

  4. Kirsten Munk's five daughters married the greatest noblemen in Denmark. Corfitz Ulfeldt, married to Leonora Christina, was steward of the realm from 1643, and the sons-in-law governed from 1648 to 1651.

  5. Brømsebro, 13 August 1645: Jämtland, Härjedalen, Gotland and Ösel ceded, Halland pledged for thirty years and never returned, and Swedish shipping exempted from the Sound toll — the first Danish cession to Sweden, and the first breach in the toll.

Questions & discussion

FOUR KINDSWork the material

Recall

Did the facts land?

  • In what capacity did Christian 4. enter the German war, and why did that matter?
  • What happened at Lutter am Barenberge, and what did it leave Denmark without?
  • Why were the terms of the Peace of Lübeck so mild?
  • Name the five things Denmark gave up at Brømsebro.
  • What did the accession charter of 1648 require, and what was unusual about it in hindsight?

Causal

Why did that follow from this?

  • Why was Jutland occupied twice in sixteen years while the islands were never taken?
  • How did raising the Sound toll in the 1630s help produce a Dutch fleet fighting alongside Sweden in 1644?
  • Why did a morganatic marriage designed to keep children out of the succession end up putting their husbands in charge of the state?
  • Why did the council make the charter of 1648 harder than any before it, and why did that make 1660 more likely rather than less?

Counterfactual

What if it had gone otherwise?

  • If Christian 4. had stayed out of Germany in 1625, does Sweden still become the dominant Baltic power?
  • If Wallenstein had partitioned Denmark in 1629 instead of restoring it, who ends up holding the Sound?
  • If the fleet had not been destroyed at Femern, what could Denmark have refused at Brømsebro?

Contested

Where the argument still is

  • Whether the German intervention was a reasonable gamble or an obvious mistake. Contemporaries were divided, the council said no throughout, and hindsight makes it look more foolish than the information available in 1625 warranted.
  • How much damage the occupation of 1627–29 actually did. The tax records show catastrophe; how much of the 1630s decline was the war and how much was the wider European depression is not settled.
  • What Ellen Marsvin was doing in 1629. Protecting her daughter, or her own position, or both. The sources allow all three readings.
  • Whether Christian 4.'s reputation survives on the buildings alone. Danish opinion has swung twice, and it is worth asking how the reign would read if nothing he built had stood.

  • — Steffen Heiberg, on Christian 4. Again the standard biography, and the source of the now-conventional split verdict between the builder and the commander.
  • — Peter H. Wilson, on the Thirty Years' War Where this chapter says the Danish intervention was a small entry in a much larger ledger, that is the comparative position it is reporting.

Go and look

STILL THEREPlaces you can visit

  • Rosenborg, Copenhagen He died here on 28 February 1648, in a room you can stand in. The blood-stained clothes he wore at Kolberger Heide are in the collection.
  • Rundetårn, Copenhagen Walk the ramp. It was built for horses and carts to carry instruments up, and it is the reason there are no stairs.
  • Nyboder, Copenhagen The oldest surviving row on Sankt Paals Gade dates from the 1630s. Still housing, still yellow.
  • Glückstadt, Schleswig-Holstein The hexagonal town plan of 1617 is intact and the fortifications that held out against Tilly are traceable on the ground. An hour from Hamburg — the city it was built to ruin.
  • Boller and Rosenvold, near Horsens Kirsten Munk's estates, where she spent eighteen years under house arrest. Boller is a private care home and Rosenvold private; both are visible from the road and the landscape is the point.
  • Brömsebro, on the Blekinge–Småland border A stone marks the site of the 1645 negotiations on the old frontier. It is a quiet spot for a place where Denmark stopped growing.
  • Trefoldigheden's guns, Tøjhusmuseet The Danish War Museum on Slotsholmen, in Christian 4.'s own arsenal building. The building is as much the exhibit as the contents.
