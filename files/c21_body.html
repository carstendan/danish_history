The Lutheran realm of the nobility

1536 – 1588 · Christian 3. · Peder Palladius · Peder Oxe · Frederik 2. · Daniel Rantzau

The richest crown in Danish history is handed to a king who cannot raise a tax, make a law or declare a war without the consent of two hundred and fifty families. For a hundred and twenty-four years the two facts live in the same building. This is the half of that period in which the arrangement worked.

Introduction

WHAT THIS PAGE ANSWERSFive questions

  1. The crown came out of 1536 with half the land of Denmark. Why did that not make the king powerful?
  2. Denmark's Reformation killed nobody for heresy. Why did the same Reformation, run by the same king, behead a bishop and his two sons?
  3. A phrase from 1460 said the duchies would never be divided. They were divided in 1544. How did both things stay true?
  4. What made a Danish nobleman rich in the sixteenth century — and who paid for it?
  5. Denmark and Sweden fought for seven years and settled nothing. Was there anything to settle?

01 / NARRATIVEWhat the winner owed

Danish terms in this section

håndfæstning
The accession charter a Danish king signed before his election took effect. The one of 30 October 1536 is the constitution of the next hundred and twenty-four years.
rigsrådet
The council of the realm. From 1536 it is entirely noble and roughly twenty to thirty men, and it is the body the king cannot govern around.
len
A fief: a district of crown land and crown rights, granted to a nobleman to administer. The man holding it is a lensmand. Almost all crown land was held this way.

Christian 3. won his civil war on credit. The army that took Aalborg and won at Øksnebjerg was Johan Rantzau's, raised in Holstein and paid in silver the king did not have; the fleet that closed the Sound was partly Swedish, lent by Gustav Vasa, who wanted Lübeck humbled as much as anyone. When Copenhagen surrendered in July 1536 the new king of Denmark owed money to German mercenary captains, to Holstein nobles, to Prussian and Swedish allies, and to his own creditors in Kiel, on a scale that no Danish crown revenue could have serviced.

This is the first thing to understand about the confiscation of the church. It was not a windfall the crown could sit on. Much of it was spoken for before it was taken, and a good deal of it went straight out again as security for loans, as payment in land to men who had fought, and as the pensions that persuaded the last abbots and canons to go quietly. The crown's share of Danish land went from roughly a sixth to roughly a half in a single autumn. Its freedom of action went up by considerably less.

The second thing is what the nobility charged for its help. The accession charter of 30 October 1536 — signed the same day as the recess that abolished the bishops, by the same assembly — is the most restrictive in Danish history and the longest-lived. The council of the realm was to be noble and only noble, filling its own vacancies by nomination to the king. The king could not declare war, make peace, raise a general tax or issue a law binding on the realm without its consent. Nobles were confirmed in their exemption from taxation and in their jurisdiction over their own tenants. And crown fiefs — the len through which every acre of the newly enlarged royal estate was actually administered — were reserved to noblemen.

That last clause is the quiet one, and it is the reason the arithmetic of 1536 misleads. The crown did not farm its half of Denmark. It granted it out in roughly two hundred len to the same families that sat in the council, and for most of the century the commonest form was the tjenestelen or free fief, in which the holder rendered military service and kept the surplus. A king who owned half the country and drew a fixed rent from most of it was, in cash terms, not obviously better off than a king who owned a sixth and drew all of it. What he had acquired was not income so much as patronage: two hundred appointments with which to reward, bind and occupy the class that had just written his charter.

Danish historians call the arrangement the adelsvælde, the rule of the nobility, and date it 1536 to 1660. It is easy to read the phrase as a description of weakness, of a crown held down. It is better read as a partnership with an unstable settlement inside it. The king had the land, the Sound and, before long, a fleet. The council had the constitution. Neither could do without the other, and neither ever agreed where the line ran. Everything in this chapter and the two after it follows from that.

02 / NARRATIVEThe church the crown built

Danish terms in this section

Kirkeordinansen
The Church Ordinance: the constitution of the new Danish church, in Latin in 1537 and in Danish in 1539. It governs worship, schools, poor relief and the appointment of clergy.
superintendent
The office that replaced the bishop — deliberately given a Latin administrative name rather than the old one. Within a generation everyone was saying biskop again anyway.
visitats
The formal visitation of a parish by its superintendent: inspection of the church, examination of the priest, and a sermon to the congregation about what it was doing wrong.
sognepræst
Parish priest. After 1536 he could marry, and was expected to preach in Danish and to keep the parish's accounts.

A church cannot be abolished into nothing; something has to say Mass on Sunday. Christian 3. sent to Wittenberg for a specialist, and got one: Johannes Bugenhagen, Luther's own parish priest, who had already written church orders for Brunswick, Hamburg and Lübeck. He arrived in the summer of 1537 and stayed two years.

What he did in his first fortnight is a fair summary of the whole settlement. On 12 August 1537 — one year to the day after the bishops were arrested — he crowned Christian 3. and Queen Dorothea in the Church of Our Lady in Copenhagen. Bugenhagen was not a bishop. He was a married priest with a doctorate, and the anointing of a Danish king by such a man was a deliberate statement that the succession of consecrated bishops had been cut and would not be repaired. Immediately afterwards he ordained seven superintendenter for the seven Danish dioceses. Then, on 2 September, the university in Copenhagen reopened under a new charter, its purpose to train Danish clergy so that the crown would not have to keep importing Germans.

The Church Ordinance itself was issued in Latin the same year and in Danish in 1539. It is a practical document rather than a doctrinal one. Services in Danish. Preaching every Sunday. A poor box in every church, funded from what had been the altar guilds, with the parish responsible for its own indigent. A school in every market town. Clergy appointed by the congregation's call and the superintendent's examination, confirmed by the crown. And in 1550 the crown published the first complete Danish Bible, some three thousand copies, of which every parish church was ordered to buy one — a genuinely enormous piece of state publishing, and for many villages the most expensive object they had acquired in living memory.

The man who had to make this real on the ground was Peder Palladius, superintendent of Zealand from 1537 until his death in 1560, a Ribe weaver's son who had taken a doctorate at Wittenberg under Luther. Zealand had something near four hundred parishes and he visited essentially all of them, some repeatedly. He wrote down what he said. The Visitatsbog — his standard visitation address, worked up over years of delivery — is the single best source we have for what the Reformation looked like from a pew, and it is nothing like a theological treatise. It is a man in a cold building telling several hundred farmers, in the plainest Danish imaginable, to mend the roof, stop holding drinking parties over the dead, keep the churchyard fenced so that pigs cannot get at the graves, pay the priest what they owe him, and leave the old images alone if they are not being worshipped, because knocking things down is not the same as believing something.

Vignette · Peder Palladius, a Zealand village church, about 1543

He is about forty. He has ridden in that morning, and he will ride on tomorrow; there are four hundred of these. The church is small, whitewashed over its paintings or not yet whitewashed — he does not much mind which — and the parish is standing, because there are no pews for them.

He goes through it in order, and the order is not what a modern reader expects. The roof. The windows. Whether the priest can be understood. Whether the parish clerk knows his letters. The poor box, and the fact that it is the parish's own money for the parish's own poor and not the king's. Whether anyone has been carrying candles to a spring in the wood, which they have. Whether the women are being charged a fee to be churched after childbirth, which they are, and are not to be. Whether the young people are being taught the catechism, which they are not.

Then he tells them a story, because he always tells them a story, and it is usually funny and frequently rude, and then he tells them that the saints will not help them and that the priest cannot save them and that the only thing standing between them and God is God. And then he asks for their horses to be brought round.

Meanwhile in Europe

Denmark settles its religion early and then stops arguing about it, which almost nobody else manages. The Schmalkaldic League is crushed at Mühlberg in 1547 and the emperor's victory is undone within five years. The Peace of Augsburg in 1555 concedes the principle Denmark had already acted on — that the ruler's religion is the country's — and concedes it only for Lutherans, which guarantees a further century of war. England changes its religion four times between 1534 and 1559. France begins thirty-six years of civil war in 1562. The Council of Trent sits, on and off, from 1545 to 1563. Against all of that, a Danish parish in 1560 had had one change of religion, twenty-four years earlier, and had kept its priest, its building and most of its calendar.

03 / NARRATIVEThe edges: Norway, Iceland, the Faroes

Danish terms in this section

statholder
Governor: the crown's standing representative in Norway, an office regularised in 1572. Norway had no council after 1536 and no separate government — it had a man at Akershus.
lagting
The Norwegian regional assembly and court, which survived 1536 untouched, along with the Norwegian law it applied.
sýslumaður
Icelandic: the district officer who held royal authority locally. The Reformation in Iceland was carried out by these men, not by Danes.

The recess of 1536 said that Norway should no longer be a kingdom but a member of Denmark, like Jutland or Funen, and that it should have no council. This is the sentence Norwegian historians have quoted more than any other, and chapter 20 argued that its importance lies less in what it did than in how long it sat on the books. What it did immediately was to remove one institution and one man. The institution was the Norwegian council; the man was Olav Engelbrektsson, archbishop of Nidaros and the last person in Norway with the standing to organise resistance. He had backed the wrong side in the Count's Feud, and in April 1537 he loaded what he could carry from the cathedral treasury into a ship at Steinvikholm and sailed for the Netherlands, where he died within the year. The shrine of St Olav went to the mint.

What did not happen is as important. Norway kept its own law, descended from Magnus the Lawmender's code of the 1270s and not replaced until 1604. It kept its lagting and its local courts. It kept a peasantry that mostly owned its own land, which the Danish peasantry did not, and which is one reason the two countries' social histories diverge so sharply afterwards. The len in Norway went to Danish noblemen, and the church and the administration worked in Danish, and both facts mattered enormously over four centuries. But nobody in 1537 set out to abolish Norway, and nobody succeeded in doing so.

Iceland is where the settlement showed its teeth. The Church Ordinance was sent there in 1538 and rejected. Ögmundur Pálsson, the old and blind bishop of Skálholt, was seized in 1541 by a royal force and died aboard ship on the way to Denmark. His see went Lutheran. The northern see of Hólar did not, because its bishop was Jón Arason, a farmer's son from Eyjafjörður who had been bishop since the early 1520s, who had imported Iceland's first printing press, who was reckoned the best poet of his generation, who was openly the father of at least six children, and who was — as an unfriendly Catholic encyclopedia put it three and a half centuries later — rather more of a war-chief than a bishop.

In 1548 his Lutheran counterpart died and Jón moved south. He and his sons Ari and Björn seized the new Lutheran bishop Marteinn Einarsson in 1549, occupied Skálholt, and for a season effectively ruled the island. The king declared them outlaws. In the autumn of 1550 they were ambushed at a parley by Daði Guðmundsson, a chieftain who had his own reasons, and handed to the king's bailiff at Skálholt. The bailiff, Christian Skriver, faced a practical problem: the ships had gone for the winter, the prisoners could not be sent to Denmark until spring, and Jón Arason's supporters were still at large in the north. On 7 November 1550, without trial and on the authority of the outlawry alone, Ari and Björn were beheaded and then their father. The tradition in Iceland is that the executioner made a poor job of it.

Vignette · Þórunn Jónsdóttir, Hólar, spring 1551

She is her father's daughter in the way that mattered in Iceland: she holds land, she holds people's debts, and when she sends word north it travels. Her father and two of her brothers have been dead since November and are buried at Skálholt, in the ground of the see they had taken and lost, four days' hard travel across the interior from home.

Two things happen that spring. The bodies are brought north to Hólar, over the mountains, and reburied in the cathedral her father had rebuilt. And at Kirkjuból in the west, Christian Skriver — the king's bailiff, the man who had signed for the axe — is killed along with his household by a party of fishermen. Nobody was ever punished for it in a way the sources record. The story that Þórunn Jónsdóttir paid them is a story: it is what Icelanders said, and it is not the same as evidence, and it has been repeated for four hundred and seventy years because it fits.

What is not in doubt is that a Danish punitive fleet arrived in 1551, that resistance was finished, and that Iceland would not have a Catholic bishop again until the twentieth century. The Danish Reformation executed nobody for heresy inside Denmark. It executed three men for it in Iceland, on a technicality, because the weather made a trial inconvenient.

Þórunn Jónsdóttir, daughter of Jón Arason of Hólar · northern Iceland · 1551 · the translation of the bodies is recorded; her hand in the killing at Kirkjuból is tradition

The Faroes went Lutheran in 1540 with no resistance recorded and one lasting consequence: Danish became the language of church, school and law, and Faroese survived for three centuries as a spoken tongue with almost nothing written in it. Greenland was, as chapter 17 described, no longer inhabited by anyone the crown could reach — the Norse settlements had gone silent in the previous century — but the claim was not renounced, and it appears on the map at the head of this part as a claim and nothing more. It will be worth something in 1721, and a great deal in 1933.

04 / NARRATIVEThree brothers, one duchy

Danish terms in this section

ridderskabet
The knighthood: the noble estate of Schleswig and Holstein, which functioned as a single body across both duchies and was the real author of the settlement of 1544.
fællesregering
Joint government: the condominium by which the king and the dukes ruled the duchies together in everything the division had not separated.
afdelte hertuger
'Partitioned-off dukes': princes given a title and an income but no share of government, because the estates refused them homage. Sønderborg from 1564 is the first, and it matters in 1863.

Chapter 19 gave you the sentence from Ribe in 1460 — that Schleswig and Holstein should remain up ewig ungedeelt, forever undivided — and argued that its nineteenth-century career had very little to do with what it originally meant. Here is the proof, and it comes eighty-four years later from a Danish king.

Christian 3. had two half-brothers, Hans and Adolf, sons of Frederik 1. by his second marriage. They came of age, they had renounced any claim to the Danish throne, and they wanted their inheritance. The duchies were the family's patrimony and there was no legal way to refuse them a share. But the knighthood of Schleswig and Holstein, which had written the 1460 clause in the first place and had been living off it for a century, refused absolutely to see the duchies cut into pieces — because a divided Schleswig-Holstein would be two sets of courts, two nobilities, and two lots of privileges to defend instead of one.

What they agreed on in 1544 is one of the strangest constitutional arrangements in European history, and it works because it takes the word divided literally. The territory was not divided. The revenue was. The districts and estates of both duchies were valued and sorted into three heaps of approximately equal yield, and one heap was assigned to each brother — with the result that a brother's income came from parcels scattered the length of both duchies, interleaved with his brothers' parcels, sometimes village by village. Adolf, as the youngest, was given first choice, in the manner of children dividing a cake; he took the heap containing Gottorp castle, and his line is called Holstein-Gottorp ever after. Hans took Haderslev. Christian, as king, took the royal share.

Government of everything the money had not divided — the courts, the defence, the relations with the Empire — remained joint. The three of them, and their heirs after them, ruled the duchies as a condominium, styling themselves identically as duke of Schleswig, Holstein, Ditmarschen and Stormarn. The knighthood got exactly what it wanted: a settlement so entangled that separation became a practical impossibility. It was not finally untangled until 1773, and by then it had produced a rival dynasty on Denmark's southern border, married into half of northern Europe and eventually onto the Russian throne, which will cost Denmark two wars and most of a century of diplomacy.

One piece of unfinished business was settled by the same three men. Ditmarschen, the peasant republic on the North Sea marsh that had destroyed a Danish royal army at Hemmingstedt in 1500 and taken the Dannebrog with it, was invaded in June 1559 by some twenty thousand men under Johan Rantzau — then sixty-seven years old, and the same commander who had won the Count's Feud. This time the dykes were dry, the season was chosen, the artillery train was adequate, and the campaign took about a month. Ditmarschen kept its local law and lost its independence, and was divided, like everything else in the duchies, into three shares. Rantzau died six years later; the standard he recovered is a different question, and chapter 13 has already said what can and cannot be said about that flag.

05 / NARRATIVEThe land, the lord and the grain

Danish terms in this section

fæstebonde
A tenant farmer, holding his farm on a lifetime lease from crown, church or noble in return for rent and services. The great majority of Danish farmers.
selvejer
A self-owning farmer. Perhaps a fifth of Danish farms around 1500 and a small fraction by 1600 — the century in which the class very nearly disappeared.
hovedgård
A manor farm: the lord's own home farm, worked with tenant labour rather than let out. Building one up is the central economic project of the sixteenth-century Danish nobleman.
hoveri
Labour service owed by a tenant to the manor — days of ploughing, carting and harvest. Undefined in law, and rising throughout this period.
vornedskab
From chapter 19: the rule, on Zealand, Lolland, Falster and Møn but not in Jutland, that a peasant's son could not leave the estate of his birth without leave.

Roughly four Danes in five lived on a farm, and this section is about them, because the sixteenth century did more to their lives than the Reformation did.

Two things were happening at once across northern Europe. Population was rising after two centuries of plague-depressed numbers, and the price of grain was rising faster — the long inflation that historians call the price revolution, driven by population, by silver from Spanish America, and by the appetite of a rapidly urbanising Netherlands for bread it could not grow. Amsterdam called the Baltic grain trade its mother trade, and it meant it. For anyone in Denmark who could put grain on a ship, the century was a long, gentle, unearned bonanza.

The people who could put grain on a ship were the people who could accumulate a surplus, and in Denmark that meant the crown and the nobility, not the farmer. So the Danish nobleman of this period does something recognisable in Poland, Brandenburg and Livonia at the same moment and for the same reason. He builds up a hovedgård: he consolidates his scattered holdings by exchange, buys out neighbours, and where he can, buys or pressures the self-owning farmers of his district into tenancy. He merges tenant farms — a documented and much-resented practice — to enlarge his own fields. And because a demesne needs to be worked and cash wages are expensive, he takes the labour from the tenants he already has, in the form of hoveri.

Hoveri is the hinge, and its danger was that nobody had ever written down how much of it there could be. A rent is a number; a duty to work is a relationship. Through the sixteenth century the number of days rose, the seasons at which they were demanded became the seasons the tenant most needed for his own crop, and the tenant's ability to argue fell — because on the islands vornedskab meant he could not simply leave, and because after 1536 his lord was increasingly also his judge. The nobility's jurisdiction over its own tenants, confirmed in the charter of 1536, is not an antiquarian detail. It is the reason the arrangement held.

Cattle did the same work in the other direction. Jutland could not compete in grain but could raise and fatten oxen, and chapter 18 followed the drove roads south from Viborg and Kolding to the Elbe crossings and the Dutch markets. In the second half of the sixteenth century something in the region of forty to fifty thousand head a year went down that road. The trade required capital, grazing and an export licence, and the crown restricted the licences to noblemen — so that the one genuinely dynamic Danish export of the century was, by law, a noble monopoly. When Danish historians argue about whether the adelsvælde was a political arrangement or an economic one, this is the exhibit.

What did not happen is worth stating, because it is what makes Denmark unlike England. There was no enclosure. The Danish village kept its open fields, its strips scattered so that good and bad land were shared, and its collective decisions about when to plough and when to graze, until the 1780s. A Danish peasant in 1588 was poorer, more heavily worked and less free than his great-grandfather. He was not, however, cleared away. He was too useful where he stood.

06 / NARRATIVEPeder Oxe and the price of a passing ship

Danish terms in this section

Øresundstolden
The Sound Dues, levied at Helsingør since 1429 — see chapter 18. Until the 1560s a fixed charge per ship; afterwards a percentage of the cargo.
rigshofmester
Steward of the realm: the senior office of the Danish state, roughly a chief minister. Peder Oxe held it from 1567 to his death in 1575.
regnskabslen
An accounting fief: one whose holder must render accounts and pass on the surplus, as against a tjenestelen where he keeps it. Shifting fiefs from the second kind to the first is how a Danish crown got richer.

Peder Oxe was born in 1520 into one of the great families, sent abroad at twelve, and spent seventeen years at universities in Germany, the Netherlands, France, Italy and Switzerland. He came home to find both parents dead and eleven younger siblings to provide for, and provided for them with a competence that his contemporaries found slightly indecent, largely by buying church land cheap in the years after 1536. He was on the council at thirty-two. In 1557 he quarrelled with Frederik 2. over an exchange of estates, and in 1558 he left the country.

What he did next was close to treason and possibly was treason. In Lorraine and in Germany he attached himself to the adventurer Wilhelm von Grumbach and to the cause of Christina of Denmark, Christian 2.'s surviving daughter, whose claim to the Danish throne was better than Frederik 2.'s if you accepted certain premises. He was, in short, working to have his king deposed. Then Denmark went to war with Sweden, the war went badly, the treasury emptied, the currency was debased, and in 1566 Frederik 2. swallowed everything and asked him to come home. His estates were restored. In 1567 he was made steward of the realm.

The reform he is remembered for is a change in how one number was calculated. Since Erik of Pomerania the Sound toll had been a fixed sum per ship: a rose noble, then various adjusted rates, levied on the vessel regardless of what was in it. This was administratively simple and economically absurd, since it taxed a ship of salt herring and a ship of Muscovy furs identically, and since the general rise in the volume and value of Baltic trade passed the crown by entirely. From 1567 the toll was levied instead on the value of the cargo, at a rate in the order of one to two per cent.

Which raises the obvious problem. The value of a cargo is whatever its skipper says it is, and a skipper standing on the quay at Helsingør has every reason to say a low number. The Danish answer to this is the detail worth carrying away from the whole chapter. The crown reserved to itself the right to buy the cargo outright, at the price the skipper had just declared. Understate, and you might lose your goods for a fraction of what they were worth; overstate, and you paid the toll on a figure you had invented. The skipper was invited to tax himself honestly by being made to bid against the possibility of being taken at his word.

The results were large and are frequently overstated. Traffic through the Sound was rising anyway; the number of ships recorded passing Helsingør roughly doubled across the second half of the century. Tying the crown's income to the value rather than the count of them multiplied the toll several times over within a decade, and the Sound became for the first time the single largest source of cash the Danish state possessed. Statements that it produced a third, or a half, or two-thirds of royal revenue are all in print and none of them should be quoted without a shrug: the crown's income arrived partly in cash and largely in kind, through fiefs, and there was no consolidated budget in which the shares could have been compared. What can be said without qualification is that this was cash, it was foreign, it required no one's consent, and it arrived whether the harvest failed or not.

Oxe did a great deal else in eight years — recoined the currency, converted free fiefs into accounting fiefs so that surpluses came to the crown, put the crown forests and the royal ox-farms on a commercial footing — and by his death in 1575 the state was solvent and the king was rich. Of the clerks at Helsingør, more incidentally: they wrote down, for every ship, the master's name, his home port, where he had come from, where he was going, what he carried and what he paid. They did it from 1497 until 1857. What was designed as a receipt book is now the longest continuous record of European seaborne trade in existence, and economic historians have been mining it for a century.

07 / NARRATIVEThe Northern Seven Years' War, 1563–70

Danish terms in this section

Den Nordiske Syvårskrig
The Northern Seven Years' War, 1563–70: Denmark, Lübeck and Poland against Sweden. The first of the eleven Danish–Swedish wars.
hvervede tropper
Hired troops. Both sides fought this war almost entirely with mercenaries, which is why it cost what it did and why it was conducted the way it was.
brandskatning
Fire-tax: the levy an army extracted from a district under threat of burning it. The standard method of paying troops in the field.

The ostensible cause was heraldry. Both kings quartered three crowns in their arms; Frederik 2. took Erik 14.'s use of them as a claim on Denmark, and Erik took Frederik's as a claim on Sweden, and both were right. Underneath it were two real questions. The first was whether the Kalmar Union was dead in fact as well as in Sweden's practice, which Denmark had not conceded in forty years. The second was Livonia, where the Teutonic Order had just dissolved itself and the eastern Baltic was being partitioned in real time between Sweden, Poland, Russia and Denmark: Reval went to Sweden in 1561, and Denmark bought the bishopric of Ösel in 1559 for Frederik's brother Magnus. Whoever held the Livonian ports held the Russia trade.

The war that followed is a study in how much destruction two states can do to each other while achieving nothing whatsoever. At sea the fighting was heavy and Denmark had the better of it, although the most famous single event was an accident: the Swedish flagship Mars, the largest warship in the Baltic, was boarded off Öland on 31 May 1564 and blew up with several hundred Danes and Lübeckers aboard her. In June 1565 the Danish admiral Herluf Trolle was mortally wounded in action off Mecklenburg. He and his wife Birgitte Gøye had, in the same year, converted their estate of Skovkloster into a free school for noble boys, which she then ran alone for nine years after his death and which still exists as Herlufsholm — a reminder that a sixteenth-century Danish noblewoman with her own property could be a founder in her own right, and that Birgitte Gøye had earlier fought a public church case to get out of a betrothal contracted for her as a child, and won it.

On land the war was mostly a matter of mercenary armies burning each other's provinces. Älvsborg, Sweden's only Atlantic port, fell to Denmark in 1563 and was ransomed back in 1571 for an enormous sum. Halland, Skåne, Blekinge, Västergötland and Småland were systematically plundered by both sides. The one campaign historians still admire was Daniel Rantzau's: in the winter of 1567–68 he took a Danish army into Östergötland, deep inside Sweden, lived off the country through the winter, and — the achievement — brought it out again. It was militarily brilliant, and it changed the war not at all. Rantzau was killed by a chance shot besieging Varberg in 1569.

Erik 14. lost his mind and then his throne, deposed by his brothers in 1568. Denmark's finances collapsed, which is why Peder Oxe was pardoned. The Peace of Stettin, 13 December 1570, was mediated by the Emperor and settled the heraldry by leaving it to arbitration, which never happened; each king went on quartering three crowns. Sweden renounced claims to Skåne, Halland, Blekinge and Gotland. Denmark renounced its claim to Sweden — in the sense that it said the words, while continuing to argue that the union was merely dormant.

Nothing was resolved because there was nothing that could be resolved by a war of this kind. Denmark held both shores of the Sound, the Norwegian coast and the islands, which meant it could close Sweden's access to the west whenever it chose. Sweden, once it had Livonia and its own Baltic fleet, had an interest in prising that grip open which no treaty could satisfy. Chapter 19 promised you eleven wars between these two states. This is the first, and it establishes the pattern: a Danish structural advantage, a Swedish structural grievance, an inconclusive peace, and a shorter interval than anyone expects before the next one.

Meanwhile in the world

The years Denmark spent fighting Sweden over a coat of arms are the years the Atlantic economy is being built. Silver from Potosí and Zacatecas is arriving in Seville in quantities that will reprice everything in Europe, including the Danish grain the nobility is exporting. The Dutch revolt against Spain from 1568 and, in doing so, begin the century in which they will buy most of what passes through the Sound and carry most of the rest. A Habsburg fleet stops the Ottomans at Lepanto in 1571; an English one meets the Armada in 1588, the year this chapter ends. And in the east, Ivan 4. spends twenty-five years fighting the Livonian War for the ports that gave Denmark and Sweden their real quarrel, and loses it.

08 / NARRATIVEKronborg, Hven, and what the toll built

Danish terms in this section

Krogen

A grant of crown land and its revenues, free of accounting — the form in which Hven was given to Tycho Brahe in 1576, along with the labour obligations of the islanders.

From 1574 to 1585 Frederik 2. rebuilt the old fortress at Helsingør into Kronborg, and it is the most legible building in Danish history: a toll booth designed to be looked at from the deck of a ship that has just paid. Dutch and Flemish architects, Hans van Paeschen and then Antonis van Obbergen, faced the medieval walls in Scanian sandstone, put a copper roof and a ring of spires on it, and set it where every vessel entering or leaving the Baltic had to pass within easy sight and easier gun-range. It cost a fortune. It was paid for out of the thing it stood over.

It is worth being precise about what it did and did not do. Kronborg's guns could reach a ship in the channel, and the fortress on the Scanian side at Helsingborg reached from the other, and between them they made non-payment risky for a merchantman. They could not have stopped a determined battle-fleet, and in 1658 they did not. The toll worked because it was cheaper to pay than to fight, and because Denmark held both shores. Kronborg is the visible sign of a system that actually rested on geography and on the fleet at Bremerholm.

The same money paid for something less obviously governmental. In 1576 Frederik granted the island of Hven in the middle of the Sound to Tycho Brahe, a twenty-nine-year-old Scanian nobleman who had refused a career in law for astronomy and had made himself famous four years earlier by demonstrating that the new star in Cassiopeia was further away than the moon — which, in a universe where everything above the moon was supposed to be unchanging, was a bomb. On Hven he built Uraniborg, and beneath it Stjerneborg, and produced over twenty years the most accurate body of positional astronomy made before the telescope. Kepler used it to find the ellipse. It is not too much to say that the Danish Sound toll paid for the observational basis of modern planetary theory.

It was also, from the islanders' point of view, a fief. Hven's forty-odd farms owed labour to their lord like anyone else's, and their lord was building continuously for two decades. There were complaints, and later a royal commission, and a documented case in which Tycho imprisoned a tenant and his family. Section 05 is not a separate subject from this one.

Vignette · Sophie Brahe, Herrevad Abbey, 8 December 1573

She is fourteen, or seventeen; the sources disagree about the year she was born and nobody has settled it. She is the youngest of ten children of a councillor of the realm, and the brother standing beside her in the cold is seventeen years older and has become, in the last thirteen months, the most talked-about natural philosopher in Europe.

They are timing a lunar eclipse. This is not Uraniborg — Uraniborg does not exist yet, and will not for three years; this is the old Cistercian house in Scania where Tycho has set up instruments. The work is exact and tedious and consists of reading angles and writing numbers with cold hands, and she is doing it.

What she went on to is documented and unusual: she learned chemistry and medicine, she made remedies that were sought after, she managed an estate alone from the age of twenty-nine, she created one of the celebrated gardens of the age, and between about 1600 and 1626 she compiled a nine-hundred-page manuscript on the genealogies of sixty Danish noble families that scholars still use. What she contributed to the astronomy is the honest gap. Tycho employed many assistants and credited few; he wrote admiringly of his sister's animus invictus, her unconquered mind, and also said he had trained her in horticulture and chemistry and had told her not to take up astronomy. She took it up. Beyond that the record does not let us go, and claims that she was a co-discoverer go past the evidence in the direction we would prefer.

Sophie Brahe, later Thott, later Lange · Herrevad Abbey, Scania · 8 December 1573 · died at Helsingør in 1643, aged about eighty-four

09 / NARRATIVEWhat Christian 4. inherited

Danish terms in this section

formynderregeringen
The regency government of 1588–96: four councillors of the realm ruling for a boy king. The adelsvælde in its purest form, and its most competent.
herremand
A lord: the ordinary word for a member of the landed nobility, as the people on his land used it.

Frederik 2. died on 4 April 1588, at fifty-three, of a life lived hard. His heir Christian was ten. For eight years Denmark was governed by four members of the council — Niels Kaas, Peder Munk, Jørgen Rosenkrantz and Christoffer Valkendorff — and it is the most flattering possible advertisement for the arrangement of 1536. They kept the peace, avoided every foreign entanglement on offer, enforced the toll, tightened the fiefs, and handed the treasury over fuller than they found it.

Take the measure of the country they handed over. Under a million people, most of them on farms, perhaps four-fifths of those farms held on tenancy. Around two hundred and fifty noble families — well under one per cent of the population — holding something in the region of two-fifths of the land, exempt from taxation, monopolising the council, the fiefs and the export licences, and possessing legal jurisdiction over the people who worked their fields. A crown holding about half the land, drawing a foreign cash income from the Sound that nobody had voted for, maintaining a standing navy of some thirty warships at Bremerholm, and controlling a church whose superintendents it appointed and whose pulpits read out its proclamations. Add the duchies, held in an incomprehensible condominium with cousins; Norway, a kingdom on the books and a province in the paperwork; Iceland and the Faroes; and a claim on Greenland that nobody had tested.

Two of those sentences describe an unusually powerful monarchy and the other describes an unusually powerful aristocracy, and both are accurate. That is the condition of Denmark in the sixteenth century, and it was survivable for as long as nobody made a serious demand on it. The regents made none. The wars of 1563–70 had been paid for and forgotten. The system had produced, in fifty years, a Lutheran church, a solvent treasury, a rebuilt fortress, the best observatory in the world and a peasantry noticeably less free than it had been — and no crisis at all.

Christian 4. was crowned on 29 August 1596, having signed an accession charter that conceded everything his predecessors had conceded. The coronation was the most expensive Denmark had ever staged: weeks of feasting, foreign princes, tournaments, fireworks over Copenhagen. The money for it came out of the surplus the four old men had spent eight years accumulating. It is not a bad image to end a chapter on. Everything careful about the Danish sixteenth century was in that treasury, and everything unrestrained about the seventeenth was in the nineteen-year-old spending it.

Myth-check

"The king owned half of Denmark, so the king was rich."
He was rich, but not for that reason, and not straightforwardly. Crown land was administered through some two hundred fiefs held by noblemen, and for most of the century the commonest kind was the free fief, where the holder rendered service and kept the surplus. Converting fiefs to accounting fiefs — Peder Oxe's unglamorous work of the 1560s and 70s — did more for royal income than the confiscation had. The crown's real cash came from the Sound, which no Dane paid and no council had voted.
"Up ewig ungedeelt meant the duchies were never divided."
They were divided in 1544, eighty-four years after the phrase was written, by a Danish king, with the consent of the same noble estate that had written it. The trick was that the revenues were divided and the territory was not — three interleaved shares under one joint government. The estates got what they had always wanted out of the sentence, which was not unity for its own sake but a single set of privileges to defend. The slogan's national career belongs to the 1840s and is Part H's business.
"Denmark's Reformation was carried through without killing anyone."
Inside Denmark that is very nearly true and it is genuinely remarkable. In Iceland the same king's Reformation beheaded a bishop and two of his sons without trial in 1550, using an outlawry decree as the legal basis and the shipping season as the reason for haste. A settlement can be bloodless at the centre and not at the edge, and which of those you report is a choice.
"The Sound Dues were two-thirds of Danish state revenue."
You will find a third, a half and two-thirds asserted, for various dates, by serious people. None of them should be repeated flatly. The crown's income came partly in cash and largely in kind through the fiefs, there was no consolidated account in which the two could be compared, and the proportion swung wildly with harvests, wars and toll rates. That the Sound was the largest single source of cash is safe. Precise shares are not.
"Tycho Brahe was driven out of Denmark by a philistine king."
He left in 1597, after Christian 4. came of age and withdrew revenues and fiefs. But the file also contains years of complaints from his tenants on Hven about labour services, a royal commission, his refusal of obligations attached to a chapel he held, and a general reluctance to be administered. He was treated as a nobleman holding fiefs, which is what he was, and he behaved like one. The story is smaller and more ordinary than the legend, and the legend was largely built in the nineteenth century.

Summary

IF YOU REMEMBER FIVE THINGSThe page in five

  1. The charter of 30 October 1536 gave the nobility a monopoly of the council, of crown fiefs and of tax exemption, and gave the king no power to legislate, tax or make war without consent. The crown's new half of Denmark was administered by the same families that had written the charter, so land did not convert straightforwardly into power.

  2. Bugenhagen crowned Christian 3. and ordained seven superintendents in August 1537; the Church Ordinance, the reopened university and the Danish Bible of 1550 built a state church within fifteen years. Peder Palladius visited some four hundred Zealand parishes and left the best record we have of what the change looked like from the floor of a village church.

  3. Norway kept its law, its courts and its self-owning peasantry despite the 1536 clause; Iceland did not go quietly, and Jón Arason of Hólar was beheaded with his sons Ari and Björn at Skálholt on 7 November 1550, without trial, because winter had closed the shipping.

  4. The duchies were partitioned in 1544 by dividing revenues rather than territory into three interleaved shares — Adolf choosing first and taking Gottorp — which satisfied the 1460 clause in the letter, created a rival dynasty, and could not be untangled until 1773. Ditmarschen was conquered in 1559 and split the same way.

  5. Peder Oxe, pardoned in 1566 after intriguing abroad against his own king, converted the Sound toll in 1567 from a charge per ship to a percentage of declared cargo value, backed by the crown's right to buy the cargo at the declared price. That, plus the reform of the fiefs, made the treasury Frederik 2. spent on Kronborg and Tycho Brahe and the regents handed to Christian 4. in 1596.

Questions & discussion

FOUR KINDSWork the material

Recall

Did the facts land?

  • Name four restrictions the charter of 1536 placed on the king.
  • Who was Bugenhagen, and what did he do on 12 August 1537?
  • What was a visitats, and what did Palladius talk about during one?
  • How were the duchies divided in 1544, and who chose first?
  • What changed about the Sound toll in 1567, and what stopped skippers from lying?
  • Who governed Denmark between 1588 and 1596?

Causal

Why did that follow from this?

  • Why did owning half of Denmark not make the crown proportionally richer?
  • Why did the rise in European grain prices make Danish peasants less free rather than more prosperous?
  • Why did the knighthood of Schleswig and Holstein prefer an unworkable division to a clean one?
  • Why was there no execution for heresy in Denmark and three in Iceland?
  • Why did the Northern Seven Years' War end without settling anything — and what would have had to change for a war between these two states to settle something?

Counterfactual

What if it had gone otherwise?

  • If Christian 3. had refused his half-brothers a share in 1544 and kept the duchies whole, what happens to the Gottorp problem, and to 1658?
  • If the toll had stayed a flat charge per ship, what does Denmark not build in the seventeenth century — and does it still fight the wars?
  • If Danish villages had been enclosed in the sixteenth century, as English ones were, what does the Danish nineteenth century look like?

Contested

Where the argument still is

  • Was the adelsvælde a constitutional settlement that happened to enrich the nobility, or an economic arrangement dressed as a constitution? Danish historians have answered both ways, and the answer tends to follow the historian's view of 1660.
  • How much did hoveri actually rise? The evidence is estate accounts and complaints, both of which are produced by interested parties, and the picture varies enormously between estates.
  • What share of state revenue the Sound provided, and when. Estimates differ by a factor of two and there is no budget to check them against.
  • Whether the Danish Reformation had reached ordinary belief by 1588, or had only changed the furniture and the language. Palladius plainly thought the second, and he was in a position to know — but complaint is his genre.
  • What Sophie Brahe contributed. The sources credit assistants sparingly and her brother barely at all; both the minimising and the maximising accounts go beyond what can be shown.

Go and look

STILL THEREPlaces you can visit

  • Kronborg, Helsingør Forty minutes from Copenhagen. Look at it from the water if you possibly can: it was designed to be seen by someone who has just paid. The casemates and the great hall are the parts worth the time.
  • Ven, in the Sound Swedish since 1658, reachable by ferry from Landskrona in summer. Uraniborg's foundations and its reconstructed renaissance garden, Stjerneborg's underground chambers, and a small museum. The whole island is walkable in a day.
  • Hesselagergård, east Funen Built from 1538 by the chancellor Johan Friis: the earliest Renaissance manor in Denmark and, with its gun-loops and curved gables, a precise picture of a nobleman in the decade after the Count's Feud. Privately owned; visible from the road.
  • Herlufsholm, Næstved Herluf Trolle and Birgitte Gøye's school of 1565, in the buildings of a Benedictine house dissolved after 1536, still a school. Their tombs are in the church.
  • Schloss Gottorf, Schleswig Adolf's first choice in 1544, and the seat of the dynasty that grew out of it. Now the state museums of Schleswig-Holstein, with the Nydam boat from chapter 6 in the same complex.
  • Hærvejen at Immervad, south Jutland A stone bridge on the ox road, on a stretch you can walk. Forty thousand animals a year went south along this line to pay for the manor houses.
  • Hólar and Skálholt, Iceland Jón Arason's cathedral seat and the place he was executed, four hours apart by road. At Hólar the sixteenth-century Icelandic church still stands over the ground his children brought him back to.
