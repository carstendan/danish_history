Losing the eastern provinces

1645 – 1660 · Frederik 3. · Corfitz Ulfeldt · Dina Vinhofvers · Jens Kofoed · Leonora Christina

In thirty-two months Denmark declares a war, loses a third of its territory, is invaded again, and comes within one February night of ceasing to exist. What survives is a country that will never again be a great power, and a nobility that has just proved it cannot defend the realm it governs.

  2. How does an army walk across the sea, and why did nobody stop it?
  3. Skåne had been Danish for seven hundred years. How was it lost in an afternoon's negotiation?

01 / NARRATIVEFrederik 3. and the hardest charter

Danish terms in this section

håndfæstningen 1648
The accession charter chapter 23 ended on: the most restrictive in Danish history and the last one ever signed.
rigsrådet
The council of the realm, still entirely noble, still filling its own vacancies, and by 1648 governing more than the king did.

Frederik 3. was thirty-nine, the second son, raised for a German bishopric rather than a throne, and not expected to have one until his elder brother died in 1647. He was reserved, bookish, a serious collector of manuscripts and natural curiosities, and much harder to read than his father — which most people in Copenhagen took for weakness and which turned out not to be.

He signed what he was given. The charter of 1648 bound him more tightly than any Danish king had been bound, and for the first three years of the reign the men who actually governed were his half-sisters' husbands: Corfitz Ulfeldt as steward of the realm, Hannibal Sehested in Norway, and the rest of the connection chapter 23's figure laid out. A king who had been handed a country with no money, a lost war behind it and a faction in charge of the treasury did not have many moves.

02 / NARRATIVEThe fall of Corfitz Ulfeldt

Danish terms in this section

Dina-sagen
The Dina affair, 1650–51: an accusation of a plot to poison the king, which destroyed one career, ended one life, and sent the steward of the realm over to the enemy.
landsforræderi
Treason against the realm. Ulfeldt was convicted of it in his absence in 1663 and executed in effigy, which is the only way it was ever going to happen.

In 1650 a woman came to the authorities in Copenhagen and said that the steward of the realm and his wife were planning to poison the king. What followed took eighteen months, ruined everyone it touched, and is still not fully explicable.

Vignette · Dina Vinhofvers, Copenhagen, July 1651

She was thirty, born in Copenhagen to German parents, and she ironed silk for a living in the household of a silk manufacturer named Samson Gertzen, where she lived with her mother and her daughter. Her mother worked at the workhouse. People in the city also called her a prostitute, which may or may not have been true and was certainly useful to say about her later.

She said she had overheard Corfitz Ulfeldt and Leonora Christina discussing how to poison Frederik 3., and that Ulfeldt was the father of the child she was expecting. Her lover, an officer named Jørgen Walter, was in the background of all of it, and Walter did well out of the affair while it lasted. The king kept the accusation quiet and looked into it. Then Dina went to Leonora Christina and told her that Walter was planning to kill them, and Ulfeldt took that to the king, and the whole thing came out into the open.

Under questioning she withdrew the first accusation and said Walter had invented it. The court cleared Ulfeldt. It then sentenced Dina to death for having made the accusation in the first place, and she was beheaded outside Copenhagen Castle in July 1651 — the sources give the seventh and the eleventh. Ulfeldt and his family had slipped out of the city a few days before the execution and did not come back. Whatever she was — an instrument of Walter's, a woman who overreached, or someone who had genuinely heard something — she is the only person in the affair who paid with her life.

Dina Vinhofvers, silk-ironer · outside Copenhagen Castle · July 1651 · born 1620; the subject of a tragedy by Oehlenschläger in 1842

Ulfeldt went to the Netherlands, then to Sweden, and put himself at the disposal of Karl 10. Gustav. Hannibal Sehested was dismissed and stripped in the same year. The party of the sons-in-law was finished within three years of the old king's death, and its two ablest members were now respectively an exile working for the enemy and a private man without office.

It is worth pausing on how strange that is. Ulfeldt had been the richest and most powerful non-royal person in Denmark. What sent him across was not a policy or a principle but a poisoning allegation from a silk-ironer that the court itself declared false. Denmark's greatest traitor was made by a case that collapsed.

03 / NARRATIVEThe war Denmark chose, 1657

Danish terms in this section

Karl Gustav-krigene
The Karl Gustav Wars, 1657–60: two wars run together, the first declared by Denmark and the second by Sweden. The fourth and fifth of the eleven.
Frederiksodde
The fortress at the narrows of the Little Belt, on the site of present-day Fredericia. Stormed on 24 October 1657, which put a Swedish army on the shore opposite Funen.

In 1655 Karl 10. Gustav of Sweden invaded Poland, and by 1657 he was deep in a war he could not finish against an enemy that would not collapse. To the council in Copenhagen this looked like the opportunity of a generation: the provinces lost at Brømsebro might be recovered while the Swedish army was six hundred kilometres away and pinned.

The reasoning was not stupid. It was, in fact, the same reasoning any European cabinet would have applied, and Denmark had allies in prospect — the Dutch disliked Sweden, Poland and Russia were already fighting it, and the Emperor was interested. Sweden's willingness to fight was not in doubt: the million rigsdaler it had raised to buy back Älvsborg in 1613, by taxation that reached into every parish, had built the fiscal machinery that made the Polish war possible in the first place. Joachim Gersdorff, who had replaced Ulfeldt as steward, was for it. Denmark declared war in June 1657.

Three things had been left out of the calculation, and chapter 23 supplies all three. Denmark had no army worth the name, because Lutter destroyed one and nothing had replaced it. Jutland was indefensible, as two occupations in sixteen years had already demonstrated. And Karl Gustav was not a cabinet: he was a soldier who reacted to a declaration of war by abandoning his Polish campaign outright, turning his veteran army around, and marching it eight hundred kilometres north-west in six weeks.

He entered Holstein in July, took the duchies, and came up the peninsula on the same road as Tilly and Torstensson. Frederiksodde, the one modern fortress, was stormed on 24 October. By the end of the year Jutland was Swedish for the third time in thirty years, and the Danish government was on Zealand behind the water, which had always been enough.

04 / NARRATIVEThe march across the ice

Danish terms in this section

Toget over Bælt
'The march across the Belt': the crossing of the frozen Little and Great Belts by the Swedish army, 30 January to 8 February 1658. The most famous military operation in Scandinavian history and the least repeatable.
isvinter
An ice winter, in which the belts freeze hard enough to carry weight. They happen; one carrying an army with artillery had not been recorded before.

The winter of 1657–58 was extraordinarily cold. By the middle of January the Little Belt was frozen, and Karl Gustav did the thing nobody plans for.

On 30 January his army crossed from Jutland to Funen on the ice, cavalry first, in a column spread wide so that the weight did not concentrate. Some men and horses went through; not many. Funen was taken within days. Then, instead of stopping, he went on: from Svendborg over to Tåsinge and from Tåsinge to Langeland on 5 February, from Langeland to Lolland, from Lolland to Falster, and from Falster to Zealand on 8 February — a chain of crossings on sea ice, with guns, against an enemy that had built its entire strategic position on the assumption that water could not be walked over.

The Danish government at Copenhagen had an army of nothing, a fortified city that had not been put in order, and a Swedish field army now on the same island. There was no defensive line left to hold, and both sides knew it. Karl Gustav's own officers thought the crossing insane; the Danish council thought it impossible, which is why there was nothing on the Zealand shore to meet it.

It is the one operation in Danish history that everybody knows about, and the reason it is worth knowing is not the daring. It is that the entire Danish state — the toll, the fleet, the confidence of chapter 22, the whole idea that a country of islands cannot be conquered — turned out to rest on a meteorological fact that failed for two weeks in 1658.

05 / NARRATIVERoskilde, 26 February 1658

Danish terms in this section

Roskildefreden
The Peace of Roskilde, 26 February 1658. The largest territorial loss in Danish history, negotiated in eighteen days under the guns of an army twenty kilometres from the capital.
Skånelandene
The Scanian lands: Skåne, Halland and Blekinge. Danish since before there was a Denmark to speak of, and the demographic and agricultural heart of the realm.

Denmark asked for terms within days. The negotiations were conducted at Høje Taastrup and signed at Roskilde on 26 February, and the Swedish delegation included Corfitz Ulfeldt, formerly steward of the realm of Denmark, now acting for the king of Sweden. Joachim Gersdorff negotiated for Denmark.

Denmark ceded Skåne, Halland and Blekinge outright; Bohuslän from Norway; the Trondheim district, which cut Norway in half; and Bornholm. It renounced its anti-Swedish alliances, agreed to bar foreign fleets hostile to Sweden from the Baltic, and undertook to pay for the Swedish army's withdrawal.

Look at what that list is. Skåne was the richest agricultural province in the kingdom, with the archbishopric at Lund and something approaching a third of the realm's population. Halland had been pledged in 1645 and was now permanent. Blekinge and Bornholm gave Sweden the eastern approaches. And Bohuslän and Trøndelag cut Norway's coast into pieces. In one document Denmark stopped being a state that held both shores of the Sound — the fact everything from chapter 18 onward has rested on — and became a state that held one.

The eastern boundary of Denmark had not moved in six hundred years. It moved on a February afternoon, and it has not moved back.

Meanwhile in Europe

The years Denmark spends being dismembered are the years the European system is being rebuilt after Westphalia. England has executed one king and is ruled by Oliver Cromwell until his death in 1658, after which the monarchy comes back in 1660 — the same year Denmark abolishes its own elective monarchy and moves the opposite way. France is emerging from the Fronde with a boy king who will make absolutism the century's model. The Dutch Republic is at the height of its wealth and has decided, for reasons of trade rather than sentiment, that a Sweden holding both sides of the Sound is intolerable. That decision is the reason there is still a Denmark.

06 / NARRATIVEThe second war, and the siege

Danish terms in this section

Stormen på København
The storm of Copenhagen, the night of 10–11 February 1659: the general assault that failed, and the single most important night in the city's history.
borgervæbning
The citizen militia. In 1658–59 the burghers of Copenhagen, the students and the guilds manned the walls alongside the soldiers, which had constitutional consequences nobody intended.
Hans Nansen
Burgomaster of Copenhagen, a merchant and former Arctic trader, who organised the city's defence and in 1660 led the burghers' attack on noble privilege.

Karl Gustav had got everything he asked for and it was not what he wanted. What he wanted was Denmark — the whole of it, absorbed, with the Sound under one crown. In August 1658, six months after Roskilde and without a pretext of any kind, he landed at Korsør and marched on Copenhagen.

This was the mistake. Taking half of Denmark had been tolerable to the rest of Europe; taking all of it, and with it sole control of the Baltic entrance, was not tolerable to the Dutch, and it converted a Danish defeat into a general European question. It also did something to the Danes themselves. In February 1658 the country had asked for terms in a week. In August 1658 Copenhagen shut its gates and held them for twenty-one months.

Frederik 3. stayed. The line attributed to him — that he would die in his nest — may or may not be his, but he did not leave, and it mattered enormously that he did not. The walls were repaired by whoever could hold a spade, including the students of the university and the women of the city. The guilds took sections of the rampart. The king promised the burghers privileges in return, in writing, which he would be held to.

On the night of 10–11 February 1659 the Swedes attempted a general assault with scaling ladders against the western wall. It failed completely — the ladders were too short in places, the ditch had been flooded and refrozen, and the defence held everywhere. Swedish losses were severe, Danish losses were light, and the siege settled back into blockade. That night is the hinge of the whole chapter: the country had lost every field engagement of both wars and won the only one that had to be won.

07 / NARRATIVEBornholm and Trøndelag

Danish terms in this section

Bornholms opstand
The Bornholm rising of December 1658: an island handed to Sweden by treaty which took itself back and then chose whom to give itself to.
Hammershus
The great fortress in the north of Bornholm, from chapter 14. In 1658 it surrendered to a party of local farmers.

Two of the territories signed away at Roskilde refused to go, and neither refusal was organised from Copenhagen.

In Trøndelag the population was hostile from the start, the Swedish garrison was thin, and Norwegian forces under Jørgen Bjelke retook the district in the autumn of 1658 with substantial local help. On Bornholm it was stranger and more complete.

Vignette · Jens Kofoed, Rønne, 8 December 1658

The islanders learned they were Swedish in April, when the ice broke and a boat came over from Skåne with the news. Nobody had asked them. In May a delegation, including four of Jens Kofoed's cousins and his own brother, went to Malmö to swear allegiance to the king of Sweden in front of Corfitz Ulfeldt. The new commandant, Johan Printzensköld, landed on 29 April with a hundred and twenty musketeers to take possession.

On 8 December Printzensköld rode down from Hammershus to Rønne. He was shot in the street by Villum Clausen. Then Jens Kofoed and Niels Gumløs fired into the body — which was already dead, and which is the detail that explains everything. It made all of them murderers together. There was no going back and no one left who could turn informer.

Kofoed took a band north to Hammershus and talked the garrison into surrendering. They picked up the Swedish reinforcements as those landed at Sandvig. Then, holding an island that legally belonged to Sweden, the Bornholmers sent to Frederik 3. and offered it to him — on the condition, written into the gift, that Bornholm should never again be pledged or granted away to anyone. It never was.

Jens Pedersen Kofoed, farmer and militia captain · Rønne, Bornholm · 8 December 1658 · the island was handed to the Danish crown on terms the islanders set

Both risings are worth more than the space they usually get, and for the same reason. Roskilde was signed by a council of noblemen on behalf of a realm; the two districts that came back were taken back by people the council did not consult and could not have commanded. When the estates met in 1660 and the nobility's claim to govern was weighed against its record, this was part of the record.

Meanwhile in the world

While Denmark and Sweden fight over a strait, the trading empires that will matter for the next two centuries are being assembled elsewhere. The Dutch take Ceylon from the Portuguese and Manhattan is a Dutch town until 1664. England passes its Navigation Acts and begins the long commercial rivalry with the Dutch that produces three wars. The Mughal empire under Aurangzeb reaches its greatest extent from 1658. And in the Caribbean the sugar islands are being converted to slave labour on a scale that will draw Denmark in within twelve years — the beginning of chapter 27, and the part of this story that the Sound toll made possible and nobody in Copenhagen described honestly.

08 / NARRATIVEThe Dutch in the Sound

Danish terms in this section

Slaget i Øresund
The Battle of the Sound, 29 October 1658: a Dutch fleet forced the strait against the Swedish navy and relieved Copenhagen from the sea.
Nyborg
The battle of 14 November 1659 that cleared Funen, fought by Danish, Dutch, Polish, Brandenburg and imperial troops together.

The Dutch Republic did not intervene out of friendship. It intervened because roughly half of all Dutch shipping passed the Sound, and a single power holding both shores could set whatever price it liked — the same logic that had made Denmark's own toll tolerable only because Denmark was weak enough to be argued with.

On 29 October 1658 a Dutch fleet under Jacob van Wassenaer Obdam fought its way through the strait past the Swedish fleet, with the citizens of Copenhagen watching from the ramparts, and brought in grain, powder and men. Obdam was killed later; the Danish admiral Henrik Bjelke was in the line. After that the city could not be starved, and the storm of February 1659 was Karl Gustav's alternative to a siege he could no longer win.

From there it became a coalition war and Sweden was outnumbered. Brandenburg, Poland and the Emperor moved into Jutland from the south; at Nyborg on 14 November 1659 an allied force destroyed the Swedish army on Funen. Karl 10. Gustav, who was thirty-seven and had been at war continuously for five years, fell ill at Gothenburg and died on 13 February 1660.

Vignette · Leonora Christina, Malmö, 1659

She had followed her husband out of Copenhagen in 1651, to the Netherlands and then to Sweden, and she had watched him negotiate at Roskilde on the other side of the table from the country she was born in. She was Christian 4.'s daughter and Frederik 3.'s half-sister, and by 1658 she had no country that would have her.

Then Sweden turned on them too. Karl Gustav suspected Ulfeldt of corresponding with the Danes — he was probably right — and in 1659 both of them were arrested and held at Malmö for seventeen months. Ulfeldt was ill and, by every account including hers, largely useless. She conducted the defence: she handled the interrogations, wrote the petitions, managed what was left of their money and their people, and got them both out.

It bought them four more years. In 1663 Ulfeldt was condemned for treason in his absence and executed in effigy in Copenhagen, and Leonora Christina was seized in England, handed over, and put in the Blue Tower, where she stayed for twenty-two years and wrote the book that made her the best Danish prose writer of the century. That is chapter 26's story. What belongs here is that the competent one of the two was never the one with the office.

Leonora Christina Ulfeldt, born 1621 · Malmö · 1659 · seventeen months' imprisonment, and not her last

09 / NARRATIVEThe Peace of Copenhagen, 27 May 1660

Danish terms in this section

Københavnsfreden
The Peace of Copenhagen, 27 May 1660. It confirms Roskilde with three corrections, and it fixes the Danish–Swedish border where it still is.

With Karl Gustav dead and a Swedish regency wanting out, the allies imposed a settlement. Denmark recovered Bornholm and Trøndelag — the two districts that had already recovered themselves — and was released from the obligation to close the Baltic to Sweden's enemies. Everything else stood. Skåne, Halland, Blekinge and Bohuslän were Swedish, and are.

The Dutch made sure of one thing above all: that neither crown should hold both sides of the Sound, and that the toll should stay moderate and open to all. Denmark kept the toll and kept the capital, and in exchange it stopped being a power that could decide anything in the Baltic by itself. That is the settlement the next hundred and fifty years of Danish foreign policy operates inside.

10 / NARRATIVEWhat was left

Danish terms in this section

stænderforsamling
An assembly of the estates — nobility, clergy and burghers — summoned in September 1660 to find money. It did rather more than that.
skattefrihed
The nobility's exemption from taxation, held since 1536 and now the only large untaxed thing left in a bankrupt country.

Take the measure of what Frederik 3. had in the summer of 1660. A third of the realm's territory gone and with it something near a third of its people and its grain. Jutland occupied for three years and stripped twice over. Copenhagen intact but starved, its trade wrecked. A fleet reduced. An army of foreign mercenaries still in the country and unpaid. Crown land mortgaged past the point where it produced anything. And a treasury that could not meet the interest, let alone the principal.

He summoned the estates in September 1660 to find the money. What happened in that assembly was not planned by anyone in advance, and it went like this. The clergy and the burghers — led by Hans Nansen, burgomaster of Copenhagen, who had organised the defence of the city, and Hans Svane, bishop of Zealand — proposed that the nobility should pay tax like everybody else. The nobility refused, on the grounds of a privilege confirmed in every charter since 1536.

The argument the burghers made in reply is the one that ended the Danish Middle Ages. The nobility's exemption was payment for its service in defending the realm. The realm had just been lost — a third of it permanently — while the nobility governed and the burghers held the walls. The privilege had a job attached, and the job had not been done.

Within three weeks the estates had offered the crown to Frederik 3. and his heirs as a hereditary possession, releasing him from the charter of 1648 and from every charter before it. Nobody had to storm anything. The council of the realm, which had governed Denmark since 1536, voted itself out of existence in a room in Copenhagen because it could not answer a question about tax.

Myth-check

"Corfitz Ulfeldt sold Denmark to Sweden out of greed."
He did work for Sweden, he was at the Roskilde table, and the treason conviction of 1663 was substantially deserved. But the sequence matters: he left in 1651 after an accusation the Danish court itself found false, and Sweden imprisoned him in 1659 for corresponding with Denmark. He was a man who could not be trusted by anyone, which is not the same as a man bought by somebody.
"Frederik 3. said 'I will die in my nest' and the words saved Copenhagen."
The attribution is late and the wording varies. What is solid is that he stayed in the city when leaving was the obvious course, that his presence was worth a great deal to the defence, and that he made written promises to the burghers to get their help. The promises are better evidence of what happened in 1660 than the quotation is.
"The march across the ice was an act of genius nobody could have foreseen."
It was audacious and it was also a gamble that came within a few days of being impossible — the ice was fit for about a fortnight and Karl Gustav's own officers argued against it. Calling it genius makes the Danish failure look inevitable. The failure was that Denmark had declared war with no army, having watched two foreign powers walk up Jutland unopposed within living memory.
"Skåne was lost because Denmark was defeated."
Skåne was lost because Denmark started a war it could not fight and then negotiated under the guns of an army outside its capital. The distinction matters: Roskilde was not the outcome of a long decline but of a specific decision taken in Copenhagen in June 1657, against a background everybody involved could see.
"Absolutism was imposed on Denmark in 1660."
It was offered. The clergy and the burghers proposed a hereditary crown, the nobility could not defend its exemption, and the whole thing was done by assembly in about three weeks without a shot. Frederik 3. took what he was given, exactly as he had in 1648 — but this time what he was given was everything. Chapter 25 has to explain why an assembly of subjects voluntarily created the most complete absolutism in Europe.

Summary

IF YOU REMEMBER FIVE THINGSThe page in five

  1. Corfitz Ulfeldt, steward of the realm, left Denmark in July 1651 after a poisoning accusation the court found false. Dina Vinhofvers, who made it, was beheaded. He went to Sweden and was at the Roskilde table in 1658.

  2. Denmark declared war in June 1657 to recover Brømsebro's losses while Sweden was pinned in Poland. Karl Gustav abandoned Poland, marched north in six weeks, and took Jutland by October.

  3. Between 30 January and 8 February 1658 the Swedish army crossed the frozen belts by way of Funen, Langeland, Lolland and Falster to Zealand — six crossings on sea ice, against a defensive system built entirely on the assumption that water cannot be walked over.

  4. At Roskilde on 26 February 1658 Denmark ceded Skåne, Halland, Blekinge, Bohuslän, Trøndelag and Bornholm. Karl Gustav then attacked again in August without pretext, which brought in the Dutch; the storm of Copenhagen failed on 11 February 1659, and the Peace of Copenhagen in 1660 returned only Bornholm and Trøndelag — both of which had already retaken themselves.

  5. At the estates in September 1660 the burghers argued that noble tax exemption was payment for defending the realm, and the realm had just been lost. Within three weeks the crown was made hereditary and the charter of 1648 was void.

Questions & discussion

FOUR KINDSWork the material

Recall

Did the facts land?

  • What was the Dina affair, and what did it cost each of the people involved?
  • Why did Denmark think June 1657 was a good moment to declare war?
  • Trace the route of the march across the belts, island by island.
  • Name the six territories ceded at Roskilde, and the two that came back.
  • What argument did the burghers make about noble tax exemption in 1660?

Causal

Why did that follow from this?

  • Why was Karl Gustav's second attack in August 1658 the thing that saved Denmark?
  • Why did the Dutch care which crown held the Sound, and what did they insist on in 1660?
  • Why did Bornholm and Trøndelag come back when Skåne did not?
  • Why did losing a war make the nobility's constitutional position indefensible, rather than the king's?

Counterfactual

What if it had gone otherwise?

  • If the winter of 1657–58 had been ordinary, what does the war look like — and does Denmark still lose Skåne?
  • If Karl Gustav had stopped at Roskilde and consolidated, is there a Denmark in 1700?
  • If the storm of 11 February 1659 had succeeded, what happens to the Sound, the toll and the Dutch?

Contested

Where the argument still is

  • Whether the war of 1657 was a reasonable gamble or an obvious blunder. The council thought it reasonable; the information available at the time was not as clear as hindsight makes it.
  • How far 1660 was a coup by the king and how far a genuine initiative of the burghers and clergy. Danish historiography has moved back and forth on this for a century and the sources are compatible with both.
  • Whether Skåne was ever recoverable after 1660. The wars of the 1670s and 1700s say it was tried; whether it could have worked is a live question.
  • What Leonora Christina's own account is worth as evidence, given that she wrote it long afterwards, in prison, and with a case to make.

  • Rønne, Bornholm Memorial stones in the street mark where Printzensköld was shot. They are easy to miss and the town is small enough to find them.
  • Fredericia Built as Frederiksodde and stormed in October 1657; refounded and refortified afterwards. The ramparts are among the best preserved in Denmark and you can walk the whole circuit.
  • Malmö and Lund, Skåne An hour from Copenhagen by train. Lund cathedral was the archbishopric of Denmark for five hundred years and the Danish-period buildings are everywhere once you start looking.
  • Nyborg Castle, Funen The battle of November 1659 was fought outside it. The castle itself is from chapter 13 and has been under a long restoration; check before travelling.

Closing Part F

1536 – 1660What this part was about

Four chapters, a hundred and twenty-four years, and one arrangement from beginning to end. In October 1536 a king who had just won a civil war signed away the right to tax, legislate or make war without the consent of two hundred and fifty families, in exchange for their help and half the land of Denmark. In October 1660 an assembly of clergy and burghers handed his great-grandson everything back, and the families could not stop them.

The thing that made the arrangement work for a century was the Sound. A crown with a large foreign cash income does not have to ask, and chapters 21 and 22 are what that bought: a Lutheran state church, a solvent treasury, Kronborg, Uraniborg, a standing fleet, Frederiksborg, Rosenborg, six new towns and the best observatory in the world. None of it was voted. None of it was paid for by anyone the council represented.

The thing that broke the arrangement was that the same independence let a king make war whenever he could find a legal costume to do it in. Christian 4. found one twice, in 1611 and 1625, and the council's power to refuse turned out to be worth nothing at the only moments it mattered. After Lutter there was no army; after 1645 there was no undiminished map; after 1658 there was no Skåne.

So the estates in 1660 were looking at a nobility that held two-fifths of the land and paid no tax, in exchange for defending a realm that had just lost a third of itself. That is not a constitutional argument that can be won. The adelsvælde did not fall to a revolution or a coup; it fell to an unanswerable question, put in a room, about who was going to pay for the mercenaries.

What Denmark had left in 1660 was the shape it still has: the peninsula, the islands, one shore of the Sound, Norway for another century and a half, and the North Atlantic. Everything grander than that has been given away or taken in the four chapters just read. Part G begins the day after, with a bankrupt king holding more power than any monarch in Europe and no obvious idea what to do with it.
