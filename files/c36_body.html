Provisorietiden and the change of system

1875 – 1901 · J.B.S. Estrup · Christian 9. · Julius Rasmussen · Line Luplau · J.C. Christensen

The constitution of 1849 never said whether a government answers to the king or to the elected chamber. For nineteen years two sides read that silence differently; for nine of them Denmark was governed on emergency decrees, with a military police force in its villages and a fortress going up round its capital that would never be fired. The rule that settled it was not written down until 1953.

Introduction

WHAT THIS PAGE ANSWERSFive questions

  1. The 1849 constitution is silent about who a ministry answers to. How did two honest readings of that silence produce nineteen years of conflict?
  2. The Folketing formally rejected the provisional finance law in January 1886 and nothing at all happened. Why not?
  3. Venstre spent years voting down expenditure to starve the government. What did that do to the treasury, and why does it change what the fight was about?
  4. One man fired two shots at Estrup in 1885. Why did everything that followed help Estrup?
  5. Denmark got parliamentary government in 1901 and did not write it into the constitution until 1953. What held it in place from 1901 to 1953?

01 / NARRATIVETwo chambers, two countries

Danish terms in this section

Højre and Venstre
"the Right" and "the Left". Not parties in the modern sense at the start of this chapter and increasingly so by its end. Venstre is the party of the farmers and the Folketing; Højre of the landowners, officials and the Landsting. Venstre is the ancestor of today's Venstre, Højre of today's Konservative.
Folketinget, Landstinget
the lower and upper chambers of the Rigsdag; after 1866 the first elected by every man of thirty with his own household, the second partly appointed by the king and partly elected on a privileged franchise (chapters 33 and 34).

The revision of 1866 gave Denmark two chambers elected on different franchises, and by 1872 they were returning different countries.

The Folketing kept universal suffrage and was carried, from 1872, by Venstre: the heirs of chapter 32's Bondevennerne, the freeholders of chapter 35, the parishes, the meeting-houses, the folk high schools and the cooperative dairies. The Landsting was elected through electors of whom half were chosen by the highest taxpayers alone, with twelve members appointed by the king for life and the town-and-country distribution frozen into the law. It was carried by Højre: landowners, senior officials, the officer corps and the established church.

Neither was unrepresentative. That is the difficulty. Both chambers accurately reflected the constituencies they had been designed to reflect, and those constituencies wanted opposite things. In 1873 Venstre drew the conclusion and demanded folketingsparlamentarisme — that the government be appointed on the basis of the Folketing's majority. Højre answered that the constitution gave the king the right to choose his ministers, and that a chamber was not a government.

Both were quoting the same document. Neither was misquoting it.

And neither chamber could dissolve the other, dismiss the other, or outvote the other. They were built to check each other and had no procedure for what to do when checking was all they did.

02 / NARRATIVEThe question nobody had answered

Danish terms in this section

parlamentarisme
here specifically folketingsparlamentarisme: the rule that a government must have, or at least not have against it, a majority in the Folketing. Demanded from 1873, conceded in practice in 1901, written into the constitution in 1953.
provisorisk lov
a provisional law, issued by the government under §25 in urgent cases when the Rigsdag is not sitting, and laid before it afterwards.
finanslov
the annual finance act. Under §48 it goes to the Folketing first, which is the whole of Venstre's constitutional argument.

The constitution of 1849, and the revision of 1866 after it, never said whether a ministry answers to the king or to the Folketing. It was not evaded; it was not thought of. In 1849 the question of who dismisses a government had no obvious answer because no Danish government had ever been dismissed by anybody but the king, and the men writing the text were more concerned to establish that ministers were answerable at all.

The men of 1849 had thought hard about ministerial responsibility, but the responsibility they had in mind was legal: a minister could be impeached for breaking the law. That a minister might be perfectly lawful and simply unwanted by the elected chamber was not a case they wrote for, because in 1849 the elected chamber was the new and untried thing and the king was the fact everyone had grown up with.

So the nineteen-year conflict that follows is a gap in a document, and both sides read the gap honestly.

Venstre's text was §48: the finance bill must be laid before the Folketing first. From a procedural precedence they argued a constitutional one — the chamber that sees the money first is the chamber the government must satisfy.

Højre's text was §25: in særdeles paatrængende Tilfælde, when the Rigsdag was not assembled, the king could issue provisional laws. The reading was not improvised. It was supplied by two professors of law, H. Matzen and J. Nellemann, and Nellemann was also the minister of justice — so the government's legal opinion and the government's law officer were the same man.

For money, the clause (§30 in the text of 1849) had been used once before Estrup: on 12 May 1853, when the Rigsdag had been dissolved on 19 April with the finance bill unpassed, a provisional law extended the temporary appropriation law of 28 March until the finance law for 1853–54 was passed, and at the latest to 31 August. That is the whole precedent: a stopgap between two parliaments.

And the clause carried a condition that shows what it was for. A provisional law could only be issued while the Rigsdag was not sitting. To keep issuing them, the government therefore had to keep sending the Rigsdag home. The emergency provision could only be used by manufacturing the emergency.

03 / NARRATIVEEstrup

Danish terms in this section

konseilspræsident
council president; the office called prime minister after 1918.
visnepolitik
the "withering policy": Venstre's tactic of voting down every expenditure not strictly necessary, to starve the government into resignation. It filled the treasury instead.

Jacob Brønnum Scavenius Estrup was a landowner, born at Sorø, whose seat was Skaføgård in Djursland, and the reader has met him already, in chapter 34 §09, as the landowner whose proposal for the upper chamber came back in the compromise of 1866. He had sat in the Rigsråd's Landsting from 1864, and he sat in the new one until his death in 1913, elected until 1898 and appointed by the king from 1900. He helped build the machine and then sat inside it.

He was not a soldier or an orator. Tuberculosis showed itself in his mid-teens; he never took the student examination, and passed the forestry examination instead, in 1844; and the illness left him with a discipline that people who disliked him described as coldness. He became council president in 1875 and stayed for nineteen years.

The conflict began before the provisional laws did. In the autumn of 1873 the Venstre groups voted down the finance bill, on the reasoning that a country cannot be governed without one and a government that cannot get one must go. The government dissolved the Folketing instead, and Venstre lost two seats at the election. In 1877 the two chambers could not agree the budget and Estrup issued a provisional finance law — a modest one, covering only what both chambers had already voted; it was rejected on 7 November, and a temporary finance law of 8 November took its place. The technique had been tested and put away.

Venstre's answer, proclaimed by Christen Berg in 1881, was the visnepolitik, the withering policy: vote down every expenditure that was not strictly necessary, and starve the government into resigning. It had a consequence its authors did not intend. By the mid-1880s the treasury was unusually full — over sixty million kroner — because almost nothing had been voted for years. So when the crisis came, the argument was not that the money was lacking. The money was sitting there. What was in dispute was who had the right to spend it.

There was a third party to this and he is easy to leave out. Christian 9. was not a bystander: the ministry was his, appointed by him and kept in office by him against the vote of the Folketing, and without his signature none of it was possible. It is worth remembering what he had learned about yielding. In 1863, three days on the throne, he had been pressed into signing a constitution he believed would cost the country its duchies, and it had — chapter 34 §01. A king who had given way once under pressure, and watched the result, was not a king disposed to give way twice. That is an interpretation and is offered as one, but it is hard to read his conduct in the 1880s without it.

It matters to say what Estrup was not. He was not a man seizing power he had no claim to. He had a clause, two professors, a majority in one chamber and the support of the king, and he believed — with a conviction that is easy to caricature and was widely shared in his class and elsewhere in Europe — that a chamber elected by every man in the country should not be permitted to appoint the government of the country. He was defending a constitution as he read it.

He was also, by the end, a man who had governed for nine years against the express vote of the elected chamber, using an emergency clause to build a fortress, and who had put a military police force into the villages of people who disagreed with him. Both of those sentences are true and the chapter needs both.

Meanwhile · Berlin, 1862–1866.

Prussia's chambers refused Bismarck a budget for army reform, and Bismarck governed without one for four years on the argument that the constitution provided no procedure for the case and the state must be administered regardless — the Lückentheorie, the theory of the gap. He was retrospectively indemnified in 1866 by a parliament that had just watched him win two wars. The resemblance to Denmark in the 1880s is not a coincidence: the same reasoning from the same kind of silence, and the provisional laws that followed the attempt on Estrup in October 1885 took, as the Danish accounts in §05 say, the German emergency laws as their model. The man who took Schleswig from Denmark supplied the method by which Denmark was then governed against its own majority.

04 / NARRATIVERuling without a budget, 1885

Danish terms in this section

Provisorietiden
the years of provisional government. Usually given as 1885–1894, sometimes as 1877–1894 from the first, much smaller provisional finance law. Both datings are in the literature and the difference is real: 1877 was a technique tested, 1885 was a technique adopted.

Christiansborg had burned down on Friday 3 October 1884, days before the Rigsdag's ordinary session was due to open on Monday the 6th, so the two chambers met that winter in makeshift conditions in Fredericiagade, in what is now the Eastern High Court: two hostile assemblies in a temporary building, in a state whose parliament no longer had a house.

Venstre would not pass a finance bill carrying the fortification of Copenhagen. Estrup did not negotiate. On 31 March 1885 he had the royal resolution ready and sent the Rigsdag home with no budget passed; on 1 April the government issued a provisional finance law whose contents it had written itself.

Then it did the same thing the next year, and the next: every year until 1894 the Rigsdag was sent home before the finance bill was through.

What the laws actually said is worth reading, because it is blander than the conflict around it and more remarkable for being bland. They authorised the government to meet the necessary expenses of the state out of the treasury, as the government judged them. That is the whole of it. There is no rhetoric in a provisional finance law and no emergency described in it. It is an accounting instrument, and for nine years it was the legal basis on which a European constitutional monarchy was administered.

Meanwhile the ordinary business continued, which is the part that startles a modern reader. Elections were held. Estrup's party lost them, repeatedly and heavily; Højre never came close to a Folketing majority in these years and never expected to. The results were published, the members took their seats, the chamber debated, and the government went on spending money the chamber had refused. Nobody was prevented from voting. It simply made no difference to who governed, and everybody could see that it made no difference, which is precisely what turned a disagreement about a clause into a crisis about the state.

The legal position was stranger than either side's slogans. A provisional law was by its own nature temporary and had to be laid before the Rigsdag when it reassembled. It was. On 25 January 1886 the Folketing rejected the provisional finance law of 1 April 1885 — and nothing happened. The money had been spent. A royal resolution the following day dealt with the consequences, and the government carried on. The constitution provided a procedure for the Rigsdag to say no and no procedure for the no to mean anything.

That is the exact shape of the thing, and it is worth resisting both easy descriptions. It was not a coup: no soldiers surrounded the chamber, elections continued to be held and lost, the courts sat, Estrup submitted his laws for rejection as the text required. Nor was it lawful government: a clause whose one earlier use for money had been a stopgap between two parliaments was used for nine years to spend money the elected chamber had refused, and the government had to send parliament home every year in order to keep using it.

What it was, precisely, is a government discovering that a constitution which provides no remedy provides no restraint, and deciding to find out how far that went.

05 / NARRATIVEThe gendarmes

Danish terms in this section

gendarm
a member of the military gendarmerie corps created by provisional law on 27 October 1885 to assist the police. Light-blue uniforms, hence de blå gendarmer.
riffelforening
rifle association. Grown up beside the Grundtvigian meeting-houses, and stopped from drilling by the Riffellov of 5 May 1885, which stood until 15 April 1887.

Up to this point the conflict was conducted between men in a building. In 1885 it came out into the country, and that is what people remembered.

It began with weapons. In impotence at their exclusion from any influence, Venstre supporters had been organising rifle associations across the countryside — descendants of the shooting clubs that had grown up beside the Grundtvigian meeting-houses, and not obviously an armed threat, but not obviously not one either. On 5 May 1885 the government issued the Riffellov by provisional law, forbidding anyone to acquire or hand out rifles, or to drill in their use, without permission — which stopped the associations' shooting. It stood until 15 April 1887.

Then came 21 October, which is §07. Six days after it, on 27 October 1885, the government issued a provisional law creating a military gendarmerie corps to assist the police anywhere in the kingdom, together with laws on police expenditure and on criminal-court assessors. On 2 November a criminal-law provisorium made it an offence to put about "invented or distorted facts" in order to render the institutions of the state or the measures of the government hated or contemptible.

Danish accounts say plainly where the model came from: the far-reaching provisional laws Germany had issued after two attempts on the Kaiser's life.

The gendarmes wore light blue and were universally known by it. They were posted into market towns and villages, and they were loathed, because what they represented was not policing but supervision: the state stationing armed men among farmers whose offence was how they voted. At Brønderslev market on 6 September 1886 the crowd turned on the gendarmes, who took refuge in an inn; two days later the district magistrate declared the town in a state of siege — drinking places shut at nine, and no groups of more than four in the streets after ten — and twenty-eight men thought to have taken part were arrested and tried.

It reached into how people bought and sold. Villages boycotted shopkeepers who took Højre's side and shopkeepers boycotted Venstre customers; a district could divide over which inn a meeting was held in, and the schoolteacher, the priest and the parish council chairman found themselves publicly on one side or the other whether or not they wished to be. This is the same social geography chapter 35 described, put under pressure: the meeting-house and the mission house, the rifle club and the rectory, now with a political test applied to each.

The prosecutions ran alongside. At a meeting in Holstebro on 14 June 1885 the Venstre leader Christen Berg refused to speak while the police chief held the platform, and local Venstre men removed the official; in January 1886 the Supreme Court gave Berg six months in prison, and he served them at once. In 1886 the theologian and folk high school principal Morten Pontoppidan, of Jørlunde, attacked Estrup for having broken his oath to the constitution, and the Supreme Court gave him three months in prison — for saying what, on Venstre's reading of the constitution, was true.

Some historians have written that Denmark could have gone to civil war in these years. That is a counterfactual and should be labelled one. What can be said without speculation is that a country with no rebellion, no barricades and no political killings lived until 1894 with a military police force in its villages, its rifle clubs forbidden to drill from 1885 to 1887, its leading opposition politician and a folk high school principal sent to prison, and its budget written by a government the majority had voted against.

06 / NARRATIVEA wall around Copenhagen

Danish terms in this section

Vestvolden
the western rampart, 1888–92: fourteen kilometres from Utterslev Mose to Køge Bugt, one of the largest construction works in Danish history, and today the country's largest protected ancient monument.

Underneath the constitutional argument was a spending argument, and underneath that was a wall.

Højre held that Copenhagen must be fortified against a modern army, and after 1864 it was hard to call the anxiety unreasonable. Venstre held that a small state cannot fortify its way out of its neighbours and that the money belonged to schools, and it was hard to call that unreasonable either. What made it irreconcilable was that Estrup would not give the works up and Venstre would not vote them even from the surplus its own obstruction had created.

So the fortification was built by decree. Work on the land defences ran from 1886, and the Vestvold itself was thrown up between 1888 and 1892: a single continuous rampart fourteen kilometres long from Utterslev Mose in the north to Køge Bugt in the south, broken only where an existing road or railway went through, with its guns in heavy concrete caponiers. It was built under the engineer officer E.J. Sommerfeldt, head from 1886 of an engineering directorate set up for the western front, whose work won him recognition among foreign specialists; the war minister answerable for it was Estrup's J.J. Bahnson. The land fortification cost about 13.2 million kroner between 1886 and 1894, by Bahnson's own figures.

It was one of the largest construction works in Danish history, and it was built by hand. About 3.15 million cubic metres of earth and chalk were moved with shovels, wheelbarrows and tip-wagons by local contractors and some two thousand workers, about a third of them immigrants from Sweden. Concrete mixing machines did not yet exist, so the concrete was mixed in large tubs and carried. A railway was laid along the rampart road using worn-out track cast off by the state railways.

The 1894 settlement stopped further building. In 1909, fifteen years later, it was decided that the land fortification should be abolished from 1922. When war came in 1914 a security force of fifty thousand men was called up to the Copenhagen fortress; it was scaled down from 1915 and disbanded in 1919, and the attack never came. The land fortification was abolished with effect from 1 April 1920, two years early.

The wall that broke the Danish constitution was never fired in anger, and it was obsolete before the men who dug it had grown old.

07 / NARRATIVE21 October 1885

In nineteen years of it there was one attempt on anyone's life, and it came from a man acting entirely alone.

Vignette · Julius Rasmussen, Toldbodvej, 21 October 1885

He was born on Christianshavn on 5 July 1866, twenty-three days before the revised constitution was signed. He was a typographer. He had worked on a Venstre paper on Lolland-Falster, where he had joined the local Venstre association and taken a seat on its committee, and joined the local rifle association, where he acquired a pistol.

Back in Copenhagen, setting type, he watched a constitutional struggle conducted with a patience he could not share, and decided to take it into his own hands.

On the 21st he waited outside Estrup's house at Toldbodvej 26. When Estrup came home late in the afternoon he asked him Er De Minister Estrup? Estrup said yes. He fired. The bullet struck a coat button and lodged in the lining. Estrup said Hvad, skyder De paa mig? — what, are you shooting at me? — and the second shot bored a couple of centimetres into the gate behind him.

He made no attempt to escape. He told those who held him that they need not hold on to him, he would not run; he had done it, he said, to save the country. He was nineteen: a small, pale young man who by every account had never hurt anyone in his life.

Estrup went on to his dinner engagement that evening, and was at the whist table after the meal when the journalists began arriving to find him.

Julius Rasmussen was sentenced to fourteen years' hard labour and sent to Horsens. He was found dead in his cell there on 27 November 1889, aged twenty-three. On his coffin lay wreaths from the Venstre voters' association in Vejle, from three provincial newspapers, from students in Copenhagen, and from the typographers of Aalborg and of Vejle.

Julius Rasmussen, typographer · Toldbodvej 26, Copenhagen · 21 October 1885 · [n]

It is worth being clear about how bad the autumn of 1885 was before he acted. The provisional finance law and the Riffellov had been issued that spring, and the money the Folketing had refused was to build the fortification. A young man reading a Venstre paper in October 1885 was reading that his country's government was governing illegally, that the Venstre leader was being prosecuted, and that nothing whatever could be done about it through any channel the constitution provided. That was accurate.

Everything he wanted, the two shots made worse. Estrup's popularity rose as never before; on 25 October thirteen thousand people gathered in a demonstration for him; Christen Berg himself left his congratulations at the house; and the laws of 27 October and 2 November (§05) were justified by what had happened outside the gate on Toldbodvej.

Meanwhile · Kristiania, 1884.

Norway got there seventeen years before Denmark, and by the opposite route. Where the Danish Folketing could only refuse and wait, the Norwegian Storting impeached the Selmer ministry before the Riksrett, removed it, and forced the king to appoint a government from the parliamentary majority. The difference is procedural and it is the whole difference: Norway's constitution contained a mechanism for removing a government and Denmark's did not. Danish Venstre watched it closely and could not copy it.

08 / NARRATIVEThe other opposition

Venstre was not the only opposition, and the other two were arranged along the same seam.

The Social Democrats entered the Folketing in 1884, with two seats. They were outside the constitutional argument in the sense that neither reading of the text did anything for the people they represented, and inside it in the sense that a Højre that could rule against the elected chamber could rule against anybody.

They did not stay at two seats. The party built outward through the 1890s while the constitutional struggle went on over its head: a national union of the unskilled in 1897, a national federation of trade unions in 1898, and in 1899 the great lockout and the September Compromise of chapter 35 §09. By 1901 the Social Democrats were a parliamentary party with a press, a union movement and a programme, and they had achieved all of it in a decade when the elected chamber they sat in did not control the government.

The religious division ran with the political one, closely enough to be the same line seen twice. Venstre's rural base was substantially Grundtvigian and made heavy use of the free-congregation law of 1868, which let a congregation choose and pay its own priest inside the folkekirke — the legal grandchild of chapter 33's sognebåndsløsning. The hierarchy of the established church sat with Højre. A parish's meeting-house, its rifle club, its cooperative dairy and its Folketing vote tended to be the same people; the mission house, the rectory and the manor tended to be the others.

Vignette · Line Luplau, Copenhagen, 15 February 1889

She was sixty-five, and she had come to this late. From 1866 to 1888 she had been a vicar's wife in Varde, where she helped found a relief society for the town's poor, and where she was one of the first women in Denmark to speak at a Constitution Day meeting.

On 15 February 1889 the Kvindevalgretsforeningen, which she had founded with the municipal schoolteacher Louise Nørlund, held its first public meeting. Fifteen hundred people came. It was the first Danish association with women's suffrage and nothing else on its programme, and she chaired it.

Two decisions of hers belong in this chapter rather than in a history of the women's movement. She built it deliberately as a mixed association, breaking with the women-only principle of the society she came from, Kvindelig Fremskridtsforening, whose chairman, Matilde Bajer, stood down, disappointed in part that suffrage had been taken out of its hands. And she took her support where the argument actually was: from Social Democrat and Venstre men, from left-liberal women, and from the "red" working women of the unions. She said she did not care whether she was called a Venstre woman or a Social Democrat so long as nobody called her a sympathiser of Højre.

She gave up the chair in the spring of 1891 and died that September. At her burial the red banners of the trade-union women flew over the churchyard. Her daughter Marie painted the group portrait of the campaign's first days, and in 1917, with the vote won in 1915, it was hung in the Folketing.

Line Luplau, vicar's wife and suffragist · Copenhagen · 15 February 1889 · [f]

Three oppositions, then, and one line: the farmers, the workers and the women were all on the far side of it from the landowners, the officers and the bishops. That alignment is why the constitutional struggle was never only constitutional, and why its resolution changed more than a procedure.

09 / NARRATIVEThe settlement of 1894

Danish terms in this section

Forliget 1894
the settlement of 1 April 1894 that ended the provisional laws, after which Estrup resigned; it answered no constitutional question at all.

From about 1890 the moderate wings on both sides began to move toward each other, and the popular protests thinned as they did. On 1 April 1894 they made a bargain.

The provisional finance laws stopped. The further build-out of the fortification that the army wanted was suspended, and the gendarmes went. And on 7 August Estrup resigned, after nineteen years.

What the settlement did not do was answer the question. It ended the emergency and left the gap in the constitution exactly where it had been: nothing was written down about whether a ministry answers to the Folketing, and nothing prevented a future government from reading §25 as Estrup had read it.

The reaction was immediate and it came from Venstre's own side. At the election of 9 April 1895 the Venstre men who opposed the settlement rose to fifty-four of the Folketing's hundred and fourteen seats, and with the Social Democrats' eight they had a majority; Højre and the moderate Venstre, the parties of the settlement, had fifty-two between them. In the same month the opponents formed the Venstrereformparti. Its real leader, J.C. Christensen, was made chairman of the finance committee and resumed the visnepolitik in a harder form: drain the treasury, stop every piece of reform legislation, and pass nothing until parliamentary government had been conceded.

He was still the village schoolmaster at Stadil.

10 / NARRATIVE1901

Danish terms in this section

Systemskiftet
"the change of system": the appointment on 24 July 1901 of a ministry drawn from the Folketing majority.

What finished it was arithmetic. Højre's share of the Folketing had been falling for a generation and by the election of 1901 it was derisory — a party holding the government of the country on the votes of a small fraction of those who voted for the chamber that mattered. The Landsting franchise still protected it in the other chamber, but no reading of any clause could make the position look like anything but what it was.

Christian 9. gave way. He did it without conceding the principle: he never accepted that the constitution required him to appoint a ministry from the Folketing majority, and he did not say that he was doing so. He simply appointed one.

The ministry took office on 24 July 1901. Its head was J.H. Deuntzer, a professor of law at the University of Copenhagen since 1872, new to parliamentary work, who took the foreign ministry as well; the composition was settled mainly by the leaders of the three Venstre wings, J.C. Christensen, P.A. Alberti and Christopher Hage. Hage had finance, Ole Hansen — a farmer — agriculture, and Viggo Hørup, the radicals' symbol and the most effective journalist of the opposition, was made minister of public works: Christensen would rather have left him out, and gave way to a thinly veiled threat.

Vignette · J.C. Christensen, Stadil, 1901

He was born in 1856 on a heath farm at Påbøl in west Jutland and began working life as a herd boy. He trained as a teacher, and from 1886 to 1901 he taught the village school at Stadil, near Ringkøbing.

It was the Provisorietid that made him political. He became chairman of his parish council in 1888 and was elected to the Folketing for the Ringkøbing seat in January 1890, as a follower of Christen Berg; he held it for thirty-four years. When Berg died in 1891 it was Christensen who carried on his fight against the moderates' policy of negotiating with Højre. The Venstrereformparti was formed without him, but he became its secretary, under Sofus Høgsbro's chairmanship, and from 1897 its chairman.

In the ministry of 24 July 1901 he was formally the minister for church and education. He was in fact the head of the government, and everyone including Deuntzer knew it. His opponents called him foxy, and the caricaturists of the day drew him with the fox in every cartoon.

He would be council president from 1905 to 1908.

J.C. Christensen, schoolmaster · Stadil, west Jutland · 1901 · [-]

What the ministry then did is the reason this is a chapter ending rather than a date, and most of it, the school law of 1903 among it, is chapter 37's. One law belongs here, because it closes a thread of this part. In May 1903 Christensen, as minister for church and education, carried a law on parish councils: an elected council of at least four members in every parish, chosen by the baptised who put themselves on a church electoral roll, women voting and standing on the same terms as men, with the priest as chairman by right.

That was a step towards something chapter 33 left open. Article 80 of the constitution of 1849 had promised that the constitution of the folkekirke would be settled by law, and from June 1849 to May 1903 no government had done it. The first parliamentary government in Danish history put an elected body into every parish in the country within two years of taking office. It was expressly temporary, for six years, pending a final settlement of the church's constitution. That settlement has still not been made.

The first ministry appointed by a parliamentary majority also contained, from its first day, its own undoing: P.A. Alberti, minister of justice, whose fraud brought down Christensen's own government in 1908, and whose story is chapter 37's.

The change of system did not put parliamentarism into the constitution. It was not written down until 1953, where this book ends. From 1901 to 1953 the most important rule in Danish politics was a rule everybody obeyed and nobody had enacted — which, if you have read the last nine sections, is a very Danish way for the argument to have ended.

Myth-check

"1901 gave Denmark parliamentary government."

It gave Denmark the practice and not the rule, and the distinction held from 1901 to 1953.

What happened on 24 July 1901 was that a king appointed a ministry from the Folketing majority. What did not happen was any change to the text. Christian 9. did not concede that the constitution obliged him; he was careful not to, and his successors inherited the same unaltered clauses. Parliamentarism was not written into the Danish constitution until 1953, which is where this book ends.

So for half a century the most important rule in Danish political life had no legal existence. It was obeyed because breaking it had been tried, at length, by a determined and intelligent government with the king and a chamber and two professors behind it — and had cost that government its reputation and finally its office. The rule held because everyone had watched what happened to the man who tested it.

There is a second, smaller myth attached, and it is the mirror image. That Estrup simply broke the constitution. He did not simply break it: he used a real clause, submitted his laws for the rejection the text required, held elections and lost them and went on. What he demonstrated is something less dramatic and more uncomfortable — that a constitution which describes a disagreement but provides no way to settle it is not a constraint on a government willing to sit still. The gap was the fault. Estrup only stood in it.

Summary

IF YOU REMEMBER FIVE THINGSThe page in five

  1. The revision of 1866 produced a Folketing carried by Venstre and a Landsting carried by Højre, and from 1873 Venstre demanded that governments be appointed from the Folketing majority. The constitution did not say whether they should be. Both sides read the silence honestly and neither could be argued out of it.

  2. Estrup governed for nineteen years from 1875, and from 1 April 1885 he sent the Rigsdag home each year without a budget and issued a provisional finance law of his own writing under an emergency clause used for money only twice before — as a stopgap in 1853, and in his own modest law of 1877. The elected chamber formally rejected the first of them in January 1886, and nothing followed, because the constitution provided a way to say no and no way for the no to matter.

  3. The conflict left the chamber and entered the country: rifle associations forbidden to drill, a military gendarmerie in light blue posted into the villages, a press law against making the government contemptible, and the Venstre leader and a folk-high-school principal in prison. One nineteen-year-old typographer fired two shots at Estrup in October 1885, and every consequence of it favoured Estrup.

  4. What the money was for was a fourteen-kilometre rampart round Copenhagen, dug by hand between 1888 and 1892, never fired in anger and abolished in 1920. The treasury was full; the argument was never about whether Denmark could afford it but about who was entitled to decide.

  5. The settlement of 1894 ended the provisional laws, Estrup resigned, and nothing was answered; and on 24 July 1901 a king who never conceded the principle appointed a ministry from the Folketing majority anyway. Parliamentarism was not written into the constitution until 1953.

Questions & discussion

FOUR KINDSWork the material

Recall

Did the facts land?

  • Why were the two chambers meeting in Fredericiagade in the winter of 1884–85?
  • What made Line Luplau's Kvindevalgretsforeningen different from the women's society she came from, and whose support did she seek?
  • Who chose the ministers of 24 July 1901, and which portfolio did J.C. Christensen hold in it?

Causal

Why did it happen that way?

  • The Folketing formally rejected the provisional finance law in January 1886 and nothing changed. Why not, and what does that tell you about the 1849 constitution?
  • Elections to the Folketing went on through the provisional years, and Højre lost them one after another. Why did losing elections make no difference to who governed, and why did that turn a quarrel about a clause into a crisis about the state?
  • The treasury was full and the fight was over money. Explain why both of those statements are true.

Counterfactual

What if it had gone otherwise?

  • Suppose the 1849 constitution had contained a mechanism for removing a ministry — an impeachment procedure like Norway's. Does Provisorietiden happen at all?
  • Suppose the settlement of 1894 had written parliamentary government into the text rather than merely ending the emergency. What in the following century looks different?

Contested

Where do historians disagree?

  • This chapter argues that Estrup was defending a constitution as he read it rather than seizing power, and also that he governed for nine years against the elected chamber with a military police force in the villages. Are those two claims compatible? What would settle it?
  • Some historians have written that Denmark could have gone to civil war in the 1880s. What evidence would support that, and what evidence is against it?

Closing Part H

1814 – 1901What this part was about

Part H opened in 1814 with a state that had just lost Norway and written its paper money down to a sixth, and that by 1816 would rule, beside Schleswig, two duchies in the German Confederation, Holstein and Lauenburg. It closes in 1901 with a small, prosperous country of about two and a half million people, governed at last from its elected chamber, that has lost all three.

The line through it is not the wars, though there were two and the second was a catastrophe. It is a long argument about who a Dane is and who gets to decide for him, conducted in four places at once. In Schleswig it was conducted in language: which tongue the church and the school used, and whose survey you believed. In the Rigsdag it was conducted in constitutional law, and turned on a silence in a document that neither side had thought about until they needed it. In the parishes it was conducted in institutions — the dairy, the meeting-house, the mission house, the rifle club — and the answer arrived at there was more radical than anything in Copenhagen: one member, one vote, whatever the size of the herd. And in the tenements and on the emigrant ships it was conducted by the people none of the other three answers included.

The part has an unusual shape for a national history, and it is worth naming. Denmark's two great constitutional gains, in 1849 and 1901, sit on either side of its worst defeat, and neither of them was a consequence of it. The June constitution came out of a European revolution, in the middle of a war Denmark went on to win; the change of system came out of a farming economy that had reorganised itself around butter. In between, 1864 cost the duchies abroad and, in the revision of 1866, the franchise of the upper house at home (chapter 34).

What is carried into Part I is therefore not one thread but four. A parliamentary convention with no text behind it, which will hold for half a century and then be written down. A labour settlement signed in 1899 whose principles still frame the Danish labour market. Two hundred thousand people — or a hundred and seventy thousand — under a government that will conscript their sons in 1914, and a promise of a vote that Denmark itself formally surrendered in 1907 and will nevertheless get in 1920. And a woman who died in 1891 with union banners over her grave, whose association had one item on its programme, and whose cause would have to wait until 1915.
