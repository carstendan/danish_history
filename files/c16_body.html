Margrete I and the making of the union

1375 – 1397 · Margrete 1. · Oluf 2. · Albrecht af Mecklenburg · Erik af Pommern

A girl married off at ten gets her five-year-old son elected king of Denmark, and when he dies at seventeen she takes the government herself under a title nobody had used before. Twenty-two years later three kingdoms are ruled from one pair of hands.

Introduction

WHAT THIS PAGE ANSWERSFour questions

  1. How does a woman come to govern three kingdoms in a century that had no office for her?
  2. What actually happened at Kalmar in 1397 — and why are there two documents that do not quite agree?
  3. Was the union a state, a treaty, or an arrangement between one person and everybody else?
  4. This is the largest territory ever ruled from Denmark. What was in it that a modern map does not show?

01 / NARRATIVEThe succession nobody had settled

Danish terms in this section

valgrige
Elective kingdom. Denmark chose its kings; the choice ran inside the royal family, but it was a choice, and it could be argued about.
håndfæstning
The written conditions a king accepted at his accession. The 1282 charter of chapter 14 is the ancestor; from here on, every accession has one.

Valdemar Atterdag died at Gurre on 24 October 1375, and chapter 15 left the kingdom exactly as he left it: reassembled, solvent enough, and with no agreed heir. His son Christoffer had died in 1363. That left two grandsons, both of them children, both of them the sons of daughters.

The elder claim belonged to Albrecht of Mecklenburg — confusingly, the same name as the king of Sweden, who was his uncle — the son of Valdemar's eldest daughter Ingeborg. By the ordinary reckoning of inheritance he came first. The younger was Oluf, aged five, the son of Valdemar's youngest daughter Margrete and of Håkon 6. of Norway.

Denmark was a valgrige, and that mattered here more than it had mattered for two centuries. There was no rule that settled the case, which meant the case would be settled by whoever could assemble the most support among the men who did the electing: the bishops, the great nobles, and the assemblies. Both grandmothers' families went to work. The Mecklenburg party had seniority and a German alliance. Margrete had something better — she was in Denmark, she was Valdemar's daughter, and she was prepared to buy.

She was also quick. On 7 December 1375, six weeks after her father's death, she is signing herself queen of Norway and Sweden, and Sir Valdemar's daughter and rightful heir. It is a formula with no legal force whatever. It is also on parchment, in circulation, months before anyone else's claim was written down.

At a meeting of the realm at Slagelse on 3 May 1376, Oluf was elected king. The margin was not overwhelming and the support was conditional: he was five, so government went to a regency consisting of his parents and the council, and his election came with written conditions. That document belongs to the series that begins with the charter of 1282 — the håndfæstning that chapter 14 spent a century arguing about. It will recur at every accession in this part: 1396, 1448, 1460, and on into the sixteenth century. The Danish nobility had learned to charge for a crown, and it never unlearned it.

Two things follow. Margrete's authority began as somebody else's — she governed because she was the mother of a child king, which is the oldest and most fragile form of power a medieval woman could have. And the passed-over Mecklenburg claim did not go away. It sat in Germany for a decade, and in 1387 it was still sitting there.

02 / NARRATIVEA daughter, sent away

Danish terms in this section

morgengave
Morning gift: property settled on a bride by her husband after the wedding, held in her own right. Margrete's, in Norway and later in Sweden, became the financial base of everything she did.
enkedronning
Dowager queen. A recognised status with an income and no authority attached — which is what she was supposed to become in 1380.

Chapter 15 ended with her wedding. Born at Søborg in 1353, the youngest of Valdemar's children, she was betrothed at six to Håkon Magnusson of Norway, married him in Copenhagen in 1363 at ten, and was sent to Norway to be brought up in the household of Merete Ulvsdatter — a daughter of Birgitta of Sweden, whose canonisation appears later in this chapter. Oluf was born at Akershus in 1370, when she was seventeen.

None of this was unusual, and all of it was formative in a way the sources show only indirectly. Between ten and twenty-two she lived inside the Norwegian court as a Danish king's daughter, married to a man who was also — briefly, and then not — king of Sweden. She learned three kingdoms from the inside before she was twenty, and learned them as somebody with no authority in any of them, which is a particular education.

She also acquired property. Her morgengave in Norway, and later a settlement in Sweden, gave her estates and revenues in her own name, and she kept adding to them. This is not incidental. Everything she did for the next forty years — buying support, redeeming castles, financing troops — ran on money she controlled personally rather than on a royal treasury, and when the union was finally written down in 1397 the document contains a paragraph guaranteeing that she keeps all of it, with full royal right, for as long as she lives.

Håkon died in 1380. Oluf, aged ten, became king of Norway as well as Denmark, and Margrete became what the age had a word for: enkedronning, a dowager, a woman with an income and a title and nothing to do. She did not become it. She simply went on governing in her son's name in both kingdoms, and by the middle of the 1380s she was doing it well enough that the men around her had stopped asking on whose behalf.

One piece of that work matters for the rest of the series. In 1386 the county of Holstein's claim on the duchy of Schleswig was settled by granting it: Gerhard 6. of Holstein was enfeoffed with Sønderjylland as a Danish fief, in return for homage and peace. Chapter 14's Constitutio Valdemariana of 1326 had tried to keep the duchy and the kingdom in separate hands forever; this was the other way of managing the same problem, and it bought two decades of quiet on the southern border at the price of admitting the Holstein counts into it permanently. That bill is presented in chapter 19.

03 / NARRATIVENorway, and what came with it

Danish terms in this section

skattland
Tributary land: a territory under the Norwegian crown that was not part of the kingdom of Norway proper. Iceland, the Faroes, Greenland, Orkney and Shetland were all skattlande.
Nordatlanten
The North Atlantic, as a Danish political category. It enters Danish history here, through Norway, and does not leave until 1944 and 1948.

When Oluf inherited Norway in 1380, he inherited more than Norway. The Norwegian crown carried a set of lands out in the Atlantic that had been attached to it for a century and a half: Iceland and the Faroes, the Norse settlements in Greenland, and Orkney and Shetland off the Scottish coast.

They paid, they received a governor and a bishop, and they were governed as lightly as the distance required. Iceland kept its own law and its own assembly and saw a royal official rarely. Greenland's Eastern Settlement was still inhabited in the 1390s, though the Western Settlement had been abandoned and the ships were few; the last written record of an event there, a wedding at Hvalsey church, dates from 1408. Orkney and Shetland stayed Norwegian until 1468, when they were pledged to Scotland for a dowry that was never paid — chapter 19's story.

This is the moment the Atlantic enters Danish history, and it enters sideways: not by conquest, not by settlement, but because a boy who was king of Denmark also became king of Norway, and Norway had been collecting islands since the Viking Age of chapters 8 to 11. For the next four hundred years the Danish crown's reach will be a European fact and an Atlantic one at the same time, and most maps of Denmark will show only the European half. The map at section 08 shows both.

What Norway itself was by 1380 shapes everything that follows. It had been hit harder by the plague than Denmark, and its native aristocracy had thinned to the point where the kingdom could no longer staff its own government from its own families; its trade ran through the German quarter at Bergen. In 1380 the arrangement with Denmark was a personal union of equals in principle. Within thirty years it was not, and nobody legislated the change: one partner had men and money to spare and the other did not.

04 / NARRATIVEFalsterbo, 3 August 1387

Danish terms in this section

fuldmægtig frue og husbond
Plenipotentiary lady and husband. The title Margrete was hailed by in 1387, and the strangest formula in Danish constitutional history: husbond is 'husband' in the older sense of the head of a household, the person who runs the estate.
formynder
Guardian, in the legal sense — the person who acts for someone who cannot act for themselves. In 1387 the person who could not act was the realm itself.
landsting
The provincial assembly. Chapter 12's institution, still the place where a Danish ruler was acclaimed.

Oluf was declared of age in 1385 and hailed in Norway and at the Danish assemblies. He governed for two years, under what was certainly his mother's supervision, and on 3 August 1387 he died suddenly at Falsterbo castle in Skåne. He was not quite seventeen. The cause is unknown; a modern guess is cardiac, but it is a guess, and the honest statement is that a healthy young man died without warning in the middle of the herring season, in the busiest commercial place in northern Europe, in front of witnesses from a dozen countries.

That last detail is why the rumours were containable. It is also why, fifteen years later, they were not.

Her position collapsed with him. She had governed as the mother of the king; there was now no king, and the Mecklenburg claim was still sitting in Germany. What she did next was done in a week.

On about 10 August, at the landsting in Lund, she was taken and acclaimed as fuldmægtig frue og husbond og det ganske rige Danmarks formynder — plenipotentiary lady and husband, and guardian of the whole realm of Denmark. Norway followed at the beginning of 1388, and there the grant was explicitly for life.

It is not 'queen', which she never was in Denmark, and not 'regent', which would have implied a principal she acted for. She was being made the person in charge, in the only vocabulary available.

How this looked at the time is genuinely disputed. Some Danish historians have read it as the natural consequence of a crisis in which no other candidate was ready; others have called it close to a coup, carried out in the days before the opposition could organise, at the assembly furthest from Jutland and closest to where she was standing. Both readings fit the evidence, which is thin, formal and produced by the winning side. What is not in doubt is the speed.

05 / NARRATIVEDalaborg and Åsle

Danish terms in this section

unionsadel
The union nobility: families holding land and office in more than one of the three kingdoms. They were the union's real constituency, and its beneficiaries.
fogedstyre
Bailiff government — rule through appointed officers rather than through local magnates. The Swedish complaint against Albrecht was that his were German.

Sweden had a king already: Albrecht of Mecklenburg, elected in 1364 after the Swedes drove out Margrete's father-in-law and her husband, and by 1388 he had spent twenty-four years making himself impossible. The grievance was specific rather than nationalist. He governed through fogedstyre, and the men he appointed were largely German, so the Swedish magnates were paying for a government that did not employ them. In 1387 the richest man in Sweden, the drost Bo Jonsson Grip, died leaving a will that made his executors — Swedish magnates all — the effective holders of most of the kingdom's castles. Albrecht moved to overturn it, and the executors went looking for another king.

They found a queen. Travelling home from Norway in 1388, Margrete met a delegation of them, and at Dalaborg on the shore of Lake Vänern, on Palm Sunday 1388, they agreed terms: she would be Sweden's fuldmægtige frue og rette husbond — the same formula a third time — for life, and would help them against Albrecht; they would keep what Bo Jonsson's will had given them, and would accept a king of her nominating in due course.

That is not a conquest and not really a dynastic claim. It is a contract, between a woman with money and an army and a group of noblemen who wanted a particular kind of government. It is the union's real foundation, and it explains how the union later behaved: strongest when the unionsadel were getting what they wanted, and broken when they were not.

Albrecht raised a German army and came north. On 24 February 1389 the two forces met at Åsle, near Falköping in Västergötland. Margrete's army — Danish, Swedish and Norwegian, commanded for her by men who had made careers in her service — destroyed his. Albrecht was captured on the field, and so was his son Erik. Both were taken to Denmark, and both stayed there.

In October 1389 she was formally installed as regent of Sweden. The Swedish crown had been won in one afternoon by a woman who held no office in any of the three kingdoms that a lawyer of the period would have recognised.

Meanwhile in Europe

1389 produced two battles that decided the shape of a region for centuries, and only one of them is remembered outside it. On 15 June, eight months before Åsle, a Serbian and Bosnian army met the Ottoman sultan Murad at Kosovo Polje. Both commanders died; the battle itself was probably a draw or near enough; and within two generations the Balkans were Ottoman and the battle had become the central story a nation told about itself. Åsle, fought on 24 February in a Swedish field, killed almost nobody of note and created the largest state in Europe.

Elsewhere: the Hundred Years' War was in one of its truces, England had just seen Richard 2. resume personal rule after the Merciless Parliament, and the Church had been split between two popes for eleven years with thirty more to run. In 1396 a crusading army from most of western Christendom was destroyed at Nicopolis on the Danube, which ended the medieval crusade as a serious military proposition. Denmark, Norway and Sweden sent nobody.

Vignette · Bo Jonsson Grip, Stockholm, August 1386

He is the richest man who has ever lived in Sweden, and he is dying. Nobody is sure how old he is. He began as a minor nobleman, made himself indispensable to Albrecht of Mecklenburg, took the office of drots, and then took, in pledge, mortgage or outright purchase, something close to a third of the kingdom — castles, districts, most of Finland.

What he does about it is the interesting part. His will names executors, and the executors are Swedish magnates, and it puts the whole vast holding in their hands to administer. It is, in effect, an instruction that the property is not to revert to the crown.

Albrecht moves to overturn the will within months, which is the reasonable act of a king who has noticed that a third of his kingdom has been privatised. It is also the act that loses him the kingdom, because the executors now need a different ruler, and eighteen months later they are at Dalaborg negotiating with a Danish woman who has no legal claim to Sweden whatever.

The Kalmar Union has many causes. One of them is a dead man's estate plan.

Bo Jonsson Grip, drots of Sweden · Stockholm · August 1386

Meanwhile in Europe

The 1370s and 1380s were a bad stretch almost everywhere. In 1378 a disputed papal election produced two popes, at Rome and at Avignon, each with cardinals, courts and revenues, and the schism ran for thirty-nine years. In 1381 the English peasants marched on London, killed the archbishop of Canterbury, and were dispersed by a fourteen-year-old king; in 1382 the Flemish towns rose and were crushed at Roosebeke. The Hundred Years' War was in one of its long truces, and both crowns were in the hands of minors or madmen for much of the period.

The common factor was demographic. A third of Europe had died in the plague of the previous generation, wages had risen, rents had fallen, and everywhere the people who lived on rents were using law and force to claw back what the labour market had taken from them. Denmark's version of that argument is in section 10 of chapter 17 — quieter than England's, and won by the same side.

06 / NARRATIVEA king in a Skåne castle

Danish terms in this section

fetaljebrødre
The Victual Brothers: privateers licensed by Mecklenburg to supply Stockholm and harry Margrete's shipping. The name comes from fetalje, victuals.
pantsætning
Pledging: handing over a castle or district as security for a debt, the creditor drawing its revenues until redemption. Chapter 14's mechanism, still running.

Winning the battle was the easy part. Albrecht's supporters still held Stockholm, which was a German town in a Swedish kingdom — council and burgher class largely Hanseatic — with no intention of surrendering to a Danish queen. Mecklenburg licensed privateers to supply it, and the fetaljebrødre spent a decade making the Baltic unusable and seizing Gotland on the way. The war won in 1389 took nine more years to finish.

It was ended by negotiation, not by force. At Lindholm in 1395 a settlement was reached under Hanseatic mediation: Albrecht was released, having sworn to renounce the Swedish throne or return to captivity; Stockholm was handed to the mediating towns to hold for three years; and Margrete finally took the city in 1398. Gotland stayed lost. The Teutonic Order took the island in 1398 to clear out the privateers, and it was not handed over until 1408, and then only for a large sum.

One detail at Lindholm is easy to skip and should not be. The magnates of all three kingdoms appeared there as a single body, acting together, for the first time. The union had a functioning political class before it had a document.

Vignette · Albrecht at Lindholmen

Lindholmen is a castle in a bog in south-western Skåne, twenty kilometres inland from the Sound, built on an artificial island reached by a causeway. In February 1389 it acquires two new residents: Albrecht of Mecklenburg, king of Sweden for twenty-five years, and his son Erik.

They are there for six years. He is not in a dungeon — the fifteenth century housed captive kings according to their rank — but he is not going anywhere either, and the terms keep moving. What Margrete wants is not a ransom. She wants Stockholm, and she wants him to stop being king of Sweden, and she is willing to keep him in a Skåne bog until his supporters concede both.

He is let out in 1395 under the Lindholm terms: a large sum within three years, or Stockholm handed over instead, or back to prison. The money is not paid. Stockholm is handed over. He goes home to Mecklenburg and lives until 1412, the same year as Margrete, having outlived his son, who died in captivity here in 1397.

Albrecht 3. of Mecklenburg, king of Sweden · Lindholmen castle, Skåne · 1389–1395

Meanwhile the succession had to be arranged, because the whole structure rested on a woman of thirty-six with no living children. In 1389 she brought in her great-nephew: Bogislav, born about 1382, son of a Pomeranian duke and of Maria, who was the daughter of Margrete's sister Ingeborg. He was renamed Erik — a name that appeared in all three royal lines and in none of them meant German — and he was presented to the Nordic magnates at Helsingborg in the summer of 1389 and hailed in Norway that autumn as hereditary king. He was seven.

She had chosen a child who was related to her, dependent on her, and foreign enough to belong to nobody. She then kept him a child, constitutionally speaking, for as long as she possibly could.

07 / NARRATIVEViborg, January 1396

Danish terms in this section

krongods
Crown land. The estates whose revenues paid for government; the more of it was pledged away, the weaker the crown.
regnskabslen
An accounting fief: a district whose holder administers it for the crown and renders accounts, rather than drawing its income as his own. The difference between this and a pledged district is the difference between an official and a creditor.
Dronning Margretes Fred
'Queen Margrete's Peace' — the undertaking given at the assemblies in 1396 that the king's law would be kept.

Erik was about fourteen in January 1396 when he was finally elected king of Denmark at the Viborg assembly, and hailed in Sweden that summer. Both elections were accompanied by legislation, and the Danish ordinance of 24 January 1396 is the single best document we have for what her government actually was.

It orders, among other things, that all krongods alienated since Valdemar Atterdag left Denmark for Germany in 1368 shall return to the crown at once; that land the crown has taken wrongly shall likewise go back to whoever has the better right; and that private castle-building shall stop, on the stated ground that very little justice has come out of the fortresses recently built. The Swedish ordinance that followed in the summer went further and ordered castles built in Albrecht's time pulled down.

Read that with chapter 14 in mind, where the whole century was crown land leaking into private hands through pantsætning and kings left with nothing to govern with. Here is a government reversing twenty-eight years of it in a single clause, and enforcing it. The Fiskbæk property, confiscated in 1406, is one of the few Danish estates whose rents around 1400 we can read at all — because the confiscation generated a record.

The other half of the method was administrative. Where her predecessors had pledged districts to creditors, Margrete increasingly placed them with officers who accounted for them — the regnskabslen that becomes the backbone of Danish provincial government for the next two centuries. She appointed her own men, kept them dependent, and moved them. The councils of the three kingdoms met when she called them and were not consulted much in between. It was, by the standards of the period, an unusually centralised and unusually personal government, and the record of it is a mountain of letters in her own chancery's hand.

It was not popular with the people it was taken from. It was accepted because she paired it with a commitment: the assemblies of 1396 recorded Dronning Margretes Fred, an undertaking that the law would be kept and private violence punished. A strong crown that enforces the peace is a bargain most fourteenth-century landholders would take, and did.

Vignette · a boy at Helsingborg, summer 1389

He is seven years old, he has been brought from Pomerania, and he is being shown to people. His name until recently was Bogislav. It is now Erik, which is a name that appears in all three royal lines and in none of them sounds German.

His qualifications are exact. He is Margrete's great-nephew — grandson of her sister Ingeborg — so the blood claim is real but thin. He is foreign enough to be nobody's man in any of the three kingdoms. And he is seven, which means that whatever is agreed now can be agreed by somebody else on his behalf for a long time.

Norway hails him as hereditary king that autumn. Denmark elects him in January 1396, Sweden that summer, and he is crowned at Kalmar in June 1397 at about fifteen. He is then kept a junior partner for another fifteen years, until Margrete dies in 1412 and he is thirty.

The arrangement worked exactly as intended, which is not the same as saying it worked well. The man who came out the other end of it had spent his entire life waiting, had never been allowed to govern anything, and got three kingdoms at thirty with no practice. Chapter 18 is about what he did with them.

Erik of Pomerania, born Bogislav, aged seven · Helsingborg · summer 1389

08 / NARRATIVEKalmar, 17 June 1397

Danish terms in this section

kroningsbrevet
The coronation document: the record that Erik had been crowned over all three kingdoms, with an oath of fidelity and a full discharge for Margrete.
unionsbrevet
The union letter: the constitutional document, setting out how the three kingdoms were to be governed together. Its status has been argued about for a hundred and thirty years.
vidisse
An attested copy of a document, made and certified later. Erik had one made of the union letter in 1425, which turns out to matter.

In the summer of 1397 the councils of the three kingdoms assembled at Kalmar, on the Swedish coast opposite Öland. The meeting must have run at least a month. On Trinity Sunday, 17 June, Erik was crowned king of Denmark, Sweden and Norway by the archbishops of Lund and Uppsala. The absence of Norwegian bishops is a clue: he had already been crowned in Norway some years earlier, and Norway's part in the meeting was, from the start, the smallest.

Two documents came out of it, and they are not the same kind of thing.

The kroningsbrevet records the crowning. The signatories declare their fidelity to King Erik and give Margrete a complete discharge for everything she has done. It imposes no particular obligation on the king beyond a general phrase that he shall do by them as he ought.

The unionsbrevet is the constitution. Three kingdoms, one king and never again more than one; on a king's death the successor chosen by all three together; each kingdom keeping its own law and officers, with no law carried from one into another; one policy abroad; an outlaw in one an outlaw in all; and war on one war on all, with the duty to serve beyond one's own border stated explicitly, because that was exactly what men would otherwise refuse. Then, at the end, a long paragraph guaranteeing that Margrete keeps everything she holds — her father's and her son's gifts, her Norwegian and Swedish morning-gifts, her acquisitions — with full royal right, for life.

Now the difficulty. The union letter itself specifies that properly sealed copies on parchment, two from each kingdom, should be drawn up and exchanged. That never happened, and we can say so with some confidence. What survives is a single document written on paper, with corrections in the text, looking for all the world like a draft — and yet ten or so of the men named at the end have hung their seals on it, and while the union was working it was treated as legally valid. Its date is not certain either: it is dated on St Margaret's day, which gives either 13 or 20 July, a month after the crowning.

What was actually agreed at Kalmar is therefore one of the genuinely open questions in Nordic history, and the argument has moved more than once.

Kristian Erslev, whose 1892 study is still the point of departure, treated the union letter as the real constitutional settlement. In 1934 the Swedish historian Erik Lönnroth proposed the reading that dominated the next fifty years: that the two documents represent two rival programmes, the coronation document expressing the monarchy's view and the union letter the council aristocracy's, and that the crown won — the coronation document took effect, the union letter did not. Aksel E. Christensen read the union letter instead as a provisional draft left over from hard bargaining. More recent Danish work has turned the older reading round: on this view the letter is drafted loosely enough to leave the king a great deal of room, and the evidence offered is that Erik himself had an attested copy, a vidisse, made of it on 11 September 1425 — which is not what a king does with a document that ties his hands.

The disagreement is not really about what the paper says. It is about whether the union was a constitution or an arrangement. The letter describes institutions; the reality for the next fifteen years was one woman with money, castles and appointments in three kingdoms. The paperwork of 1397 was drawn up by people who knew it would be tested only after she was dead.

There is one more thing to say about the union letter, and it is physical rather than constitutional.

Both documents are in the Danish National Archives and you can see the difference across a room. The coronation document is a large parchment, and hanging from it on cords are sixty-seven seals, one for each of the men who issued it — the councils of three kingdoms, in wax, in order. It is what a binding act was supposed to look like in 1397, and it has survived looking like one.

The union letter is a sheet of paper. Seventeen men are named in it. Under the text there are the traces of ten impressed seals, and they are now so faint that it cannot be established with certainty whose they were — whether they are ten of the seventeen, and if so which ten. The text itself says that the issuers hang their seals below the letter, which is the formula for a parchment document, and the seals are pressed into paper.

So the constitution of the largest state in Europe survives as a corrected draft, in a form its own wording does not describe, authenticated by ten men who can no longer be identified. Whatever was agreed at Kalmar in the summer of 1397, the object that records it is a piece of paper that has been quietly failing to be a treaty for six hundred years.

Myth-check

"Margrete 1. was Denmark's first queen."
She was never queen of Denmark. Queen consort of Norway by marriage, yes, and she used a Swedish queen's title from her husband's brief reign there — but in Denmark she was never crowned and never claimed the word. What she was hailed as, three times in three kingdoms, was fuldmægtig frue og husbond. The real story is stranger than the myth: not a woman who became king, but a government that invented a word rather than admit what it was doing.
"The Kalmar Union united Scandinavia into one country."
The union letter says the opposite in as many words: each kingdom keeps its own law, its own officers and its own council, and no law is to be carried from one into another. Shared were the king, foreign policy and the duty to defend each other. Its central weakness was written in from the start — it worked when one person could hold all three, and there was no machinery for when the three disagreed about who that should be.
"There is a signed and sealed founding document of the Kalmar Union."
There are two documents and they are not the same kind of object. The coronation record is parchment with sixty-seven seals hanging from it. The union letter — the constitutional one — is paper, with corrections in the text, naming seventeen men and carrying the traces of ten impressed seals so faint that it cannot be established which ten. It specifies that six sealed parchments be exchanged between the kingdoms; they never were. It was treated as legally valid anyway while the union worked.
"Margrete poisoned her son to keep power."
A modern romantic addition with nothing behind it. Oluf died in front of an international commercial crowd at Falsterbo, and his mother's position was very much weaker with him dead than alive — she had to improvise a title in a week to survive it. What people did believe at the time is in chapter 17, where a man turns up in Prussia claiming to be him.

Summary

IF YOU REMEMBER FIVE THINGSThe page in five

  1. Margrete governed Denmark from 1376 as the mother of a child king, and when that king died at seventeen in 1387 she was acclaimed within a week under a formula nobody had used before — fuldmægtig frue og husbond, plenipotentiary lady and husband. She was never queen of Denmark.

  2. Sweden came by contract, not conquest: the magnates in revolt against Albrecht of Mecklenburg accepted her at Dalaborg in 1388 — a revolt whose immediate cause was a dead man's will — and her army destroyed his at Åsle in February 1389 and took him prisoner. Stockholm held out until 1398.

  3. The method of government was crown land recovered by decree — everything alienated since 1368, reclaimed in the ordinance of January 1396 — plus a ban on private castles, districts run by accounting officers instead of creditors, and appointments kept firmly in her own hands.

  4. Her successor was chosen for exactly the qualities that made him harmless: a seven-year-old great-nephew from Pomerania, renamed Erik, related to her, dependent on her, and nobody's man in any of the three kingdoms.

  5. At Kalmar in 1397 Erik was crowned over all three kingdoms. Two documents survive — a parchment coronation record with sixty-seven seals, and a paper union letter, corrected, with ten faint ones, never exchanged in the sealed form it prescribes. What it established has been argued over since Erslev in 1892.

Questions & discussion

FOUR KINDSWork the material

Recall

Did the facts land?

  • Who were the two candidates in 1375, and why did the elder one lose?
  • What title was Margrete given at Lund in 1387, and what does husbond mean?
  • What was agreed at Dalaborg in 1388, and what happened at Åsle on 24 February 1389?
  • Name three things the ordinance of January 1396 ordered.
  • What are the two Kalmar documents called, and how do they differ as physical objects?

Causal

Why did that follow from this?

  • Why did Margrete's power become more secure after her son died, when it had rested entirely on being his mother?
  • Why did the Swedish magnates prefer a foreign ruler with no legal claim to a crowned king they already had?
  • Why did recovering crown land do more for the government's strength than raising taxes would have?
  • Why choose a seven-year-old foreigner as heir when there were adult claimants with better blood?

Counterfactual

What if it had gone otherwise?

  • If Oluf had lived to forty, would there have been a union at all — and would it have been built on the same terms?
  • Suppose the sealed parchments of 1397 had been drawn up and exchanged as the union letter required. Does the union last longer, or does it break earlier and more formally?
  • If Bo Jonsson Grip had left his estates to the Swedish crown instead of to executors, is there a Dalaborg?

Contested

Where the argument still is

  • What did the union letter actually establish? Erslev treated it as the settlement; Lönnroth in 1934 read the two documents as rival programmes with the crown's winning; Aksel E. Christensen read the letter as a bargaining draft; recent Danish work argues it favours the king, citing Erik's own attested copy of 1425. The document is paper, corrected, and partly sealed, and every reading has to explain that.
  • Was 1387 a legitimate settlement or a coup? The election at Lund happened within a week, at the assembly furthest from the Mecklenburg interest, and the record was written by the winners.
  • Did Margrete intend a permanent union or a personal one? The letter provides for perpetual joint election — and also guarantees her own holdings for life in a paragraph that reads like the real point of the exercise.
  • How much did the union's Danish dominance owe to design and how much to demography? Norway had lost its aristocracy to plague; Sweden had a functioning one and kept far more autonomy.
