1864

1863 – 1864 · Christian 9. · D.G. Monrad · C.J. de Meza · Otto von Bismarck · Niels Kjeldsen · J.B.S. Estrup

A king three days on the throne signs a constitution he knows breaks the promise made beside the treaty that made him king. Within a year Denmark has lost the duchies, the army that abandoned an indefensible rampart has been vilified for saving itself, and the bronze lion over the graves at Flensburg has been taken down. The bill for the second loss is paid at home, in 1866, by Danes.

Introduction

WHAT THIS PAGE ANSWERSFive questions

  1. Christian 9. owed his throne to the Treaty of London and his first act broke the undertaking that came with it. Why did he sign, and what did he think would happen?
  2. In 1848 Russia made Prussia take its troops out of Jutland. In 1864 nobody did. What changed between those two wars — and whose doing was it?
  3. The general who abandoned the Dannevirke was destroyed for it and had been following his written orders. Who needed him blamed?
  4. Denmark won the fleet action off Heligoland in May 1864. Why did it not matter?
  5. Denmark lost territory to Prussia and Austria in October 1864 and lost the franchise of its upper house in July 1866. Which loss shaped the next forty years?

01 / NARRATIVEA king three days on the throne

Danish terms in this section

Novemberforfatningen
the November Constitution of 18 November 1863, a common constitution for the kingdom and Schleswig with Holstein left outside. The direct cause of the war.

On 13 November 1863 the Rigsråd passed a new constitution for the kingdom and Schleswig together, and left Holstein out. Two days later Frederik 7. died without signing it, at Glücksburg Castle in the duchy — the last king of the Oldenburg line dying in the house that gave its name to the dynasty about to replace him.

His successor was proclaimed from the balcony of Christiansborg the next day by the prime minister, C.C. Hall. Christian 9. was forty-five, born at Gottorp, raised in the borderland, a conservative believer in the composite state who spoke Danish with a German accent.

He was not the only claimant to move. On 16 November, the day after Frederik 7. died, Prince Frederik of Augustenborg, from his headquarters at Gotha, proclaimed himself Friedrich VIII, duke of Schleswig and Holstein. His family had renounced in 1852 and had been paid to do it. In the duchies he was received as the rightful heir, and at the end of December he came to Kiel and set up his court there.

It is worth being clear about which body had passed the thing the new king was being asked to sign. It was not the Rigsdag of 1849 but the Rigsråd, the joint assembly created by the composite-state constitution of 1855 for kingdom and duchies alike, and since 1858 sitting for the kingdom and Schleswig only — the institution designed to hold the monarchy together, voting to take it apart.

Vignette · Christian 9., Copenhagen, 18 November 1863

He has been king for three days. In front of him is a constitution his predecessor did not sign, which joins Schleswig to Denmark and leaves Holstein outside — and he knows, better than almost anyone in the country, that Denmark undertook in 1851 and 1852 never to do this. He knows it because the same settlement is why he is king at all.

He takes two days to decide. The delay is read in Copenhagen as sympathy for the composite state, which it is, and the national liberal press is waiting. There are demonstrations. There are calls for him to go. His Danishness is questioned in print by people who had not questioned it a week earlier.

On the 18th he signs, reluctantly and with grave misgivings. After the defeat he will consider abdicating. He will reign for forty-two more years and outlive every politician who pressed him.

02 / NARRATIVEWhy the powers did not come

Danish terms in this section

forbundseksekution
federal execution: the German Confederation enforcing its own law against a member state, carried out in Holstein and Lauenburg in December 1863. Legally quite different from the invasion of Schleswig that followed, which is why Prussia and Austria had to step outside the Confederation to do it.

The Danish expectation in the winter of 1863 was that Denmark would not be left alone. It was left alone, and the reasons are worth setting out plainly, because the Danish account of 1864 has generally preferred betrayal to the alternative.

The alternative is that Denmark had put itself in the wrong. The composite-state constitution of 1855 had been rejected in Holstein and declared invalid there and in Lauenburg by the German Confederation in 1858, and in November of that year Copenhagen suspended it for both, which left the monarchy with a common constitution for only part of itself. The November Constitution was Copenhagen's answer, and it was a step towards the incorporation of Schleswig that Denmark had promised in the notes of 1851–52 not to make. Every foreign ministry in Europe could read that, and did.

So the German Confederation moved first and legally, executing federal law in Holstein and Lauenburg in December 1863. Then Prussia and Austria stepped outside the Confederation and moved on their own account, crossing the Eider into Schleswig — which was not federal territory — on 1 February 1864. What began as an enforcement action became a war of conquest between those two dates, and the transition was Bismarck's work. He had no interest in Friedrich VIII or in an independent Schleswig-Holstein. He wanted the duchies for Prussia, and a Danish breach of its undertakings gave him a way to take them with Austria's signature on it.

Britain had encouraged Denmark and would not act without France. France wanted compensation on the Rhine and was not offered any. Russia, whose Tsar had been a guarantor in 1852 and whose interest in 1848 had been decisive, had no interest now in propping up a state that had broken the settlement Russia had imposed. Sweden-Norway talked about Scandinavian brotherhood and sent nobody.

The lesson Denmark drew from 1848 to 1850 was that the balance of Europe would always protect it. That was true while Denmark kept the terms the balance was built on. It stopped being true in November 1863, and nobody in Copenhagen appears to have thought the two facts were connected.

03 / NARRATIVEDannevirke, 5–6 February

Danish terms in this section

Dannevirke
the earthwork across the neck of Jutland, begun in the eighth century (chapter 7) and the frontier of chapter 12. In 1864 a line that needed more men than the army holding it had.

The army Denmark sent south in the winter of 1863–64 was sent to a rampart. The Dannevirke had held the neck of Jutland on and off for a thousand years, and in the Danish imagination it was a wall. In fact it was a badly maintained line of earthworks that needed at least fifty thousand men and was held by fewer than forty thousand, with a frozen Schlei on one flank that an enemy could walk across.

On 2 February the fighting at Missunde showed what that meant: the position could be turned. On the 4th the king and the war minister left. That evening the commanding general, Christian de Meza, held a council of war.

The council had a document in front of it. The War Ministry's own instruction of 13 January had told de Meza that what mattered was still to possess, in the coming spring, "a capable and battle-ready army". The council concluded that the army could only remain capable and battle-ready by withdrawing to the flank positions at Dybbøl and Fredericia. It voted to go.

The retreat began on the 5th. The fortress artillery was left in the works so the enemy would see guns where there were no longer men; instructions went out unit by unit rather than as a single plan, to keep it quiet; the last outposts left the forward ground between eleven and midnight. It was snowing, the roads were sheet ice, and the men had been sleeping outdoors in it for weeks. The Prussians and Austrians did not notice until the early morning of the 6th that the wall in front of them was empty.

That was precisely what the enemy least wanted. Wrangel had instructions from Bismarck and the war minister von Roon to destroy the Danish army before it could reach its flank positions, and the withdrawal denied him it. Austrian cavalry caught the rearguard south of Flensburg, and at Sankelmark on the 6th there was a short, savage fight with bayonets against an Austrian brigade: 53 Danish dead and 95 Austrian. The army got away.

Shortly after one in the morning on the 6th, in Flensburg, de Meza was handed a telegram from the war minister in Copenhagen. I do not acknowledge that the Commanding General of the Army has complied with the Instruction, and I reserve investigation accordingly.

He had complied with it. That is what the instruction said and what the council minuted. It made no difference. The public had been told nothing and expected a battle at the Dannevirke; what it got was a retreat in the dark, and Copenhagen smelled treason. Crowds demanded the high command's dismissal, and a good many people believed the king had ordered the retreat himself, which he had not. The war minister called de Meza to Copenhagen on 6 February, and at the end of February, with Monrad behind him, the king at last agreed to remove him from the high command. He took full responsibility, never recovered his position, and died the following year, withdrawn from the world — a man who had begun as a teacher of history and geography at the officers' school, and later of German and French, and was known for his fastidious and unusual dress.

The withdrawal from the Dannevirke was the best decision anyone made in the war.

Meanwhile · Geneva, 22 August 1864.

Twelve states signed the first Geneva Convention, providing that the wounded should be collected and cared for regardless of which side they belonged to, and that those doing it should be protected. It was signed while the Danish war was still being settled, and it was signed because of a battle — Solferino, 1859 — that had looked exactly like the ground below Dybbøl Mill on 18 April: several thousand wounded men lying in the open with nobody whose job it was to fetch them. Denmark was one of the twelve; so was Prussia.

04 / NARRATIVEDybbøl

Danish terms in this section

skanse
a redoubt: an enclosed earthwork for guns and men. Ten of them made the Dybbøl position, built 1861–62 in a three-kilometre arc.
bagladegevær
breech-loading rifle, loaded lying down. The Prussian needle gun against the Danish muzzle-loader is the standard explanation for 1864 and it explains less than it is asked to: see the myth-check.

The war reached Jutland in the middle of February, when Prussian troops rode into Kolding, and it reached it as small groups of horsemen on winter roads.

Vignette · Niels Kjeldsen, south of Højen, 28 February 1864

He is twenty-four, a farmer's son from Bæksgårde in Give parish, and he has been a soldier for eight months. He did his conscript service from June 1863 with the 6th Dragoon Regiment's 4th squadron at Itzehoe in Holstein — a Danish peasant garrisoned in the German duchy he is now defending against Germans.

On a Sunday morning six men ride out from Højen kro, south of Vejle, along the Kolding road. Nine Prussian hussars surprise them. Two of his companions are caught and surrender. Kjeldsen is overtaken, refuses quarter, and defends himself with his sabre while still riding. One of the pursuing officers shoots him through the head. The skirmish attracts no particular notice at the time.

In 1881 it does. An article in Vort Forsvar, in the middle of the constitutional struggle, printed a different version: he had stood and faced them, beaten three Prussians with cold steel, and been shot from behind by a coward whose own regiment then reprimanded him. In 1901 Frants Henningsen painted that scene for the museum at Frederiksborg. In 1902 Karl Larsen took it apart, using five contemporary accounts and ten surviving eyewitnesses, and the army protested; the painting came down from Frederiksborg in the summer of 1913. It hangs in the dragoon barracks at Holstebro now.

What survives every version: he had refused to surrender, which is what he was supposed to do, and a Prussian shot him from behind. In the one account that is what happens to a man riding away; in the other it is what a coward does. The Prussian who killed him was, by one account, promoted at once.

Niels Kjeldsen, dragoon, 6th Dragoon Regiment · Blåkær skov, south of Højen · 28 February 1864 · [n]

The army meanwhile sat down at Dybbøl. The position was a half-moon of ten small earth redoubts running three kilometres from Vemmingbund to Als Sund, built by Danish engineers in 1861 and 1862, with concrete powder magazines and wooden blockhouses. It had been designed as an offensive flank position, from which an army could threaten the supply lines of an enemy pushing north into Jutland. It had not been designed to be besieged, and it was not finished. The first troops arrived on 7 February and spent the month digging.

What happened next was an engineering operation, not a battle. On 15 March the Prussians emplaced rifled breech-loading guns on Broager Land, across the water to the south, and from the twin towers of Broager church their staff watched the fall of shot and corrected it. Danish artillery could not reach them. From 2 April Sønderborg was shelled and much of the town was reduced to rubble. On 8 April, by the count of Sønderborg's mayor, the Broager batteries alone fired 1,150 rounds, perhaps two or three hundred of them into the town. The Prussians dug parallel trenches, each closer than the last, until the forward one was three hundred metres from the Danish line.

From 15 March to 18 April the men in the redoubts were shelled without being able to reply. They dug at night, repaired what had been knocked down in the day, and were shelled again. The one thing that could answer Broager was the ironclad Rolf Krake, bought in Glasgow the year before — four heavy guns in two revolving armoured turrets, sail as a reserve if the steam failed, and the beginning of a different kind of navy. She came into Vemmingbund and fired on the Prussian flank and was driven off, and she could not be everywhere.

On 18 April the bombardment began at four in the morning and ran for six hours. About eight thousand shells fell. At ten the storm troops went in, and the central and southern redoubts were gone in half an hour. The Danish 8th Brigade, some three thousand men, counter-attacked under Colonel Scharffenberg, fought its way forward to Dybbøl Mill and Langdamgård, and was thrown back with the loss of 1,399 men, dead, wounded and taken — about forty-five per cent of its strength. What was left of the army crossed to Als.

How many Danes died there is not known, and the published figures do not agree, chiefly because they do not count the same thing. The National Museum gives 700 dead, more than 500 wounded and more than 3,500 taken prisoner. The historical centre at Dybbøl Banke and Grænseforeningen give 1,669 dead and wounded together, and 3,131 unwounded prisoners and deserters. The state's guide to Dybbøl Banke gives about 1,800 killed, wounded or missing, and about 3,000 prisoners. danmarkshistorien gives nearly five thousand men lost in all. On the Prussian side the figure is steadier: 1,201 dead and wounded, or about 1,200. This page reports the figures and does not average them.

05 / NARRATIVEThe London Conference

Danish terms in this section

Ejderpolitik
glossed in chapter 32; the position Denmark was still holding in London after losing the battle that made it impossible.

A conference met in London on 25 April, a week after Dybbøl fell, and a ceasefire took effect on 12 May. Britain, France, Russia, Prussia, Austria, Sweden-Norway and the German Confederation sat down with Denmark to decide what would become of Schleswig.

Three days before the ceasefire, Denmark won the bloodier of the war's two fleet actions, and it is worth putting beside the conference table because of how little it bought.

Denmark had held the sea from the start. The navy was the one arm of the Danish service that was not outclassed. From 26 February it blockaded the ports of Schleswig and Holstein, and from March the Prussian Baltic ports as well, which hurt. On 9 May a squadron of three ships under Edouard Suenson — the screw frigates Niels Juel and Jylland and the corvette Hejmdal — met an Austrian-Prussian force under Wilhelm von Tegetthoff about ten sea miles south-east of Heligoland, then a British island and neutral. The Prussian gunboats were too slow to engage. The two Austrian frigates did, the Danish gunnery set the flagship Schwarzenberg's foretop on fire, and Tegetthoff ran for neutral British waters under the island, where Suenson could not follow.

Jylland fired six hundred and eleven rounds that afternoon and took eighteen direct hits. Fourteen Danish sailors were killed and fifty-four wounded, against more than thirty killed and more than ninety wounded on the other side. The squadron went to Kristiansand and buried its dead there, and Suenson came home to Copenhagen a national hero and to a criticism that has never quite gone away: he did not wait for the enemy to come out, and the Austrians slipped away in the night to Cuxhaven.

It was a tactical and a moral victory, and it made no difference to anything. The war was being decided by rifled artillery on a hill in Sundeved, and a country that controls the sea and cannot hold a hill loses.

At the table, Denmark negotiated as though none of April had happened. The position held by Monrad's government began at the Eider — the whole of Schleswig to the kingdom — which was the war aim rather than an opening bid. Christian 9. tried as long as he could to keep the composite state whole and had to give that up too. When partition became unavoidable the argument moved to where the line should run, and there it stuck. The neutral powers proposed the Schlei, and Denmark accepted it and would go no further north; Prussia and Austria demanded a line from Aabenraa to Tønder; a line from Flensburg to the west coast was floated on the Prussian side and not adopted. The neutrals proposed that a friendly power decide the line. Denmark refused, on the reasoning that an arbitrator might award less than the line it had accepted.

The conference broke up on 25 June with nothing agreed. This is the point in 1864 at which a Danish decision, rather than a Prussian one, determined the outcome — and the decision was to go on asking for what the country had already shown it could not hold. A line drawn in June would have run well south of the one Denmark got in October, and most historians think it likely that a more flexible Danish delegation could have had very nearly the border it would get in 1920.

06 / NARRATIVEAls, and Jutland occupied

Danish terms in this section

Lundby
the last engagement of the war, 3 July 1864: a bayonet attack across open ground against entrenched breech-loaders.

The fighting resumed on 26 June. Three days later, before dawn on the 29th, Prussian troops crossed Als Sund in rowing boats from the fishing hamlet of Sottrupskov — about a hundred and sixty-five boats in the first wave — against a Danish force on Als of some twelve thousand men.

Danish resistance was broken completely. About three thousand men were killed, wounded or captured; one account puts it at 216 dead and 2,932 wounded, taken or missing. Prussian losses were around 372. Everything that could be got away was evacuated to Funen. Als was the last part of Schleswig in Danish hands and it was lost to a single assault.

Austrian and Prussian forces had meanwhile taken Jutland. They had crossed the Kongeå in February and they went the whole way: by July the occupation reached Skagen, and for the first time since the Swedish wars of chapter 24 a foreign army was quartered in every part of the peninsula. What that meant locally was requisitions — hay, oats, horses, wagons, quarters for men — paid for in receipts against a government that had lost.

On 3 July, at Lundby near Aalborg, a Danish force attacked entrenched Prussian infantry across open ground with the bayonet. The Prussians were armed with the needle gun and were lying down. Twenty-two Danes died on the field and seventy-six more were wounded or taken, without reaching the position, against three Prussians wounded; it is the one place in the war where the rifle explanation is simply correct. It was the last action of the war and it decided nothing whatever.

Monrad's government fell on 11 July. The new ministry agreed an armistice, which took effect on 20 July. By then the Danish army had no capacity left, the country north of the Kongeå was under occupation, and the question at the peace table was not what Denmark would keep but what the victors would take.

07 / NARRATIVEVienna, 30 October

Danish terms in this section

Wienerfreden
the Peace of Vienna, 30 October 1864.
kongerigske enklaver
the royal enclaves: land south of the Kongeå that belonged to the kingdom rather than the duchy of Schleswig, most of it medieval church estates of the see of Ribe; all but Ribe Herred traded away in the settlement.

A preliminary peace was signed on 1 August under the threat of resumed fighting. The treaty itself was settled in Vienna and signed on 30 October 1864.

Its central article is short, and it is worth reading in the shape it was written: the king of Denmark renounces all his rights to the duchies of Schleswig, Holstein and Lauenburg in favour of the Emperor of Austria and the King of Prussia, and undertakes to recognise whatever dispositions those two majesties may make concerning them. The cession of Schleswig covers every island belonging to the duchy as well as the mainland.

Read it again. Denmark did not cede the duchies to Schleswig-Holstein, or to Germany, or to the duke whose claim had been the pretext. It ceded them to two monarchs personally, to do with as they liked. Friedrich VIII, who had been received in Kiel as the rightful heir, received nothing. Sovereignty passed to a Prussian–Austrian condominium; the two powers quarrelled over the administration of the spoils; and in 1866 they fought each other over it, after which the duchies became provinces of Prussia in 1867.

Something went home before the treaty did. Thousands of Danish soldiers had been taken prisoner at Dybbøl and on Als, and they came home after months in German captivity to a country that had no particular use for them.

There were border adjustments, and they ran both ways. The royal enclaves south of the Kongeå — land that belonged to the kingdom, not the duchy, most of it the old estates of the bishop and chapter of Ribe — went to Austria and Prussia, all but Ribe Herred itself. In exchange, a string of border parishes south of Kolding and the island of Ærø came into the kingdom. The old Kongeå line, which had divided kingdom from duchy since the Middle Ages, became a state frontier for the first time.

Meanwhile · America, the same years.

The last full year of the American Civil War ran in parallel with this one, and its scale makes the Danish figures look small: at Gettysburg, in July 1863, more men were killed, wounded or taken in three days than Denmark had in the Dybbøl position on 18 April. The connection is not scale but destination. Over the following four decades a large number of the young men of the districts this chapter is about left for that country, and chapter 35 is partly the story of where they went.

08 / NARRATIVEWhat was ceded, and who

Danish terms in this section

optant
a person in the ceded territories who opted for Danish indfødsret under Article XIX of the treaty, which gave six years to choose and the right to move to the kingdom with one's goods. Many stayed where they were, as foreigners.
Pragfredens artikel 5
Article 5 of the Peace of Prague, August 1866, promising that northern Schleswig would return to Denmark if its people voted for it. No vote was held; Prussia and Austria abrogated the article in 1878.

How much Denmark lost in 1864 depends on what is being counted, and the published answers do not agree. The area is given as a third of the monarchy by one authority and two-fifths by the same authority elsewhere. The population loss is given as forty per cent. The number of Danish-speakers who passed under German rule is given as two hundred thousand in some accounts and a hundred and seventy thousand in others.

Two things can be stated exactly, because Danmarks Statistik computed them at the time. The parishes of Ribe Amt ceded under the treaty held 13,053 people at the census of 1860. The territory that came the other way — Ærø and the smaller mainland stretches — held 20,864. The land Denmark gained in the settlement therefore held 7,811 more people than the land it gave up in the same transaction, which is a fact nobody remembers and which changes nothing about the scale of the loss: the kingdom stood at about 1.6 million in 1860 and the monarchy at roughly two and a half.

The treaty gave the people in the ceded territories a choice. Under Article XIX they had six years to opt for Danish nationality and move to the kingdom with their goods, keeping any land they owned in the duchies. Thousands opted — by 1870 about a fifth of the population of North Schleswig — and a great many did not move. These were the optanter: Danish subjects living as foreigners in their own parishes, waiting for a vote. Chapter 35 §10 follows what the status cost them.

In 1866 Prussia and Austria went to war and Prussia won, and the Peace of Prague of 23 August 1866 carried an Article 5: the northern districts of Schleswig were to be ceded back to Denmark if their population voted for it. It was a Prussian concession to Napoleon III rather than to Denmark, no vote was ever held, and in 1878 Prussia and Austria abrogated the article between themselves. The promise lasted twelve years and was extinguished by the two parties who had made it, in the absence of the party it concerned.

Counting is also what the year looked like from inside a house in Copenhagen.

Vignette · Ilia Fibiger, Lægeforeningens Boliger, 1864

She is forty-six, and she is the elder sister of chapter 33's vignette by thirteen years. In 1853, when cholera was killing Copenhagen, she volunteered to nurse at Almindelig Hospital — a commandant's daughter presenting herself for unpaid duty in the epidemic — and from 1854 to 1860 she was employed at Frederiks Hospital to oversee the watch-women and the patients. Søren Kierkegaard died in her care there. She published plays and tales and a novel, always anonymously.

Her brother had tried to make a match between her and his friend Osvald Marstrand, a naval officer, and it came to nothing. Marstrand was killed at Eckernförde in 1849, in the war of the previous chapter; the brother had died in 1841; and the two deaths marked her for good. She never married.

By 1864 she is living in Lægeforeningens Boliger on the edge of Copenhagen — the terraces the doctors built after the cholera, still standing and now called Brumleby. She is bringing up other people's daughters there. In the year the state loses its duchies she takes in three more: Ane Sophie, Louise and Johanne Mathilde, all arriving in 1864. Another follows in 1865, the ninth since 1857. Six of them end up carrying her surname, though the family does not accept them after her death.

It uses her up. She dies on 10 June 1867, aged forty-nine, worn out in the housing scheme. Her poems are published posthumously that year, and the nine-page life at the front of them is written by Mathilde — the sister respectable Copenhagen had cut in 1851, and who had had Ilia's disapproval for causing it.

Ilia Marie Fibiger, nurse and writer · Lægeforeningens Boliger, Copenhagen · 1864 · [f]

09 / NARRATIVEThe constitution of 1866

Danish terms in this section

Den gennemsete Grundlov
"the revised constitution", in force 28 July 1866. Also called the July constitution.
privilegeret valgret
privileged franchise: the arrangement by which half the electors who chose the Landsting were themselves chosen by the highest taxpayers alone.

Denmark came out of the war with two constitutions and no territory to apply the second one to. The June constitution of 1849 governed the kingdom; the November constitution of 1863 had been written for the kingdom and Schleswig, and Schleswig was gone. Something had to be repealed, and the price of repealing it was set by the people who had wanted the June constitution amended since the day it was signed.

The mechanics matter, because they are where the leverage was. The November Constitution could not simply be repealed: it was the constitution under which the Rigsråd sat, and the Rigsråd had to consent to its own abolition. That gave the Landsting of the Rigsråd — the chamber the 1863 document had already given a restricted franchise — a veto over the terms on which Denmark returned to a single constitution. The conservative and landowning members used it, and what they asked for in exchange for winding up the joint institutions was the June constitution amended in their favour. The argument ran through 1865 in both chambers of the Rigsråd and was settled by a joint committee.

The revision was passed in 1866 and came into force on 28 July. The Folketing kept universal suffrage and was left alone. The Landsting was rebuilt. It would have sixty-six members: twelve chosen by the king for life, one by the Faroese Lagting, and the rest — seven for Copenhagen, forty-five for the larger districts, one for Bornholm — elected indirectly through electors, with half the electors chosen by the ordinary voters and the other half by the highest taxpayers alone.

The idea of that split — half the electors chosen by the most heavily taxed, half by everyone — was D.G. Monrad's, the man who had drafted the June constitution and had been dismissed as prime minister in July 1864. Its architects were the new prime minister, C.E. Frijs, and the landowner J.B.S. Estrup, interior minister in his ministry from November 1865, whose own stiffer proposal had been withdrawn in the face of opposition and came back, modified, in the compromise Frijs made with the peasant leader J.A. Hansen. Estrup sat in the Landsting he had helped design, elected from October 1866 to 1898 and one of the king's appointees from 1900, until his death in 1913.

Two details did the real work. The distribution of seats between town and country was frozen into the law itself, weighted heavily towards the countryside and with no provision for future shifts in population, so that the landowners kept their weight as the towns grew around them. And nothing whatever was written down about what should happen if the two chambers disagreed.

That mattered from 1872, when the united Venstre took a majority in the Folketing while Højre held the Landsting, and the machinery locked. What followed was a constitutional struggle that lasted until 1901, government by provisional law, and a king insisting on choosing ministers a parliamentary majority did not want — chapter 36's subject, built here, in 1866, out of the wreckage of a war.

That is the part of 1864 that is usually a footnote. Denmark lost its duchies to Prussia and Austria, and lost the franchise of its upper house to its own landowners, and only the first of those was done by foreigners.

10 / NARRATIVEWhat was to be won inward

Danish terms in this section

Hedeselskabet
the Danish Heath Society, founded 1866 to drain and plant the Jutland heath.
skuemønt
an exhibition medal, struck to be sold and looked at rather than spent. The one struck in 1872 carries the sentence this section is about.

In 1866 a group of men founded Det danske Hedeselskab to plant and drain the Jutland heath, and its first director was an engineer officer named Enrico Mylius Dalgas, who had served in both wars. What they did was real: over the following half-century the heath was substantially converted, and the argument that a country could compensate for lost ground by improving the ground it kept is the argument Denmark told itself for a generation.

The sentence it told itself with was not Dalgas's. Hvad udad tabes, skal indad vindes — what is lost without shall be won within — appears nowhere in anything he is known to have said or written. It was composed by the poet H.P. Holst for a medal struck for the Nordic Industry and Art Exhibition in Copenhagen in 1872, and what he actually wrote was For hvert et Tab igjen Erstatning findes; hvad udad tabes, det maa indad vindes — for every loss a compensation is found; what is lost without must be won within. It was about Danish industry, not about heather. The thought was not original either: in Svea, in 1811, Tegnér had proposed reconquering Finland, lost in 1809, inside Sweden's borders.

H.P. Holst had written something else, in 1850. Slumrer sødt i Slesvigs Jord — sleep sweetly in Schleswig's earth — was the song Denmark sang over the dead of the Three Years' War, the men in the ground at Flensburg.

Chapter 33 closed at that cemetery, and at the lion the Prussians took from it to Berlin after this war. The Danes buried underneath it stayed where they were, on the other side of a border.

Myth-check

"De Meza abandoned the Dannevirke and lost the war."

He abandoned it, and the abandonment is the reason there was still a Danish army in April. The reputation never recovered, and the record is unambiguous.

§03 gives it. The position could not be held with the force available and had already been shown to be turnable; a battle in front of it was the enemy's plan, not Denmark's; and de Meza was following the War Ministry's own instruction, which put a battle-ready army in the spring above any position. He was disowned by telegram for doing what it said.

What actually went wrong was not military. The government had allowed the country to believe that the Dannevirke would be held, because the Dannevirke was the argument for the war; and when the army did the sensible thing, the government had no way to explain it that did not also expose the policy. Somebody had to be blamed who was not the cabinet.

A smaller myth, worth killing in passing. That Denmark lost because the Prussians had breech-loading rifles and the Danes did not. The needle gun was real and it mattered at Lundby, where it did nothing but kill. It does not explain Dybbøl, which was lost to artillery — rifled breech-loading guns emplaced on Broager Land on 15 March, outranging every Danish piece on the position, and about eight thousand shells in the six hours before a man went forward. The redoubts were destroyed before they were stormed. A better rifle would not have rebuilt them.

Summary

IF YOU REMEMBER FIVE THINGSThe page in five

  1. The Rigsråd passed a constitution for the kingdom and Schleswig on 13 November 1863; Frederik 7. died two days later without signing it, at Glücksburg; and Christian 9., king for three days and king only because of the Treaty of London, signed the document that broke the promise made beside it.

  2. Nobody came. Britain would not act without France, France wanted paying, Russia had no interest in defending a state that had broken the settlement Russia imposed, and the German Confederation was acting lawfully until Prussia and Austria stepped outside it and crossed the Eider on 1 February 1864.

  3. De Meza abandoned the Dannevirke on the night of 5–6 February, exactly as the ministry's own instruction of 13 January implied he should, and was destroyed for it; the retreat saved the army. Dybbøl was lost on 18 April to artillery that Danish guns could not reach, after about eight thousand shells in six hours, and the casualty figures have never been reconciled.

  4. Denmark then negotiated in London as though it had not lost, refused arbitration, and got nothing; Als fell to a single assault on 29 June; and at Vienna on 30 October the king renounced all three duchies to the Emperor of Austria and the King of Prussia personally, who quarrelled over them and fought each other in 1866.

  5. The price at home was paid in 1866, when the revised constitution rebuilt the Landsting on a privileged franchise, froze the town-and-country seat distribution into the law, and wrote down no procedure for deadlock. That was done by Danes, and it produced a constitutional struggle that lasted until 1901.

Questions & discussion

FOUR KINDSWork the material

Recall

Did the facts land?

  • Which body passed the November Constitution on 13 November 1863, and which parts of the monarchy did it still sit for?
  • What did the council of war at the Dannevirke decide on 4 February 1864, and what happened to the rearguard at Sankelmark two days later?
  • What did Ilia Fibiger do in the cholera of 1853, and what was she doing in Lægeforeningens Boliger in 1864?

Causal

Why did it happen that way?

  • Denmark expected the great powers to intervene as they had in 1848–50, and none did. What had changed, and when?
  • Dybbøl is remembered as a battle. In what sense was it decided before the assault began?
  • The 1866 revision left the Folketing untouched and rebuilt the Landsting. Why was that a more effective change than restricting the Folketing would have been?

Counterfactual

What if it had gone otherwise?

  • Suppose Denmark had accepted arbitration, or a line north of Flensburg, at the London conference in June 1864. What would have been different in 1920?
  • Suppose Frederik 7. had lived another year and signed the November Constitution himself. Does anything about 1864 change?

Contested

Where do historians disagree?

  • Niels Kjeldsen was shot by a pursuing officer while riding away, and had refused to surrender. One version of that is a hero and the other is not, and Denmark argued about which from 1881 until the painting came down in 1913. What was actually at stake in the argument?
  • This chapter argues that Denmark was not betrayed in 1864 but had put itself in the wrong. What would count as evidence against that reading?

Go and look

STILL THEREPlaces you can visit

  • Dybbøl Banke , Sundeved. The redoubt line is marked on the ground and the mill has been rebuilt again. The view south to Broager church explains the battle better than any account of it: the towers the Prussian gunners spotted from are still there, and still in range.
  • Sankelmark , south of Flensburg. A memorial by the road where the rearguard turned on 6 February 1864.
  • The old cemetery, Flensburg (Flensborg Gamle Kirkegård). Chapter 33 closes here, and gives the lion's travels.
  • Als Sund at Sottrupskov. The Prussians crossed here in rowing boats before dawn on 29 June 1864. It is a short row.
  • Bæksgårde , Give parish, and the memorial stone of 1891 where Niels Kjeldsen fell, south of Vejle on the road to Kolding.
  • Brumleby , Østerbro, Copenhagen. Lægeforeningens Boliger, built after the cholera of 1853, where Ilia Fibiger raised her foster daughters and wore herself out.
  • Kongeåen. The stream was a state frontier from 1864 to 1920, and the old customs houses are still standing along it.
