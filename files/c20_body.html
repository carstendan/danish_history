Reformation and the Count's Feud

1523 – 1536 · Frederik 1. · Hans Tausen · Skipper Clement · Christian 3. · Johan Rantzau

A king promises to burn heretics and protects them instead. Ten years later the country tears itself apart over a king in prison, and when the shooting stops the richest institution in Denmark is being transferred to the crown — the bishops' estates in an afternoon, the monasteries over the next forty years.

Introduction

  2. How does a religious argument become a civil war — and whose war was it?
  3. Why did Danish peasants fight to restore the king who had been deposed thirteen years earlier?

01 / NARRATIVEA promise not kept

Danish terms in this section

håndfæstning
The accession charter, from chapter 14 onward. Frederik 1.'s of 1523 is the most restrictive yet, and it contains the promise this section is about.
kætter
Heretic. Frederik swore to prosecute them; the men he was swearing about were Lutherans, and within three years he was paying one of them.
beskyttelsesbrev
A letter of protection: the king's written undertaking that a named person may not be molested. Hans Tausen got one in 1526.

Chapter 19 left Frederik 1. on the throne, put there by the Jutland nobility and the bishops, and bound by an accession charter that went further than any before it. Among its clauses he undertook to prosecute heretics: no Lutheran was to preach, and the church's rights were guaranteed.

He kept none of it, and he did so slowly enough that the bishops never had a single moment at which to object. In 1526 he took Hans Tausen, a Johannite monk preaching Lutheran doctrine at Viborg, out of his order's jurisdiction and made him a royal chaplain with a letter of protection. In the same year he stopped the payment of annates to Rome and began confirming bishops himself. He appointed evangelical preachers to town churches. And at the assembly of the realm at Odense in 1527 he refused to act against the reformers, on the ground — carefully phrased — that his oath bound him in matters of the body and not of the soul, and that faith was between a man and God.

It would be pleasant to call that a principle. It was mostly arithmetic. The bishops were the richest and most independent men in the kingdom, they held castles and sat in the council, and the Reformation offered a way to reduce them that cost the crown nothing and could be presented as piety. Frederik was also, as duke, in his son's debt: Christian had introduced a Lutheran church order in his own district at Haderslev in 1528, the first in the Danish realm, and had shown that a prince could reorganise a church without asking a bishop.

02 / NARRATIVEThe preachers and the towns

Danish terms in this section

prædikant
A preacher: a man licensed to preach rather than to hold a benefice. The Reformation in Denmark arrives as preaching before it arrives as doctrine.
borgmester og råd
Mayor and council — the government of a market town. In several towns they hired the preacher and defended him against the bishop.
Confessio Hafnica
The Copenhagen Confession of 1530: forty-three articles in Danish, presented by the evangelical preachers to the assembly of the realm.

The Danish Reformation was urban, and it was carried by a small number of men who had been to Wittenberg. Hans Tausen, from a peasant family at Birkende on Funen, studied there in 1523 and came home to preach in Viborg; his sermons drew crowds the churches could not hold, and the town council protected him. Malmø went over in 1529, with its mayor Jørgen Kock behind it. Copenhagen followed. Odense, Aalborg, Haderslev, Aarhus — the pattern is the same, and the pattern is a town council deciding it would rather appoint its own preacher than pay a bishop.

Why the towns? Partly conviction. Partly a language question: preaching in Danish, in plain words, about a doctrine whose central claim was that you did not need an institution to be saved. And partly straightforward interest. Town councils resented the monasteries in their midst, which owned property, competed in trade and paid no town dues. The dissolution of a friary and the sale of its site were an attractive proposition to a corporation short of building land.

In 1530 the argument was formalised. At the assembly in Copenhagen the evangelical preachers presented forty-three articles of belief — the Confessio Hafnica — and the bishops replied. Nothing was decided. Later that year a crowd stripped the images out of Copenhagen's Church of Our Lady, which decided rather more.

Vignette · Hans Tausen, Copenhagen, summer 1533

He is about thirty-nine, he is the best-known preacher in Denmark, and his protector has been dead since April. The assembly that met after Frederik 1.'s death has gone against the evangelicals, and Tausen is condemned for his writings against the bishops and ordered out of Zealand.

What happens next is the story that has come down, and it is worth telling with the caution it deserves, because it is exactly the kind of scene that improves in the retelling. A crowd gathers, angry on his behalf and dangerous to the men who condemned him. The bishop of Roskilde, Joachim Rønnow — the man who had just prosecuted him — is threatened, and Tausen calms the crowd and gets the bishop away. Rønnow then moderates the sentence, and within a short time Tausen is preaching in Copenhagen again.

Three years later Rønnow was arrested with the other bishops, and alone among them was never released; he died in prison at Copenhagen Castle on 1 May 1542. Tausen became a superintendent and lived to 1561.

Whether the scene happened as told, the situation behind it certainly did: in 1533 the Danish Reformation could still have been stopped, and what stopped anyone from stopping it was that the crowd in the street was on the other side.

Hans Tausen, preacher, later superintendent of Ribe · Copenhagen · summer 1533

Meanwhile in Europe

Denmark's 1520s are late. Luther's theses were 1517; he was excommunicated and outlawed in 1521; the German Peasants' War ran through 1524–25 and was crushed with perhaps a hundred thousand dead, after Luther himself published against the peasants — which is the moment the Reformation stopped being a movement that frightened princes and started being one they could use. By 1531 the Lutheran princes had formed the Schmalkaldic League and the confessional division of Germany was permanent.

Meanwhile the Ottomans took Belgrade in 1521, destroyed the Hungarian army at Mohács in 1526 and besieged Vienna in 1529, which kept the Emperor Charles 5. — Christian 2.'s brother-in-law, and the one man with an interest in restoring him — permanently occupied elsewhere. Danish politics had a great deal of room in these years precisely because everyone who might have interfered was busy.

03 / NARRATIVEA throne left empty

Danish terms in this section

rigsråd
The council of the realm, which between 1533 and 1534 governed Denmark without a king — the only time it ever tried.
interregnum
The gap between reigns. Chapter 14 had one of eight years; this one lasted about a year and produced a civil war.

Frederik 1. died in April 1533. The obvious successor was his eldest son Christian, duke in Haderslev, who was thirty-nine and an open Lutheran. The council could not agree to elect him, because the bishops on it understood exactly what electing him meant, and so it did something no Danish council had done before: it postponed the election and governed itself.

It also, at the same assembly, passed measures against the preachers and restored the bishops' right to appoint. Having spent ten years losing ground, the bishops had won a vote — and in winning it they made the case that the only way to settle the religious question was to settle the political one.

Into the vacuum came Lübeck. Chapter 18 followed the town from its wars of 1426–35 to its defeat by Kong Hans in 1512; it was now governed by Jürgen Wullenwever, who had come to power on a popular and evangelical platform and who wanted the old Hanseatic position in the Baltic back. Denmark without a king was an opportunity. And Lübeck had a candidate with a real following in Denmark: the imprisoned Christian 2., whose town law and land law of 1521–22 had made him, to burghers and peasants, the king who had been on their side.

04 / NARRATIVEThe count lands, June 1534

Danish terms in this section

Grevens Fejde
The Count's Feud, 1534–36: the last Danish civil war, named after Count Christoffer of Oldenburg, who commanded Lübeck's forces.
landsknægt
A professional German mercenary infantryman. Both sides used them, and whoever could pay them longest won.

Count Christoffer of Oldenburg landed on Zealand in June 1534 with a Lübeck army, in the name of the imprisoned Christian 2. In six weeks he had Zealand and Skåne. Copenhagen opened its gates; Malmø had already risen and taken its castle; the nobility of both provinces, seeing which way the wind blew, acknowledged Christian 2. as king.

That concentrated minds elsewhere. On 4 July 1534, at Rye in Jutland, the Jutland nobility and bishops elected Duke Christian king — the Lutheran they had refused to elect a year earlier. The logic was stated almost openly: a Lutheran king who would leave the nobility's estates and jurisdictions alone was preferable to a restored Christian 2. who had legislated against both. The bishops of Jutland voted for the man who would abolish them, because the alternative frightened them more.

Christian 3. had one great asset: Johan Rantzau, his father's field marshal, a Holstein nobleman who had put down Ditmarschen's neighbours, beaten peasant risings before, and understood that in this kind of war the decisive question is who can keep an army in the field over a winter. Rantzau made peace with Lübeck in November 1534 — separating the town from its own expedition — and turned north.

05 / NARRATIVESkipper Clement and the Jutland rising

Danish terms in this section

vendelbo
An inhabitant of Vendsyssel, north of the Limfjord — the same country that rose in 1441 in chapter 18, and for related reasons.
herregård
A manor house. Something over a hundred of them were burned in Jutland in the autumn of 1534.
rettertinget
The king's court of justice, which tried the leaders at Viborg.

Clement Andersen — Skipper Clement, born about 1484 to a farming family in Aaby parish in Vendsyssel — was a merchant captain and privateer who had served Christian 2. at sea. In September 1534 he took Aalborg for the exiled king and raised North Jutland.

What followed was the largest peasant rising in Danish history and it was not a jacquerie. The peasants were armed with firearms, crossbows and long spears; they were organised; they had a commander who had fought a naval war. The Jutland nobility assembled at Randers and marched north, and on 16 October 1534 Clement's army came out of Aalborg and met them on a ridge north of the village of Svenstrup, and destroyed them.

For two months the rebels held most of North Jutland. Noblemen were driven out and their manors burned. Then Rantzau came up through West Jutland with professional infantry, and on 18 December stormed Aalborg. Perhaps two thousand people died in the assault and the plundering that followed. Clement got out wounded, was recognised by a peasant at Storvorde a few days later, and was handed over.

Vignette · Skipper Clement, Viborg, 9 September 1536

He is about fifty-two, he has been a prisoner for twenty months, and the war he started has been over since July. He is tried at the king's court at Viborg with the other leaders and condemned.

The tradition — and it is tradition rather than record — is that he was crowned with a crown of lead before he was executed, in mockery of the peasant king. What is certain is the sentence and the date, and what is certainly true is the aftermath, which is the part worth carrying.

The peasants of the rebel districts were condemned in a body: their lives and property forfeit, their pardon purchased with heavy fines, and — the clause that matters — the survivors took their own farms back as tenants of the crown. Men who had held their land freely now held it on fæste. Chapter 17 watched the Danish peasantry become tenants through economics over a century; here a whole province is converted by sentence of a court in a single year.

The other thing the rising did was to end the argument between the crown and the nobility about who needed whom. After 1534 they had both seen what the alternative looked like from a burning manor house.

Clement Andersen, called Skipper Clement, captain and rebel · executed at Viborg · 9 September 1536

06 / NARRATIVEØksnebjerg, and a year outside Copenhagen

Danish terms in this section

belejring
Siege. Copenhagen's lasted just over a year and was decided by hunger, not by assault.
undsætning
Relief: the attempt to break a siege from outside. The Dutch tried it from the sea in 1536 and failed.

On 8 March 1535 Christian 3. was hailed by all estates at the Viborg assembly, which settled Jutland. In the spring Rantzau crossed quietly from Als to Funen, where the count's garrison shut itself in Assens, and on 11 June 1535 he met and destroyed the relieving army at Øksnebjerg. The count of Hoya, commanding it, was killed. Funen was over in a day.

Zealand followed through July. By the end of the month Rantzau was outside Copenhagen, and there he stopped, because Copenhagen was walled, held by professional troops and a burgher militia that believed in what it was doing, and supplied by sea. Meanwhile the Skåne nobility had thrown off the count with help from Gustav Vasa — the new king of Sweden had no wish whatever to see Christian 2. restored, since Christian 2. was the man from chapter 19's Stortorget.

The siege lasted a year. Lübeck had made its peace; the Netherlands tried to take over the war and failed to break the blockade by sea. Inside the city people ate horses, then worse. Copenhagen surrendered on 29 July 1536, and Malmø with it.

The terms were mild for the towns and the mercenaries, which was deliberate: Christian 3. needed a functioning capital and had no interest in punishing burghers he intended to tax. Count Christoffer was allowed to leave. Christian 2. stayed where he was, in Sønderborg, for another thirteen years.

Vignette · Johan Rantzau outside Aalborg, 18 December 1534

He is fifty-two — a Holstein nobleman who has served two dukes and two kings, fought in Italy, and put down risings before. He has brought professional infantry the length of Jutland in winter, and the town in front of him is held by peasants and a few hundred mercenaries behind hastily strengthened walls.

The assault takes the day. Then comes the part that is not tactics: the storming and the plundering that follows kill perhaps two thousand people in a town of a few thousand. It is the worst single day in Denmark in the sixteenth century and it was inflicted by a Danish-employed army on Danish subjects.

Rantzau went on to win at Øksnebjerg and to reduce Zealand, and he was rewarded with estates and a career; his son Henrik built Breitenburg and became one of the great figures of the northern Renaissance. In Vendsyssel he is remembered differently, and the memory of 1534 lasted in North Jutland for centuries — which is what happens when a peasantry is defeated, fined, and put on tenancies it did not previously hold.

He was also, in the strictest sense, the man who made the Danish Reformation possible. Without the campaign of 1534–36 there is no Christian 3. on the throne, and without Christian 3. on the throne there is no recess of 30 October 1536.

Johan Rantzau, field marshal · Aalborg · 18 December 1534

07 / NARRATIVEThe night of 12 August 1536

Danish terms in this section

recessen
A recess: the written resolution of an assembly of the realm. The one of 30 October 1536 is the founding document of the Danish state church.
superintendent
The office that replaced the bishop: appointed by the king, salaried, with oversight of doctrine and clergy and no castles, no council seat and no land. The Danish word biskop crept back within a generation, but the office is the new one.
Kirkeordinansen
The Church Ordinance of 1537, drafted with Johannes Bugenhagen of Wittenberg: the constitution of the Danish church, in force in essentials for three hundred years.

Two weeks after Copenhagen fell, on the night of 12 August 1536, the king's men arrested the bishops of Denmark. It was done in one night, across the country, by agreement with the leading nobles, and it was not a religious act — no charge of heresy was laid, because heresy was not the point. The charge was political: that the bishops had obstructed the election of 1533 and were responsible for the war.

The reason for doing it before the assembly met is obvious once stated. The bishops sat in the council of the realm. They could not vote against the transfer of their own property if they were in prison.

On 30 October 1536 the assembly of the realm met in Copenhagen and passed the recess. The episcopal office was abolished. All the property of the bishoprics — estates, castles, rents, courts — passed to the crown. The monasteries were to be wound up, mostly by attrition: the monks and nuns in them were allowed to stay until they died, and the houses passed to the crown or to noblemen as they emptied. The bishops were released one by one as they renounced their office. Ove Bille of Aarhus, who had opposed the Reformation outright but had served three kings as chancellor, was let out as early as 1537 and given a series of rich fiefs; at the most lucrative of them, Skovkloster near Næstved, some forty people waited on him. He died in 1555. That a bishop on the losing side could be released within a year and pensioned handsomely for eighteen more is the best evidence there is for what August 1536 was and was not about.

In the same recess, in a clause with a long future, it was set down that Norway should thereafter be under the Danish crown like any other Danish province, with its council abolished. In practice it was not enforced: Norway kept its own law and its own institutions, and calling it a Danish province was a claim rather than a description. The claim nevertheless stood in the constitution of the realm until 1814, and Norwegians have quoted it ever since.

The following year Bugenhagen came from Wittenberg, crowned Christian 3. and his queen in August 1537, ordained seven superintendents, and helped draft the Church Ordinance that governed the Danish church into the nineteenth century.

08 / NARRATIVEWhere the land went

Danish terms in this section

krongods
Crown land. The word has run through this series since chapter 14, usually in the context of losing it. From 1536 it roughly triples — fast where the bishops' estates were concerned, slowly where the monasteries were.
tiende
The tithe. Chapter 12 established that the Danish version was divided three ways — bishop, parish priest, church fabric — with no share for the poor, unlike most of Europe.
gejstligt gods
Church property: the estates of bishoprics, chapters, monasteries and parish endowments, accumulated by gift over four centuries.

This is the section chapter 12 has been waiting for. It described a church being built into an institution with its own income, its own courts and its own law; chapter 17 watched the pious gifts of the late Middle Ages pile up until the church held something like a third of the land in Denmark. On 30 October 1536 the bishops' share of it moved in an afternoon, and the rest began to.

Note what the figure shows and what it does not. The nobility did not get the church's land. That is the single most common misunderstanding about 1536, and the numbers contradict it: the noble share of Danish land is roughly what it was before and after. What the nobility got was different and arguably better — the removal of the only body in the kingdom that could outbid them for influence, the end of church courts and church jurisdiction over their tenants, and first refusal on monastic estates as those fell in over the following decades.

The crown got the land, and with it something the Danish monarchy had never had: a domestic income to set beside the Sound Dues of chapter 18. Between the two, Christian 3. and his successors could run a state without asking the estates of the realm for very much. That is the fiscal precondition for the century of Danish power that Part F opens with, and, eventually, for a king who did not need to ask anyone anything.

The tithe is the neat detail. The Danish tithe had been divided in three since chapter 12, and after 1536 it was still divided in three: parish priest, church fabric, and — where the bishop's third had been — the crown. Nothing changed for the man paying it. The grain went to the same barn. Someone else came to collect.

The monasteries went differently, and more slowly. There was no general dissolution on the English model — no commissioners, no single act, no auction. The recess simply provided that the religious houses came under the crown, and what followed was attrition: the houses were forbidden to take novices, the monks and nuns already in them were allowed to remain and be maintained until they died, and as each house emptied its lands passed to the crown or were granted to a nobleman. Nunneries in particular lasted a long time — several were still functioning as residences for unmarried noblewomen decades later, which is a use the founders would have recognised and the reformers had not intended.

The buildings were treated as material. Some became manor houses, some parish churches, some hospitals, and a great many became quarries: the brick of a dissolved friary is in the walls of half the sixteenth-century houses of any Danish market town. Æbelholt in North Zealand, one of the richest houses in the country, is now a field with foundations in it and a museum of the bones of the people its infirmary failed to cure.

Meanwhile in Europe

The Danish settlement of 1536 sits in the middle of the decade in which Europe's confessional map was fixed. The Peasants' War in Germany had been crushed in 1525 with perhaps a hundred thousand dead. The Schmalkaldic League of Lutheran princes was formed in 1531. In 1534 Henry 8. made himself supreme head of the church in England and began dissolving the monasteries — a transfer of property so like Denmark's, and so close in time, that it is worth resisting the temptation to say either copied the other; both were kings with debts looking at the same balance sheet. In 1534–35 the Anabaptist kingdom at Münster ended with its leaders' bodies in cages on the church tower, which is the event that made every prince in northern Europe nervous about what happened when reformation ran past the point where princes controlled it.

And in 1536 Calvin published the first edition of the Institutes, and the Council of Trent was two years from being summoned. The choices being made in Copenhagen were being made everywhere, and almost nowhere else were they made with so little blood spilt over doctrine.

09 / NARRATIVEA reformation without martyrs

Danish terms in this section

statskirke
A state church: doctrine, appointments and property under the crown. Denmark's was among the most complete in Europe, and it lasted.
sognepræst
The parish priest. Most of them kept their livings; a good number kept their wives, who had previously been unofficial.
almue
The common people. What they believed in 1536 is the hardest question in this chapter and the least documented.

Nobody was executed in Denmark for religion during the Reformation. There is no Danish Thomas More, no Danish Latimer, no Danish Anabaptist rising. Set against England, Germany, France or the Netherlands, the Danish Reformation looks astonishingly bloodless — and then you remember that two thousand people died at Aalborg, several thousand more across the war, and that the whole thing was settled by a mercenary army and a year-long siege.

The right way to put it is that Denmark had its violence over politics and property rather than over doctrine. The war was fought about who should be king; the church question was settled by the winner afterwards, administratively, with the losing bishops in prison rather than at the stake. Two of the arrested bishops went on to serve the new church. It is a very Danish outcome and it is not the same thing as tolerance.

What it meant in a parish took decades. The mass became a Danish service; the images mostly stayed on the walls, which is why Danish churches still have their medieval wall paintings while English ones do not; the priest married; and the saints' days, the pilgrimages and the guild masses faded out over a generation or two rather than being abolished at a stroke. A Christian Petersen born in 1520 in a Zealand village would have found his parish church in 1560 recognisable in almost every particular except the language and the man's wife.

The one thing that did change immediately was the destination of a great deal of grain, and the one thing that did not change at all was who grew it.

Myth-check

"The Danish nobility got the church's land in 1536."
They did not, and the numbers are clear enough to say so: the noble share of Danish land is about the same before and after. The crown took the church's third, going from roughly a sixth to roughly a half. What the nobility got was the abolition of the only rival power in the kingdom, the end of church jurisdiction, and a good position when monastic estates came on the market over the following decades. That is a real gain. It is not the same as the land.
"Denmark had a peaceful Reformation."
Nobody was executed for heresy, which is genuinely unusual and worth saying. But the Reformation was carried through by the winner of a civil war that killed thousands, including perhaps two thousand at the storming of Aalborg, and it was completed by arresting every bishop in the country on one night. Denmark fought about politics and property instead of about doctrine. The blood is in a different column of the ledger, not absent from it.
"The peasants of 1534 were fighting for the Reformation."
They were fighting for Christian 2., who was a Catholic king when he reigned, and for the laws of 1521–22 that had protected them against their landlords. The religious and the social arguments overlap in this period and are not the same argument. The peasants of Vendsyssel wanted the king who had restricted noble jurisdiction; the towns wanted their own preachers; the crown wanted the bishops' estates. All three called it the same war.
"1536 made Norway a Danish province."
The recess says so, in as many words. It was not carried out: Norway kept its own law, its own courts and its own administration, and the abolished Norwegian council was in practice replaced by the same men doing much the same work. The clause matters not because of what it did in 1537 but because it was on the books until 1814, and because it is the single sentence most often quoted in Norwegian arguments about the four hundred years that followed.
"Christian 2. was forgotten after 1523."
An entire civil war was fought in his name eleven years after his fall, by people who had never met him, while he sat in a castle on Als. He was moved to easier confinement at Kalundborg in 1549 and died in 1559, outliving Christian 3. by three weeks.

Summary

IF YOU REMEMBER FIVE THINGSThe page in five

  1. Frederik 1. swore in 1523 to prosecute heretics and instead protected Hans Tausen from 1526, stopped payments to Rome, appointed evangelical preachers, and refused at Odense in 1527 to act — on the ground that faith was a matter between a man and God, and because reducing the bishops cost him nothing.

  2. The Reformation came through the market towns, carried by preachers trained at Wittenberg and protected by town councils that preferred appointing their own clergy to paying a bishop.

  3. When Frederik died in 1533 the council postponed the election, and Lübeck exploited the vacuum: Count Christoffer landed on Zealand in June 1534 in the name of the imprisoned Christian 2. and took Zealand and Skåne in six weeks. The Jutland nobility and bishops then elected the Lutheran Duke Christian, because a restored Christian 2. frightened them more.

  4. Skipper Clement raised North Jutland and beat the nobility at Svenstrup on 16 October 1534; Rantzau stormed Aalborg on 18 December with perhaps two thousand dead, won at Øksnebjerg on 11 June 1535, and starved Copenhagen out by 29 July 1536.

  5. On 12 August 1536 every bishop in Denmark was arrested; on 30 October the assembly abolished the episcopate and put all church property under the crown — the bishops' estates at once, the monasteries by attrition over the following decades — taking the crown from about a sixth of Danish land to about a half. The nobility's share barely moved.

Questions & discussion

FOUR KINDSWork the material

Recall

Did the facts land?

  • Who was Hans Tausen, where did he study, and where did he first preach?
  • What was decided at the Odense assembly of 1527?
  • Who was Count Christoffer fighting for, and who was paying him?
  • What happened at Svenstrup, and what happened at Aalborg two months later?
  • What did the recess of 30 October 1536 do — three things.
  • What replaced the bishops, and who ordained the first of them?

Causal

Why did that follow from this?

  • Why did the Reformation suit a Danish king, quite apart from what he believed?
  • Why did an interregnum of one year produce a civil war, when the eight-year interregnum of chapter 14 did not?
  • Why did peasants fight for a king who had been a prisoner for eleven years?
  • Why did the crown, rather than the nobility, take the church's land — given that the nobility had the votes?
  • Why did Danish churches keep their wall paintings when English ones did not?

Counterfactual

What if it had gone otherwise?

  • If the council had elected Duke Christian in 1533, is there a Count's Feud — and does the church still lose its land?
  • If Christian 2. had been restored in 1535, what happens to the nobility, and what happens to 1660?
  • If the Danish church had kept its estates and lost only its allegiance to Rome, as in England the church did not, what pays for the Danish state in the seventeenth century?

Contested

Where the argument still is

  • Was the Danish Reformation driven from below by conviction in the towns, or imposed from above for fiscal and political reasons? The evidence supports both, and which one a historian emphasises has tracked Danish church politics closely.
  • How large was Clement's rising, and how much of it was religious? The sources are the records of the court that condemned it.
  • The land shares of 1536 are estimates. A third for the church and a sixth for the crown are the conventional figures, and both have been argued up and down.
  • Was the arrest of the bishops a coup or the lawful arrest of men who had caused a war? It was carried out by agreement with the nobility, on political charges, two weeks after the king won.
  • Did Christian 2.'s reputation among the common people reflect his laws, or was he simply the available alternative? He had been in prison since 1523 and the peasants of 1534 were fighting for a memory.

Coda

CLOSING PART EWhat the hundred and sixty years did

Danish terms in this section

adelsvælde
The rule of the nobility: the period from 1536 to 1660 in which the council of the realm and the noble estate were the dominant power in Denmark.
helstaten
'The whole state': the composite monarchy of kingdom, duchies and overseas possessions, held together by one ruler. What Part E has been assembling.

Part E began in 1375 with a woman assembling three kingdoms and ends in 1536 with a king assembling a church. In between the largest state in Europe came apart, and it is worth setting down plainly what the hundred and sixty years actually did.

It ended the union. Not in 1523, which is only when the claim died, but progressively — a Swedish council that learned in 1434 that it could raise the country, a Danish council that learned in 1439 that it could dismiss a king, and no machinery anywhere for deciding what to do when the three kingdoms disagreed. The union letter of 1397 provided for perpetual joint election and made no provision for refusal. Norway went the other way and stayed, and after 1536 was a claim as well as a kingdom.

It attached the duchies. In 1460 the Danish king became a German prince on his own southern border, under conditions written by a nobility, with a sentence in them that four hundred years later gave a national movement its slogan.

And it found the crown two incomes it had never had — a toll at Helsingør that foreigners paid without anyone's consent, and a third of the land of Denmark. A Danish king in 1375 governed by persuading the men who elected him. A Danish king in 1536 had a fleet, a mercenary army, half the kingdom's land and the Sound. It took another hundred and twenty-four years for that to become absolutism, and everything necessary for it was in place by the time this chapter ends.

What the period cost is visible in the maps. The map of 1397 is the largest territory ever ruled from Denmark. The map of 1500 is smaller, and the map at the head of Part F is smaller again. Seven of the eleven territorial maps remain, and not one of them will ever be bigger than the third.
