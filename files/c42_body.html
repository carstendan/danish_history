1943: the year the policy broke

1943 · Werner Best · Georg Ferdinand Duckwitz · Ellen Wilhelmine Nielsen · Paul Aron Sandfort · Vice Admiral Vedel · Christian 10.

The cooperation policy was ratified in March 1943 by the largest turnout in Danish history and was finished by the end of August, and what broke it was a strike. Three weeks after the government stopped functioning the occupier moved against the Jews of Denmark, on the stated ground that there was no longer a government to lose. Most of them were across the Sound within a fortnight, and the year ended with a council nobody had elected claiming to speak for the country.

Introduction

WHAT THIS PAGE ANSWERSFive questions

  1. The German ultimatum of 28 August 1943 made one demand that was about Danes rather than about Germans. What was it, and what would signing it have committed the Danish state to?
  2. Why did the occupier leave Denmark's Jews alone for three and a half years, and what changed in September 1943? Use Best's own argument.
  3. Four hundred and seventy-two people were deported and about seven thousand crossed to Sweden. Which of those two numbers is a count and which is an estimate, and how can you tell?
  4. Christian 10. declined to sign his government's resignation. What did that deny the occupier, and what did it cost?
  5. What was Danmarks Frihedsråd, who recognised it, and what did it have instead of a mandate?

01 / NARRATIVEThe strikes of August

Danish terms in this section

augustoprøret
the August rising: the wave of strikes, street fighting and sabotage that ran through the Danish provincial towns in August 1943 and ended the cooperation policy. Not a rising against anybody's government; there was nobody to call it and nobody to call it off.

The war turned somewhere else and Denmark felt it. The Sixth Army surrendered at Stalingrad in February 1943; the last Axis troops in Africa gave up in May; the Allies landed in Sicily on 10 July; and on 24 July the Fascist Grand Council deposed Mussolini. None of it happened within a thousand kilometres of Copenhagen and all of it was read there. A country told for three years that cooperation was the only realistic policy began to work out how much longer realism would be required.

The sabotage figures measure the change, because they are not a mood. Setting aside burnt haystacks and cut telephone cables, industrial sabotage ran to seventy-three actions in the whole of 1940, 1941 and 1942 together. In 1943 it was eight hundred and sixteen. Railway sabotage over those three years was two actions; in 1943 it was a hundred and eleven. The graph does not rise. It starts.

The unrest of that August has no agreed beginning. Workers at Odense Steel Shipyard struck on 30 July, when the occupier posted armed guards against sabotage on their own yard. Esbjerg had the first town-wide strike, on 10 and 11 August; Odense followed from the 18th to the 23rd, Aalborg from the 23rd to the 29th, and there was trouble in Aarhus. One standard account says seventeen towns were affected and it is the only one that gives a number.

The mechanism repeated itself almost mechanically. An act of sabotage; a local German demand for a curfew; a strike against the curfew; crowds in the street; fighting with German soldiers; a further German demand. Nobody in Denmark called these strikes and nobody could call them off, which is exactly what made them unanswerable. The cooperation policy had been built to deliver order, and what had stopped was the delivery of order.

Five months earlier the same country had returned the cooperating parties on the largest turnout in its history. Hold the two facts together, because the second is usually offered as evidence that the first was a sham and it is not. March 1943 was a real verdict on the parties. It was not a verdict on the policy, because the policy was not on the ballot — and when the country was finally asked about the policy, it answered by walking off the job.

02 / NARRATIVE29 August: the fleet

Danish terms in this section

undtagelsestilstand
a state of emergency. The occupier demanded that Denmark declare one on 28 August 1943 and imposed a military one of its own the next morning when Denmark refused.
departementschefstyre
the permanent secretaries' administration: from September 1943 to May 1945 each department head issued orders with the force of law inside his own field, chaired by the Foreign Ministry and watched by a committee of politicians. A government in everything but responsibility.

On the morning of Saturday 28 August 1943 Werner Best handed the Danish government a list of demands and gave it until four o'clock.

Every clause describes something the occupier had not been able to get by asking. A state of emergency over the whole country. No more than five people together in public. No strikes, and no support of strikers. No meetings, indoors or out. Nobody on the streets between half past eight at night and half past five in the morning; restaurants shut at half past seven. Every firearm and explosive in private hands surrendered by 1 September. Press censorship under tysk medvirken, with German participation. Danish special courts for offences against the new orders. And then the sentence the rest of it was for: for sabotage and any help given to it, for attacks on German forces, and for merely possessing a weapon after 1 September, the death penalty, ufortøvet — without delay.

One demand tells you more than the others. Point four forbade any generen — harassment — of Danes on account of their own or their relatives' cooperation with German authorities. The occupier was requiring the Danish state to protect the Danes who had worked for it. A government that signed would not merely have been administering a dictatorship. It would have been underwriting its own collaborators against its own people, in writing, three weeks after Mussolini fell.

The refusal was delivered at half past three, thirty minutes inside the deadline, and its ground was capacity rather than principle: the measures demanded would destroy the government's own ability to keep order, which was the one thing the arrangement existed to supply.

The ministers then asked to resign and Christian X did not sign the request. This looks like hesitation and is the opposite. A government that has not resigned cannot be replaced, and the missing signature denied the occupier the vacancy it needed to install anything of its own. The ministers stopped functioning and could no longer be held to account. The forms stayed where they were, empty.

At four on the morning of the 29th the German forces moved. The army and navy were disarmed and interned after fighting that killed twenty-three Danish soldiers, or twenty-four; a couple of hundred prominent Danes were interned; and at Sorgenfri there was a firefight between German troops and the royal guard with the king inside. German soldiers stood around the palace until October.

At eight minutes past four Vice Admiral A. H. Vedel's signal went to every unit of the navy: K N U. The first explosion came five minutes later. Thirty-two ships were scuttled at their moorings, thirteen reached Sweden, and fourteen were taken undamaged. The artillery ship Niels Iuel ran from the Isefjord for Swedish water, was hit from the air, and was sunk by her own crew. Nine men of the navy were killed at Holmen.

By that afternoon the fleet Denmark had not used on 9 April 1940 was on the bottom of its own harbours, put there by its own officers, in about eleven minutes. It is the most exact image the occupation produced of what the cooperation policy had cost and what ending it would cost.

At Horserød the camp passed into German hands and about ninety interned communists got out in the confusion. Around a hundred and fifty were left, including seven women and the communist leader Martin Nielsen, and on 2 October they went to Stutthof.

From September the country was run by its permanent secretaries, each able to issue orders with the force of law in his own field, chaired by the Foreign Ministry's Nils Svenningsen and watched by a committee of thirteen politicians. It lasted until May 1945.

Whether 29 August was a rupture at all is disputed by the historian who knows it best. Hans Kirchhoff's three volumes argue that the continuity across the date is stronger than anyone had assumed: agricultural exports to Germany rose afterwards, the administration went on, the king and the politicians kept the structure formally alive, and cooperation continued to 5 May 1945 in everything but name. The encyclopedia's own entry on the permanent secretaries calls their regime a continuation of the cooperation policy on the administrative level. The break was real for the men who were shot at and for the ships on the bottom. This book's answer is that 29 August ended the policy's politics and left its administration standing — which is why the next three sections are possible at all.

03 / NARRATIVEThe warning

Danish terms in this section

jødeaktionen
the Jewish action: the German operation against the Jews of Denmark, proposed on 8 September 1943 and carried out on the night of 1 October.

On 8 September 1943 Werner Best telegraphed Ribbentrop proposing that the Jews of Denmark be deported. It is the most important Danish document of the occupation, and what makes it so is not the proposal but the timing argument attached.

The necessary measures, Best wrote, would have to be taken while the present state of emergency lasts, because at a later stage they would provoke reactions in the country — and then any constitutional government that existed would resign, and the king and the Rigsdag would stop taking part in governing.

Read it twice. Best is not saying that Denmark's Jews were in danger because the emergency existed. He is saying the reverse: that they had been safe because the government existed, that the occupier had left them alone for three and a half years because touching them would have cost him the Danish administration, the Danish police and the Danish food supply — and that the days since 29 August were the only window in which that price was not payable, because there was no longer a government to resign.

The protection had never been affection. It was leverage, and the leverage was what the strikes of August had knocked over.

Best put the number at about six thousand including women and children, which is his own estimate and low. Danish accounts give between six and a half and eight thousand, and the denominator is genuinely unstable, because the categories differ: members of the congregation, everyone the Germans would have counted, and the fourteen hundred or so stateless refugees who had reached Denmark in the thirties and had papers nowhere.

What follows is the best-known story in modern Danish history and the worst-documented. Best told the shipping attaché at his own legation, Georg Ferdinand Duckwitz, on 11 September. Duckwitz flew to Berlin on the 13th and failed to have it stopped, began warning Jewish acquaintances on the 17th, wrote in his diary on the 19th that he knew what he had to do, and went to Stockholm. On 28 September Best gave him the date of the operation and he passed it to Hans Hedtoft and the Social Democratic leadership.

That is Hans Kirchhoff's chronology and this book follows it, with two things said out loud. The Stockholm journey — the secret errand to ask Per Albin Hansson whether Sweden would take them, the most repeated detail in the whole story — may not have happened: Gunnar Paulsson reported in 1995 that the newly opened Swedish archives show Duckwitz issued a visa on 19 March 1943 and another on 15 January 1944 and nothing in between. And the underlying papers cannot be checked by anybody. The Duckwitz collection passed from the Danish national archives to Washington, where the catalogue lists letters, travel reports, the record of Kirchhoff's 1968 conversation with him — and a calendar for 1943 and 1944, not a diary. It is restricted until 2048.

A German official warned Denmark and Danes acted on it: both certain. Whether he did it on his own account, or was used by the superior who had proposed the action to make it fail, is not settled and may not be settleable this century.

Vignette · Copenhagen, 28 September 1943

He is a shipping man. He came to Copenhagen in 1928 selling coffee, joined the party in 1933, was disappointed by it, and came back in 1939 as the man at the German legation who handles cargo and tonnage. He is not important. That is the reason he is useful.

On the eleventh of September his superior tells him what is going to happen. He goes to Berlin to stop it and cannot. On the nineteenth he writes the line people will still be arguing about eighty years later: I know what I have to do.

On the twenty-eighth he is given the date. He takes it to a party office and hands it to a man who will twice be prime minister of Denmark. Then he goes back to his desk and processes shipping.

Almost everything else about him is contested. The trip to Sweden is contradicted by the visa records. The memoirs do not survive examination. The papers that would settle it are sealed until 2048, and what the catalogue lists is a calendar and not a diary.

What is not contested is that on the night of 1 October the Gestapo went to addresses in Copenhagen and found most of them empty.

Georg Ferdinand Duckwitz · Copenhagen · 28 September 1943 · [-]

Hedtoft went to the chairman of the Jewish community. At the morning service in Krystalgade on 29 September the acting chief rabbi, Marcus Melchior, told the congregation there would be no service, and to go and tell everyone they knew. It is usually called the New Year service and was not one: the new year began at sunset that evening, and what Melchior had in front of him was a weekday congregation gathered early for a festival that was not going to happen.

Meanwhile · Amsterdam, February 1941 and after.

Chapter 41 left this comparison standing and it has to be faced here. The dockworkers and tram drivers of Amsterdam struck in February 1941 against the deportation of Jews — the only strike in occupied Europe called over that — and were broken in two days with people shot for it. The Netherlands then lost about three-quarters of its Jewish population, the worst proportion in western Europe, and it was not for want of Dutch people willing to hide them: eighteen thousand or so were hidden, under conditions far harder than Denmark's. The Danish outcome was not produced by better Danes. It was produced by three things the Netherlands did not have: a fortnight's warning, a neutral country four kilometres away, and an occupier who had spent three years deciding that the Danish government was worth more than the Danish Jews. Remove any one of them and October 1943 reads like February 1941.

04 / NARRATIVEThree weeks in October

The raids began on the night of Friday 1 October and failed on their own terms. About two hundred and two people were taken in Copenhagen and eighty-two west of the Great Belt — some two hundred and eighty-four, against a population of several thousand who by then were not at home.

What followed was three weeks of amateur logistics. People were moved to the coast in ambulances, taxis and delivery vans and on foot; hidden in hospitals, where the staff invented diagnoses and funerals; put onto fishing boats from Dragør, Gilleleje, Snekkersten, Rungsted, Køge and a dozen smaller places; and carried over a strait that is four kilometres wide at the north end and twenty-five at the south. Most of them paid. The boats were not free and some of the prices were extortionate, which is a fact the story can absorb without difficulty and is usually not asked to.

It was not clean. At Gilleleje on the night of 6 October, between sixty and ninety people — most accounts say eighty — were hidden in the loft of the church, and the Gestapo hammered on the door before four the next morning and took them. Who informed has never been established. A woman named Ruth Nielsen was convicted for it after the war and acquitted on appeal for want of evidence, which leaves the question exactly where it was.

Vignette · Dragør, October 1943

She sells fish at Gammel Strand in Copenhagen, which means she is on the road before light and knows everyone on the harbour at both ends. She is fifty-five. Her house is at Rønne Allé 42.

The story her family tells begins with two brothers who sold flowers and asked her, offering to pay, whether she could get them to Sweden. She could — not in her own boat, because she has no boat, but because she has known the Dragør fishermen all her life and they will listen to her. After that people keep arriving. They stay in the house a few days at a time. Once there are thirty of them in it.

How many altogether is the part nobody knows. The published figures run from two to seven hundred, and one writer has given three different numbers in four articles; the seven hundred is the total for everyone who left from Dragør and is routinely printed as hers. Her own family says fifty to seventy-five.

She did not stop in October. Illegal post, weapons, couriers. On the evening of 27 July 1944 the Gestapo came for her and for eleven Dragør fishermen. Vestre Fængsel, then Frøslev, then Ravensbrück in December as prisoner 94,315, then the Jugendlager at Uckermark. The Swedish Red Cross buses brought her out on 8 April 1945 and she was home by the end of the summer. She went back to work, at a film studio, for twenty years.

Ellen Wilhelmine Nielsen · Dragør · October 1943 · [f]

How many reached Sweden is the number everybody knows and nobody can source, and this book is going to be awkward about it rather than tidy.

Every account says about seven thousand. The precise-looking version is 7,742, and it is a construction: Sofie Lene Bak states 7,056 Jews and 686 non-Jewish spouses as two separate facts, other people have added them, and where the sum appears alone it is unsourced and relabels all 7,742 as Jews, which is the opposite of what the 686 means. The only figure attached to a named archive is the Danish Jewish Museum's, from the Safe Haven database — copies of the Swedish police arrival reports held in Stockholm, of which about 6,336 have been analysed, for an estimate near 7,400, and the museum says the material is incomplete. At least seven thousand people crossed; the best archival estimate is about seven thousand four hundred; and the four-digit figure in circulation is arithmetic performed on somebody else's prose.

The database gave up something better than a number. Of those registering with the Swedish police, more than a thousand gave their religion as Protestant, Lutheran or Christian. They were fleeing because of who the Germans said they were, not because of anything they believed or practised — children of mixed marriages, converts, people whose connection with the synagogue had ended two generations back. The tidy partition into Jews and non-Jewish spouses dissolves on contact with the registers, and what it dissolves into is better: a racial category invented elsewhere, applied to people who did not recognise themselves in it, and escaped from by men and women who had to accept the label long enough to run from it.

05 / NARRATIVEThose who did not get away

Danish terms in this section

de hvide busser
the white buses: the Swedish Red Cross convoys of spring 1945 which brought Scandinavian prisoners out of German camps, including the survivors of the Theresienstadt transports.

Four hundred and seventy-two people were deported from Denmark. Four hundred and seventy of them reached Theresienstadt.

That pair is the most solid arithmetic in this chapter and it is recent. Silvia Goldbaum Tarabini Fracapane built it from the transport registration lists — the numbers issued to every prisoner on arrival — in the Yad Vashem archives, and she can name the two who are the difference: Michael Singerowitz, sent on from Sachsenhausen to Majdanek, where he died on 21 January 1944, and one other man who stayed at Sachsenhausen and was moved to Mauthausen with the unit that forged British banknotes. She also disposes of the rival figure: the 481 that circulated for years double-counts the ten people who arrived from Sachsenhausen and Ravensbrück in the first months of 1944, once on each list.

Fifty-one died at Theresienstadt over eighteen months. The two men above died elsewhere, which is how Fracapane reaches fifty-three, and two babies born in the camp to women from Denmark died there as well, which she counts separately. Four hundred and nineteen of the original 472 came home; four hundred and twenty-three people came home on the white buses, and the four in the gap are spouses and children born in the camp. Nearly every published confusion about this death toll comes out of that gap, and the only honest way to print the number is to print the definition with it.

They survived at a rate nobody else deported to Theresienstadt survived at, and the reason is bureaucratic rather than miraculous. The Danish authorities went on asking after them by name, parcels went in, and in June 1944 a Danish and Swedish Red Cross delegation was allowed to visit — the visit for which the camp was cleaned, painted and partly emptied by a further deportation to Auschwitz, so that the delegation could be shown a model town. The Danes were kept alive partly because they were being watched, and the show that kept them alive was staged by murdering other people. Both halves belong in the same sentence.

The same days carry another transport. On 2 October the men from Horserød — about a hundred and fifty, communists interned by Danish police in 1941 under a law the Rigsdag wrote afterwards to cover it — went to Stutthof, and twenty-two of them died: six in the camp, nine on the death marches, seven after liberation. Two ships out of Copenhagen in one week, and only one of them is in the story Denmark tells about October 1943.

The crossing killed people too. Bak's count, which does not separate the categories because the sources will not support separating them, is at least forty-two dead of suicide, illness, exhaustion and drowning, two shot by German police, and at least a hundred and four deaths altogether connected with the action.

Ninety-five per cent of Denmark's Jews were not deported. That is true, it is the best figure in occupied Europe by a distance, and it was produced by thousands of ordinary people taking a real risk for strangers. It is also true that four hundred and seventy-two went east, that at least a hundred died getting out or failing to, and that the country which saved them had spent three years handing communists to the same occupier. This chapter is not going to choose between those. It is going to set them beside each other, which is what §04 and §05 are for.

Vignette · Horserød, 4 October 1943

He is thirteen. He was born Paul Efim Rabinowitsch in Hamburg in 1930, and his mother and stepfather brought him to Copenhagen in 1936 to get him away from what Germany was becoming. He learned the trumpet. He plays in the Tivoli Boys' Guard, which is about as Danish as a boy can be.

The family is caught trying to cross to Sweden. On 4 October they are sent to Horserød, and on the 13th they go to Theresienstadt, where he is put in the youth dormitory L 414 and recruited into the ghetto orchestra because he can play. He appears in the propaganda film. He plays in more than fifty performances of a children's opera called Brundibár, in which the children defeat a bully, staged by a camp the Germans are using to prove how well Jews are treated.

The Red Cross buses take him out on 15 April 1945. He goes back to Copenhagen, studies music, directs opera in Rome, and in 1972 takes the name Paul Aron Sandfort. He spends the rest of his life writing about the place, and starts a project that has children across Europe performing Brundibár again. He dies at Hornbæk in 2007.

He escaped Germany as a small boy and Denmark sent him back into it at thirteen.

Paul Aron Sandfort · Horserød, then Theresienstadt · October 1943 · [n]

06 / NARRATIVEDanmarks Frihedsråd

Danish terms in this section

Frihedsrådet
the Freedom Council: the body constituted on 16 September 1943 by representatives of the resistance organisations, which had no legal existence and by 1945 supplied half the government.
illegale blade
the illegal papers: the underground press, which by 1945 was printing in the hundreds of thousands and was the only way most Danes learned anything the occupier had not approved.

On 16 September 1943, eighteen days after the government stopped functioning, seven men met in a borrowed office in Copenhagen and constituted themselves as the Freedom Council of Denmark.

Nobody had asked them to and nobody could have, and that is the point of them. The country had a civil service and no politics; an occupier with no Danish counterpart to negotiate with; and a resistance that had grown out of nothing into several organisations which disliked one another and were beginning to get people killed in uncoordinated ways. The Council was created by a vacancy rather than by any authority.

Who founded it depends on which reference work you open, and the disagreement is not trivial. Every list has Mogens Fog for Frit Danmark, Børge Houmann for the Communist Party and Frode Jakobsen for Ringen. One adds Arne Sørensen for Dansk Samling and stops at five; another drops Sørensen and adds Erling Foss and Aage Schoch; a third adds Jørgen Staffeldt. The founding membership of the body that claimed to speak for Danish resistance is recorded three ways by three respectable sources, which tells you what conditions it was founded in.

Aage Schoch is the interesting case, because he represented nothing. He had edited Nationaltidende until the Germans had him removed on 1 January 1942, and what he brought was the illegal press and a position in the middle of everything. He sat as himself.

Flemming Muus sat for SOE — or for the free Danes abroad, depending again on the source, and the difference decides what the Council thought it was. If Muus was Britain's man, the Council had a British member. If he held the seat for Danes abroad, then the Council had quietly awarded it to a British-trained agent parachuted in six months earlier, rather than to the Danish politician in London who thought the job was his.

That politician was John Christmas Møller, who had left Denmark illegally on 1 May 1942 and broadcast to Danes ever since telling them to make trouble: the voice chapter 41 ends by describing. He chaired a Danish Council in London which decided nothing. Kauffmann in Washington opposed him, the British thought him careless with secrets, and the men at home gave his seat to somebody else. He came home in May 1945 as foreign minister, bitter that Vilhelm Buhl and not he had been made prime minister — with the Freedom Council's consent. The man who had told Denmark to resist spent the war being told by the resistance that he was not required.

The Council announced itself in Frit Danmark that October: Den nationale Folkefront mod Nazismen er nu en Kendsgerning — the national popular front against Nazism is now a fact — signed simply as representatives of the Danish movements.

Its authority was nil, and then it was total. It had no law behind it, no territory, no revenue and no army. It was recognised de facto as a government by the Soviet Union in 1944 and never by the Western allies, an asymmetry that would look odd in 1945 and odder in 1949. By the summer of 1944 it could call the capital out on strike and, harder, call it back in. By May 1945 it took nine of the eighteen seats in the liberation government, the politicians taking the other nine and the premiership.

That is the fact chapter 44 cannot be told without, and it was settled in a borrowed room by seven men, three of whose names are not agreed.

Meanwhile · Rome, 16 October 1943.

Germany occupied Rome on 10 September, two days after Italy surrendered, and found about eight thousand Jews in the city. On 26 September the SS commander demanded fifty kilograms of gold within thirty-six hours or two hundred heads of families would be deported. The community raised it, with help from Roman gentiles, and delivered it at midday on 28 September — which is the same day, the same hours, that Georg Ferdinand Duckwitz was telling Hans Hedtoft what was coming in Copenhagen. Three weeks later, at dawn on 16 October, the Germans took 1,259 people from the old ghetto — 363 men, 689 women and 207 children. They went to Auschwitz on the 18th from Tiburtina station. Sixteen came back.

Myth-check

"The Danish fishermen rowed the Jews to Sweden for nothing." Most of the people who crossed paid, and some of the prices were extortionate. This is in the sources and it is usually left out, and leaving it out makes the story worse rather than better: a rescue carried out by people who also wanted paying is a thing human beings can actually do, and the version in which four thousand Danes act from pure motive on a fortnight's notice is a thing they cannot. The boats went either way.
"Denmark saved its Jews because Denmark was uniquely decent." The occupier left them alone for three and a half years because touching them would have cost him the Danish government, and moved against them in the one window when there was no government left to lose — which is Werner Best's own argument in his telegram of 8 September 1943. Then Best, who had proposed the action, appears to have arranged for it to leak. Danish decency is real and it is the second half of the explanation, not the first.
"About seven thousand" and "7,742". The first is a round number nobody can source and the second is a sum nobody published: Sofie Lene Bak's 7,056 Jews and 686 non-Jewish spouses are two separate statements and somebody added them. The only figure behind which there is a named archive is roughly 7,400, from Swedish police arrival reports, and the museum that holds them says the material is incomplete.

Summary

IF YOU REMEMBER FIVE THINGSThe page in five

  1. The war turned elsewhere in 1943 and Denmark felt it. Strikes ran through the provincial towns from Odense at the end of July to Aalborg at the end of August, called by nobody and stoppable by nobody, and on 28 August the occupier demanded a state of emergency, a curfew, censorship, special courts and the death penalty for sabotage — and that Denmark protect the Danes who had worked for Germany. The government refused half an hour inside the deadline. Christian X declined to sign its resignation, so the ministers stopped functioning without leaving a vacancy, and the country passed to its permanent secretaries.

  2. At four the next morning the army and navy were interned, and at 04.08 Vice Admiral Vedel signalled the fleet to scuttle: thirty-two ships sunk at their moorings, thirteen away to Sweden, fourteen taken. It was the largest single act of Danish defiance of the occupation and it was also the destruction of the Danish navy, carried out by the Danish navy, which is the shape most of this chapter takes.

  3. On 8 September Werner Best proposed the deportation of Denmark's Jews and argued that it had to be done while the emergency lasted, because later it would cost him the government, the king and the Rigsdag. The protection had been leverage, and August had knocked it over. Best told his shipping attaché, Georg Ferdinand Duckwitz, who warned Hans Hedtoft on 28 September; the acting chief rabbi told the congregation in Krystalgade the next morning. On the night of 1 October the Gestapo found most of the addresses empty.

  4. Something over seven thousand people crossed to Sweden in three weeks, in fishing boats, mostly for money. The best archival estimate is about 7,400, from Swedish arrival reports the holding museum says are incomplete, and the 7,742 in circulation is a sum somebody performed on two separate statements. Four hundred and seventy-two were deported and 470 reached Theresienstadt, where fifty-one died; 419 of the 472 came home. On 2 October about a hundred and fifty communists went from Horserød to Stutthof, and twenty-two of them died — the same state, the same week, delivering one group of its own people while another got away.

  5. On 16 September, in a borrowed office, seven men constituted Danmarks Frihedsråd. It had no law behind it, no mandate and no army; it was recognised by the Soviet Union and never by the Western allies; and by May 1945 it held nine of the eighteen seats in the government of Denmark. The year that began with a ratified cooperation policy ended with a self-appointed council claiming to speak for the country, and with the country largely agreeing.

Questions & discussion

FOUR KINDSWork the material

Recall

Did the facts land?

  • What did the German ultimatum of 28 August 1943 demand, and which of its demands was about Danes rather than about Germans?
  • How many people were deported from Denmark to Theresienstadt, how many arrived, and how many came home?
  • What was Danmarks Frihedsråd, who recognised it, and what did it have instead of a mandate?

Causal

Why did it happen that way?

  • Explain how the strikes of August 1943 led to the attempt on the Danish Jews in October, using Best's own argument of 8 September.
  • Christian 10. refused to sign his government's resignation. Explain what that denied the occupier, and what it cost Denmark.
  • About 7,400 people crossed the Sound and 472 did not. Account for the difference without appealing to luck or to character.

Counterfactual

What if it had gone otherwise?

  • Suppose Christian X had signed the government's resignation on 28 August 1943. What would the occupier have been able to do that it could not?
  • Suppose no warning had reached Hans Hedtoft on 28 September 1943. Estimate what October 1943 looks like, and say which of your assumptions is doing the most work.
  • Suppose the fleet had been ordered to Sweden a week earlier rather than scuttled. Argue the case for it, then against.

Contested

Where do historians disagree?

  • Was 29 August 1943 a break or a continuation? Argue both, using the exports, the administration and the fleet.
  • Denmark saved almost all of its Jews and delivered about a hundred and fifty communists to Stutthof in the same week. Argue that these express one policy, and then that they express two.
  • Werner Best proposed the action against the Jews and appears to have arranged for it to leak. Argue that he was saving himself, then that the distinction does not matter to the outcome. ---

  • Paul Aron Sandfort. Born Paul Efim Rabinowitsch in Hamburg on 12 July 1930 to Aron Rabinowitsch and Maria Warschavsky; brought to Copenhagen in 1936 by his mother and stepfather; trumpet in the Tivoli Boys' Guard. Caught attempting the crossing, sent to Horserød on 4 October 1943 and to Theresienstadt on the 13th, aged thirteen, into youth dormitory L 414; recruited to the ghetto orchestra, played in more than fifty performances of Hans Krása's Brundibár and appeared in the propaganda film; freed by the Scandinavian Red Cross on 15 April 1945. Studied musicology in Copenhagen, trained as an opera director in Rome, took the name Paul Aron Sandfort in 1972, wrote Ben. Vogel aus der Fremde, and died at Hornbæk on 29 December 2007. From the Hamburg university LexM biographical database and the Danish Jewish Information Centre's material on the Danes in Theresienstadt.
  • Rome, for the Meanwhile. The occupation of Rome on 10 September 1943, the fifty-kilogram gold demand of 26 September and its delivery at midday on the 28th, the raid of 16 October — 1,259 taken, of whom 363 men, 689 women and 207 children — the deportation from Tiburtina on the 18th and the sixteen survivors. That the gold was handed over on the same day Duckwitz warned Hedtoft is arithmetic on two separately sourced dates, not a claim either source makes. ---

Go and look

STILL THEREPlaces you can visit

  • Langelinie and Holmen, Copenhagen. Where the fleet went down on the morning of 29 August 1943. Peder Skram was raised and is a museum ship; the others are a line of moorings.
  • Dragør old town. The harbour the boats went from and the streets Ellen Nielsen knew. There is a stone in the pavement at Rønne Allé 42, laid in 2024, the first in the town.
  • Krystalgade synagogue, Copenhagen. Where the congregation was told, on the morning of 29 September 1943, not to be at home. It was built in 1833 and it has never been destroyed, which in European terms is the whole story.
  • Horserød, North Zealand. The camp that held Danish communists from 1941 and Jews caught on the coast in October 1943, and from which both were sent onward. It is still a prison.
  • Gilleleje and the North Zealand coast. The harbours the boats went from, and the church loft at Gilleleje where about eighty people were taken by the Gestapo after somebody told. The crossing is four kilometres at its shortest and looks it.
