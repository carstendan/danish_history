Christian 4.: ambition and the building years

1588 – 1625 · Sophie of Mecklenburg · Jens Munk · Ove Gjedde · Johanne Tommesis

He is the king every Dane can name, and the reason is that he is still visible: four hundred years later you can stand inside his decisions. This chapter is the half of his reign in which the money was there. The bill is chapter 23.

Introduction

WHAT THIS PAGE ANSWERSFive questions

  1. The regents handed over a full treasury in 1596. Where did it go?
  2. Why did a king build so much, and was it vanity or was it policy?
  3. Can a town be founded by decree? Six were. How many worked?
  4. Denmark and Sweden fought again in 1611. What had actually changed since 1570?
  5. Sixteen women went on a list at Køge between 1612 and 1615, and thirteen were burned. What made that possible, and what did the state do about it in 1617?

01 / NARRATIVEThe boy, his mother, and the charter

Danish terms in this section

enkedronning
Dowager queen. A widowed queen with her own lands, her own income and, in this case, her own credit operation.
livgeding
The dower estate settled on a queen for her widowhood. Sophie's was Lolland and Falster, and she ran it as a small state.

Frederik 2. died on 4 April 1588 and left a son of ten, a full treasury and a widow of thirty who fully intended to govern. She got none of it. The council of the realm excluded Sophie of Mecklenburg from the regency on the grounds that she was German and a woman, and appointed four of its own members instead — the arrangement chapter 21 described, and the best advertisement the adelsvælde ever produced for itself.

She did not go quietly. From 1590 she was recognised as regent in the duchies, which the council could not prevent because the duchies were not Denmark. She arranged her daughter Anne's marriage to James 6. of Scotland in 1589 over the council's objections, which is how a Danish princess came to be queen of England. She pressed for the duchies to be divided again between her younger sons. And she lost: in 1593 the council had Christian declared of age, partly to cut the ground from under her, and in 1594 she gave up and withdrew to Nykøbing on Falster.

Vignette · Sophie of Mecklenburg, Nykøbing Slot, 1594

She is thirty-six and she has just lost a six-year argument. What she has instead is a castle on Falster, the islands of Lolland and Falster as her dower, an annual charge on the Sound toll, and thirty-seven more years.

What she does with them is the point. She rebuilds the castle. She takes her estates in hand personally, experiments with agricultural methods, buys in more property year on year, and turns Lolland-Falster into the most profitable landholding in Denmark. She goes into trade. Then she goes into lending, at scale, to Danish noblemen and to German princes — and to her son, repeatedly, to pay for wars the council would not fund. A good deal of what she lent him was never returned.

She also studied chemistry and astronomy, which places her oddly close to the other Sophie in this part: two women of the same generation, both running an estate alone, both at work on the sciences their brothers and husbands were credited with. When she died at Nykøbing in October 1631 she was said, by people repeating each other, to be the wealthiest woman in northern Europe. That claim is still repeated and the estate figure attached to it traces back to nothing that can be checked; what can be checked is the lending, the land and the fact that her son owed her money. The superlative is not needed and is not evidence.

Sophie of Mecklenburg-Güstrow, dowager queen · Nykøbing Slot, Falster · 1594 · excluded from the regency in 1588, regent in the duchies 1590–94, died 1631

Christian was crowned on 29 August 1596, having signed an accession charter that conceded everything his predecessors had conceded and one thing more: an explicit undertaking not to make war without the council's consent. The coronation was the most expensive Denmark had ever staged, and it was paid for out of the surplus four careful noblemen had spent eight years accumulating. That is the whole reign in one transaction, and it happened before he had governed for a day.

02 / NARRATIVEThe king in his own hand

Danish terms in this section

egenhændige breve
Letters in the king's own hand. About three thousand of Christian 4.'s survive — an unmatched record of how one early modern ruler actually spent his days.
Bremerholm
The naval yard in Copenhagen: dockyard, ropewalk, foundry and prison. The largest industrial site in Scandinavia and the king's favourite place.

Most sixteenth-century rulers reach us through other people's paperwork. Christian 4. reaches us directly, because he wrote constantly and by preference himself, and roughly three thousand of those letters survive. They are the reason he feels like a person rather than a portrait, and they are not flattering.

He writes about everything and at every scale, in the same voice and often in the same letter: the design of a ship's stern, a nobleman's debts, the price of Norwegian timber, what is wrong with a particular sluice, the behaviour of his daughters, the correct treatment of a servant who has stolen. He drafts his own building instructions. He works out sums in the margin. He is funny, frequently coarse, quick to anger and quicker to move on, and he almost never delegates anything he thinks he could do better, which is nearly everything.

Two habits mattered. The first is that he travelled continuously — to Norway seven times, which no Danish king had done, and around his own kingdom constantly. He inspected. He turned up. The second is that he understood technical things and enjoyed them: he could read a ship's draught, he took a serious interest in mining and metallurgy, and Bremerholm under his direction became the largest industrial establishment in the north.

Set against that is the thing every account has to reckon with. He was not a good judge of when to stop, he could not tell an achievable project from an unachievable one, and he had no instinct whatever for cost. The council had. That is the tension the rest of this chapter runs on, and because he had money for thirty years, it stays a tension rather than a crisis.

Meanwhile in Europe

Christian 4.'s first decade is the one in which northern Europe's dynasties reshuffle. His sister Anne becomes queen of England in 1603 when James 6. of Scotland succeeds Elizabeth — a match their mother arranged against the Danish council's wishes, and the reason a Danish princess is buried in Westminster Abbey. The Dutch fight Spain to a standstill and agree a twelve-year truce in 1609, which is when their carrying trade takes off and the Sound toll with it. France ends its wars of religion in 1598 and Henri 4. is murdered in 1610. And the Holy Roman Empire spends the decade accumulating the confessional grievances that go off in 1618.

03 / NARRATIVEBuilding as policy

Danish terms in this section

Christian 4.s stil
The Dutch Renaissance manner he imported and then used so consistently that it reads in Denmark as his personal style: red brick, sandstone banding, copper spires, stepped gables.
Børsen
The exchange, built 1619–24 on the new Slotsholmen waterfront, with a spire of four dragons' tails twisted together. Still standing, and still an exchange until 1974.

Frederiksborg, rebuilt from 1600 into the largest Renaissance palace in Scandinavia. Rosenborg, begun in 1606 as a summer house and enlarged twice. The Holy Ghost hospital rebuilt, Holmens Kirke, the naval stores, the armoury, Nyboder for the fleet's crews from 1631, the Round Tower from 1637. The Exchange from 1619. A ring of new fortifications round Copenhagen, and a whole new town on the mud opposite it. Christian 4. built more than any Danish ruler before or since, and about half of it is still in use.

It is easy to file this as vanity, and vanity is certainly in it — Frederiksborg is a building that argues, at length, that its owner is the equal of any prince in Europe. But three other things were going on, and they are more interesting.

The first is that building was how an early modern state converted cash into permanence. The crown's income was largely a foreign cash flow that could stop; a palace could not be voted away by the council, and neither could a dockyard. The second is mercantilist: he was importing an industry. Dutch architects, Dutch engineers, Dutch hydraulic specialists and Dutch craftsmen came for the work and a good many stayed, and the drainage schemes, the brickworks and the shipyards they brought were meant to outlast the buildings. The third is that a great deal of it was military. The Copenhagen fortifications, Christianshavn's bastions, Kronborg's outworks, the foundries at Bremerholm: the ornamental king and the defensive king are the same man spending the same money.

What paid for it was the Sound. Chapter 21 followed the toll through Peder Oxe's reform of 1567 to the point where it was the largest single source of cash the Danish state possessed, and Christian 4. raised the rates repeatedly on top of that. This is the crucial fact about the whole building programme and it is easy to miss: almost none of it was paid for by Danes. It was paid for by Dutch, English, Scottish and Hanseatic shipmasters, a few rigsdaler at a time, at Helsingør. A king who could build on that scale without asking his council for a penny was a king the council had no way to restrain — until the money stopped.

04 / NARRATIVETowns made by decree

Danish terms in this section

købstad
A market town: a chartered borough with a monopoly of trade in its district. Founding one meant granting privileges, not just laying out streets.

He founded towns the way other men plant trees. Christianopel in Blekinge in 1599, to watch the Swedish border. Christianstad in Skåne in 1614, on drained marsh, to replace two older towns he thought badly placed. Glückstadt on the Elbe in 1617, sited precisely to take trade off Hamburg. Christianshavn opposite Copenhagen from 1618, laid out on a Dutch grid with canals and bastions. Christiania in 1624, when Oslo burned for the fourteenth time and he moved the whole town across the bay under the guns of Akershus. Kongsberg the same year, at the silver.

The theory was that a town could be willed into existence by granting it privileges and pointing merchants at it. The record is mixed in an instructive way. Christiania, Christianstad and Christianshavn are all substantial places today. Kongsberg worked as long as the silver did. Glückstadt never took Hamburg's trade, because Hamburg's trade rested on a century of connections that no charter could transfer. Christianopel is a village.

What the successes have in common is that something was already there — a fire that had to be answered, an ore body, a harbour a capital city genuinely needed. Where he was working with a real economic fact he could accelerate it by decades. Where he was working against one, a charter and a grid of streets bought him nothing.

05 / NARRATIVENorway governed hard

Danish terms in this section

Norske Lov 1604
Christian 4.'s Norwegian Law: Magnus the Lawmender's code of the 1270s translated into Danish and revised. The first new Norwegian law book in three hundred years, and in Danish.
sagbruk
Water-powered sawmill. The technology that turned Norwegian forest into a bulk export and made the timber trade a Danish crown interest.
bergverk
Mining works. Kongsberg silver from 1623–24, Røros copper later; both run on imported German expertise and privileges granted from Copenhagen.

Chapter 21 left Norway with its own law, its own courts and a peasantry that mostly owned its land, inside a state run from Copenhagen in Danish. Christian 4. is where that arrangement tightens, and he is the reason Norwegian historians find him hard to place: he did more for Norway and more to Norway than any king since the union.

The Norwegian Law of 1604 is the emblem. It is the first new Norwegian law book since the 1270s, which is a genuine act of government — and it is Magnus the Lawmender translated into Danish, which is the same act seen from Oslo. He appointed a resident governor at Akershus with real authority. He reorganised the len. He legislated against the remains of Norwegian legal custom he thought superstitious, and pursued witchcraft in Finnmark with a severity that has no Danish parallel.

He also treated Norway as the thing Denmark did not have: resources. Timber, and the sawmill technology to cut it at scale for the Dutch market. Copper and iron. And silver, found at Kongsberg in 1623, where he had a mining town laid out within months, German miners imported, and privileges written to keep the ore flowing to the crown. It was the largest industrial undertaking in the northern kingdoms, and it made real money for two centuries.

The honest summary is that Norway got roads, laws, towns, mines and seven royal visits, and paid for them with a Danish administration, Danish as the language of record, and a war in 1611 fought partly over Norwegian territory in which Norwegian farmers did the dying. Both halves are true and neither cancels the other.

Meanwhile in the world

The years Christian 4. spent founding towns are the years the European trading companies are invented. The Dutch East India Company is chartered in 1602 and the English in 1600; by 1620 the Dutch are the carrying trade of the world and the largest single customer of the Sound toll. The Spanish silver flow from Potosí peaks and begins to fall. Jamestown is founded in 1607 and Plymouth in 1620. Galileo turns a telescope on Jupiter in 1610 using the observational tradition Tycho Brahe had built on a Danish island. And in 1618 a quarrel over a Bohemian throne begins a war that will run for thirty years, which nobody yet knows.

06 / NARRATIVEThe companies and the sea road east

Danish terms in this section

Ostindisk Kompagni
The Danish East India Company, chartered 1616 on the Dutch model: a royal monopoly with private subscribers, the king the largest shareholder.
Dansborg
The fort built at Trankebar on the Coromandel coast from 1620. Denmark's first overseas possession, and held for two hundred and twenty-five years.

In 1616 Christian 4. chartered a Danish East India Company, and in 1618 sent Ove Gjedde east with five ships and about three hundred men. It took him two years to reach Ceylon, by which time the treaty he had been sent to conclude had evaporated. He went on to the Coromandel coast instead and in November 1620 agreed terms with the Nayak of Tanjore for a trading station at Trankebar, where he built the fort called Dansborg. Gjedde got home in 1622 with sixty men left out of three hundred.

Trankebar is where Denmark's overseas history starts, and chapter 27 takes it from here through the Guinea coast, the West Indies and the slave trade — which is the part of this story that the buildings do not prepare anyone for. What matters at this point is the pattern: a royal monopoly company, a fort, a garrison, and a mortality rate that nobody at the time found remarkable.

The same logic pointed north. The North-West Passage was still believed to exist, and a route to Asia over the top of America would have been worth more than any fort. In 1619 the king gave Jens Munk, a Jutland-born captain who had been to sea since he was twelve, two ships and orders to find it.

Vignette · Jens Munk, the Churchill River, spring 1620

Sixty-five men sailed from Copenhagen in May 1619 in the Enhiørningen and the Lamprenen. They crossed the Atlantic, worked through the ice of Hudson Strait, and by September were in a bay on the western shore with winter closing. Munk hauled the ships into a river mouth — the Churchill, in what is now Manitoba — and prepared to sit it out.

The cold was worse than anything in Danish experience and the food was wrong: salted meat and beer, and nothing with vitamin C in it. Scurvy started in December. The surgeon died. The priest died. By the middle of February men were dying every few days and there were too few of the living to bury them. Munk kept the journal going, and the entries thin out into a list.

On 4 June 1620 there were three men alive out of sixty-five. They crawled out, ate green shoots and raw berries until they could stand, and then those three sailed the smaller ship home across the North Atlantic, which is a feat of seamanship with almost nothing to compare it to. They reached Norway in September. Munk published the journal in 1624. He went back to sea, and died in 1628 of wounds after a fight in the Baltic — in the war the next chapter is about.

07 / NARRATIVEThe Kalmar War, 1611–13

Danish terms in this section

Kalmarkrigen
The Kalmar War, 1611–13. The second of the eleven Danish–Swedish wars, and the one Christian 4. started against his council's advice.
Älvsborgs løsen
The Älvsborg ransom: one million rigsdaler, paid by Sweden over six years from 1613 to buy back its only Atlantic port. The largest single transfer of cash in Scandinavian history to that date.

Chapter 21 argued that the Danish–Swedish problem could not be settled by a war of that kind, because it was structural: Denmark held both shores of the Sound and could close Sweden's access to the west whenever it chose. The Kalmar War is that argument's second demonstration, and it is sharper than the first because by 1611 Sweden was actively trying to engineer its way out.

Sweden had been building at Göteborg, on the narrow corridor to the Kattegat between Danish Halland and Norwegian Bohuslän, precisely to trade without paying at Helsingør. It had also been pushing north, claiming a route to the Arctic across Finnmark that would have bypassed the Sound altogether. Charles 9. began styling himself king of the Lapps in the Nordland. Christian 4. wanted that stopped, and — importantly — he wanted a war, having spent fifteen years building a fleet and an army he had not used.

The council refused for years, on the grounds that the charter of 1596 required its consent and that the war was unnecessary. In 1611 he went round it: he declared war in his own name as duke, using the Holstein forces, and dared the council to disown him. It did not. That manoeuvre is worth more attention than the campaign, because it is the constitutional settlement of 1536 being tested and found to bend.

Militarily it went well and proved nothing. Kalmar fell in 1611, Älvsborg in 1612; a Swedish raid into Skåne and a Danish push toward Jönköping burned a good deal of countryside. Charles 9. died in the middle of it and left the throne to his seventeen-year-old son Gustav Adolf, who would turn out to matter. The Peace of Knäred in January 1613 gave Denmark no territory and one enormous sum: a million rigsdaler for the return of Älvsborg, payable over six years. Sweden paid it, by extraordinary taxation that squeezed the whole country — and got its port back, and its route west, and a lasting and specific grievance.

08 / NARRATIVEThe devil in Køge

Danish terms in this section

trolddom
Sorcery. Distinguished in Danish practice from signeri, folk healing and charms, which was punished far more lightly.
trolddomsforordningen 1617
The Witchcraft Ordinance of 1617: the statute that defined the pact with the Devil as the crime and made burning the penalty.
huskors
Literally 'house cross': the burden a household must bear. Anne Bartskær's name for what she believed was happening in her home, and now the name of the whole affair.

In 1608, in the house of the merchant Hans Bartskær on the corner of the square in Køge, someone heard a hen at night and found nothing. Over the following four years the household reported knocking, objects moving, apparitions, a toad that walked on tall legs, a sick maid, and a foster son who convulsed and said the Devil was tormenting him. Anne Bartskær wrote it all down and called it the huskors.

In 1612 her husband took it to court, and accused a neighbour. Johanne Tommesis was a widow, sharp-tongued, and had quarrelled with Anne the previous year. That is the whole of the evidence against her that a modern reader will recognise as evidence. She was tried, tortured, confessed, and named four other women.

Vignette · Johanne Tommesis, Køge, 24 August 1612

She is the first. What the record shows of her before the trial is that she was poor, that she was widowed, that she spoke back, and that people in the town were a little afraid of her tongue — which in a market town of two thousand people was a form of standing, and is exactly what made her usable.

She held out and then she did not, which is what torture is for. What she said under it did the real damage: she named four more women. One of those, Mette Banghors, said she had met the Devil in the shape of a rat, and named others. Annike Christoffersdatter named five before she was burned in June 1613. Johanne's own servant, Kirstine Lauridsdatter, was burned three weeks after her mistress.

Johanne Tommesis was burned outside Køge on 24 August 1612. Over the next three years fifteen more names went on the list: twelve of them were burned, two did not wait — one killed herself in prison, one in her own well on the day the summons came — and one got out of the town and was convicted anyway. The list survives. It is the most ordinary document imaginable — a run of women identified as somebody's wife or by their husband's trade, Maren Muremester, Kirsten Væverkvinde, Magdalene who was Søren the tailor's wife — and that is what it should look like, because that is who this happened to.

Johanne Tommesis, widow · Køge · burned 24 August 1612 · the first of sixteen names on the list, of whom thirteen were burned

Thirteen of the sixteen were burned, two killed themselves and one escaped. Two things about the Danish pattern are worth holding on to. The first is that the great Danish persecution comes after the state got involved, not before: the ordinance of 1617 defined the crime as a pact with the Devil, prescribed burning, and was followed by the worst decade of trials in Danish history. Køge happened before the statute; the 1620s were worse. Regulation here did not restrain a panic, it standardised one.

The second is who was accused. Overwhelmingly women, overwhelmingly poor, very often widows, usually with a local reputation for a sharp tongue or for healing, and almost always known to their accuser. The exceptions prove the shape of the rule: Christence Kruckow, executed in 1621, is the only Danish noblewoman to die for it, and her case ran for twenty-five years and needed two separate scandals before it could reach her. A charge that killed a Køge widow in four months took a quarter of a century to reach a woman with a family behind her.

09 / NARRATIVEWhat the money was doing

Danish terms in this section

rigsdaler
The silver daler, the unit everything in this chapter is counted in: the ransom, the building accounts, the toll, the debts.
kongens egne penge
The king's own money, as against the realm's: the distinction Christian 4. relied on to act without the council, and the one that collapses in chapter 23.

Set the pieces side by side as they stood in about 1624. The Sound toll was yielding more than it ever had, both because traffic was rising and because he had raised the rates. The Älvsborg million was in. The Norwegian mines were beginning to pay. He had his own trading ventures, his own share of the East India Company, and his mother's credit behind him when he needed it.

Against that: a building programme running continuously for a quarter of a century, a navy enlarged past any Danish precedent, a standing garrison establishment, two new fortified towns, and the ordinary running costs of a court that was deliberately magnificent. He was solvent. He was not solvent with much margin, and almost every source of the money was a flow rather than a stock — tolls, ransoms and mines, all of which depend on nothing going wrong.

The council understood this better than he did, which is why it had spent twenty-five years saying no to wars. Its position was consistent and, in the event, correct: the realm's income could support the realm's defence, and could not support the ambitions of a German prince. In 1625 he went to war in Germany anyway, in his capacity as duke of Holstein, using the same manoeuvre that had worked in 1611.

The last document of this chapter is not a building. In 1625 the council of the realm formally declined to commit Denmark to the German war, and Christian 4. went in without it — with his own money, his own duchy's troops, and an army of mercenaries hired on credit against revenues he had not yet received.

Myth-check

"Christian 4. built Denmark into bankruptcy."
The building was paid for, over thirty years, out of a foreign cash flow at Helsingør, and most of it was finished before the trouble started. What broke the finances was two wars — the German intervention from 1625 and the Torstensson War of 1643 — and both are chapter 23's. The buildings are, if anything, the part of the reign that returned its investment: half of them are still standing and several are still doing their original job.
"He could not go to war without the council's consent."
That is what the charter of 1596 said, and it had a hole in it the size of Holstein. In 1611 and again in 1625 he declared war in his capacity as duke rather than as king, using ducal troops and his own money, and presented the council with a war already in progress. It worked twice. The second time it took the country down with it.
"The witch trials were a medieval hangover that the state eventually outgrew."
Backwards. The worst period of Danish witch-hunting followed the ordinance of 1617, which was a modern piece of legislation defining the offence and standardising the penalty. Køge, in 1612–15, was a local panic under older law; the 1620s, under statute, were worse. The state did not inherit the persecution and slowly suppress it. It joined in, and gave it a procedure.
"Glückstadt was built to break Hamburg, and did."
It was, and it did not. Hamburg's position rested on a century of credit relationships, shipping connections and accumulated skill that no charter could relocate sixty kilometres downriver. Glückstadt survived as a modest place and as the administrative seat of Holstein. It is the clearest case in the reign of a project that was well conceived on paper and had no economic fact underneath it.
"Trankebar was the beginning of a profitable Danish empire."
Eventually, unevenly, and not yet. Gjedde's expedition lost four ships' worth of men, the first company struggled for decades and was reorganised more than once, and Dansborg spent long periods nearly abandoned. What made the Danish overseas trade pay came later and came mostly from the Atlantic, not the Indian Ocean — and chapter 27 is where that has to be looked at properly.

Summary

IF YOU REMEMBER FIVE THINGSThe page in five

  1. The council excluded Sophie of Mecklenburg from the regency in 1588 for being German and a woman. She was regent in the duchies instead, married her daughter to the king of Scotland over the council's objections, and after 1594 built Lolland-Falster into the most profitable estate in Denmark and a credit business her son borrowed from repeatedly.

  2. The building programme — Frederiksborg, Rosenborg, Børsen, Christianshavn, the fortifications, Bremerholm — was paid for out of the Sound toll, which is to say by foreign shipmasters, which is why no council could restrain it.

  3. Six towns were founded by decree. The ones that worked had something already there: a burnt-out Oslo, an ore body at Kongsberg, a harbour Copenhagen needed. Glückstadt, aimed at Hamburg's trade, had nothing underneath it and never took.

  4. The Kalmar War of 1611–13 was begun against the council's refusal, by declaring war as duke of Holstein rather than as king. It gained no territory and one million rigsdaler for the return of Älvsborg — and the same manoeuvre was used again in 1625.

  5. Johanne Tommesis was burned at Køge on 24 August 1612 after naming four other women under torture; fifteen more names followed by 1615, and thirteen of the sixteen were burned. The Witchcraft Ordinance of 1617 then defined the offence and prescribed burning, and the 1620s were worse than anything before them.

Questions & discussion

FOUR KINDSWork the material

Recall

Did the facts land?

  • Why was Sophie of Mecklenburg kept off the regency council, and what did she do instead?
  • Name four things Christian 4. built and say what each was for.
  • Which of the six new towns took, and what did the survivors have in common?
  • What did Sweden pay for the return of Älvsborg, and how did it raise the money?
  • What did the ordinance of 1617 define, and what followed it?

Causal

Why did that follow from this?

  • Why did a toll collected at Helsingør make the council unable to restrain the king?
  • Why could Christian 4. make war in 1611 when the charter of 1596 said he could not?
  • Why did a confession under torture have to include naming other people, and what did that do to the number of deaths?
  • Why did Sweden care so much about Göteborg and a route across Finnmark?

Counterfactual

What if it had gone otherwise?

  • If the council had found a way to close the Holstein loophole in 1611, does Denmark still enter Germany in 1625?
  • If Sophie had been given the regency in 1588, what changes — and is there any reason to think she would have governed differently?
  • If the Sound toll had been a Danish tax rather than a foreign one, would any of these buildings exist?

Contested

Where the argument still is

  • Whether Christian 4. was an unusually bad ruler or an unusually visible one. Danish opinion has swung twice, and the buildings make an unbiased verdict nearly impossible.
  • How much the Sound toll actually contributed, and when. The same problem as chapter 21: no consolidated accounts exist to check the estimates against.
  • What drove the Danish witch trials — state formation, Lutheran doctrine, local conflict, or the ordinary economics of poor widows in small towns. Current work leans local and economic; the 1617 statute complicates any purely bottom-up account.
  • Whether Norway under Christian 4. is best read as a province being developed or a province being exploited. Danish and Norwegian historiography have answered differently for two hundred years.

It is evidence of what people thought was happening, and of nothing else. The court record and the list of the executed are separate and are what the dates come from.
  • — the Sound Toll Registers, and the Norwegian Law of 1604 The money and the administration.
  • — danmarkshistorien.dk Aarhus University: the Kalmar War, the 1617 ordinance, the town foundations, the East India Company.
  • WHERE THE ARGUMENT STANDS Named as positions in a historiography, not as books this page has read.
  • — Steffen Heiberg, on Christian 4. The standard modern Danish biography, and the source of the now-conventional view that the peace years are the achievement and the wars the failure.
  • — Jens Christian V. Johansen and Louise Nyholm Kallestrup, on the Danish witch trials The quantitative and the comparative sides of the current scholarship, including the argument about what the 1617 ordinance changed.
  • — Ole Feldbæk, on the trading companies Where this chapter says Trankebar was not immediately profitable, that is the position it is reporting.

Go and look

STILL THEREPlaces you can visit

  • Frederiksborg, Hillerød Forty minutes from Copenhagen and the single best argument the reign makes for itself. Now the Museum of National History. Much of the interior is a nineteenth-century reconstruction after the fire of 1859 — worth knowing before you admire it.
  • Rosenborg, Copenhagen Small, dense and much more personal than Frederiksborg. The regalia are in the basement; the king's own rooms are the reason to go.
  • Børsen, Slotsholmen The exchange of 1619–24, with the four-dragon spire. It burned on 16 April 2024 and the spire came down; reconstruction is under way, so what you see will depend on when you go. The façade survived.
  • Christianshavn Walk the canal and the surviving bastions. The street grid you are standing on is the 1618 plan, and it is Dutch, not Danish.
  • Kvadraturen and Akershus, Oslo The grid Christian 4. laid out in 1624 after the fire, under the fortress that dictated where it went. The city was called Christiania until 1925.
  • Kongsberg silver mines, Norway Ninety minutes from Oslo. The mine train goes two kilometres into the mountain. Founded 1624 and worked until 1958.
  • The Køge Huskors plaque, Nørregade, Køge On a wall on the corner of the square, marking the Bartskær house. It is very small and easy to walk past, which is a fair reflection of how the sixteen women are remembered.
  • Dansborg, Tharangambadi, Tamil Nadu The fort of 1620, restored, with a museum. The Danish street names survived longer than the Danes did.
