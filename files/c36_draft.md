# Chapter 36 — Provisorietiden and the change of system, 1875–1901

*Draft, ten sections plus the Part H coda. Prose only; apparatus follows.*
*Notes marked in italics inside the text are drafting flags, not copy.*

## Two chambers, two countries

The revision of 1866 gave Denmark two chambers elected on different franchises,
and within six years they were returning different countries.

The Folketing kept universal suffrage and was carried, from 1872, by Venstre: the
freeholders of chapter 35, the parishes, the meeting-houses, the folk high schools
and the cooperative dairies. The Landsting was elected through electors of whom
half were chosen by the highest taxpayers alone, with twelve members appointed by
the king for life and the town-and-country distribution frozen into the law. It
was carried by Højre: landowners, senior officials, the officer corps and the
established church.

Neither was unrepresentative. That is the difficulty. Both chambers accurately
reflected the constituencies they had been designed to reflect, and those
constituencies wanted opposite things. In 1873 Venstre drew the conclusion and
demanded *folketingsparlamentarisme* — that the government be appointed on the
basis of the Folketing's majority. Højre answered that the constitution gave the
king the right to choose his ministers, and that a chamber was not a government.

Both were quoting the same document. Neither was misquoting it.

And neither chamber could dissolve the other, dismiss the other, or outvote the
other. They were built to check each other and had no procedure for what to do when
checking was all they did.

---

## The question nobody had answered

The constitution of 1849, and the revision of 1866 after it, never said whether a
ministry answers to the king or to the Folketing. It was not evaded; it was not
thought of. In 1849 the question of who dismisses a government had no obvious
answer because no Danish government had ever been dismissed by anybody but the
king, and the men writing the text were more concerned to establish that ministers
were answerable at all.

The men of 1849 had thought hard about ministerial responsibility, but the
responsibility they had in mind was legal: a minister could be impeached for
breaking the law. That a minister might be perfectly lawful and simply unwanted by
the elected chamber was not a case they wrote for, because in 1849 the elected
chamber was the new and untried thing and the king was the fact everyone had grown
up with.

So the nineteen-year conflict that follows is a gap in a document, and both sides
read the gap honestly.

Venstre's text was **§48**: the finance bill must be laid before the Folketing
first. From a procedural precedence they argued a constitutional one — the chamber
that sees the money first is the chamber the government must satisfy.

Højre's text was **§25**: in *særdeles paatrængende Tilfælde*, when the Rigsdag was
not assembled, the king could issue provisional laws. The reading was not
improvised. It was supplied by two professors of law, H. Matzen and J. Nellemann,
and Nellemann was also the minister of justice — so the government's legal opinion
and the government's law officer were the same man.

Before 1875 the clause had been used exactly once, in 1853, when the Rigsdag had
been dissolved and could not reassemble before a finance law fell due. That is the
whole precedent: an administrative gap of a few weeks.

And the clause carried a condition that shows what it was for. A provisional law
could only be issued while the Rigsdag was *not* sitting. To keep issuing them, the
government therefore had to keep sending the Rigsdag home. The emergency
provision could only be used by manufacturing the emergency.

---

## Estrup

Jacob Brønnum Scavenius Estrup was a landowner from Zealand, and the reader has
met him already: he was the interior minister who designed the revision of 1866,
and he then took one of the twelve Landsting seats the king appointed for life and
held it, with one interruption, until his death. He built the machine and then sat
inside it.

He was not a soldier or an orator. He was tubercular all his life, which is why he
never took a student examination — he trained as a forester instead, on the grounds
that it meant less sitting indoors — and the illness left him with a discipline
that people who disliked him described as coldness. He became council president in
1875 and stayed for nineteen years.

The conflict began before the provisional laws did. In 1874 the Venstre groups
voted down the finance bill, on the reasoning that a country cannot be governed
without one and a government that cannot get one must go. The government dissolved
the Folketing instead. In 1877 the two chambers could not agree the budget and
Estrup issued a provisional finance law — a modest one, and both chambers passed a
proper law shortly afterwards. The technique had been tested and put away.

Venstre's answer was the *visnepolitik*, the withering policy: vote down every
expenditure that was not strictly necessary, and starve the government into
resigning. It had a consequence its authors did not intend. **By the mid-1880s the
treasury was unusually full**, because almost nothing had been voted for years. And
Estrup had refused, year after year, to introduce income and wealth taxes to pay
for defence. So when the crisis came, the argument was not that the money was
lacking. The money was sitting there. What was in dispute was who had the right to
spend it.

There was a third party to this and he is easy to leave out. Christian 9. was not
a bystander: the ministry was his, appointed by him and kept in office by him
against the vote of the Folketing, and without his signature none of it was
possible. It is worth remembering what he had learned about yielding. In 1863,
three days on the throne, he had been pressed into signing a constitution he
believed would cost the country its duchies, and it had — chapter 34 §01. A king
who had given way once under pressure, and watched the result, was not a king
disposed to give way twice. That is an interpretation and is offered as one, but it
is hard to read his conduct in the 1880s without it.

It matters to say what Estrup was not. He was not a man seizing power he had no
claim to. He had a clause, two professors, a majority in one chamber and the
support of the king, and he believed — with a conviction that is easy to caricature
and was widely shared in his class and elsewhere in Europe — that a chamber
elected by every man in the country should not be permitted to appoint the
government of the country. He was defending a constitution as he read it.

He was also, by the end, a man who had governed for nine years against the express
vote of the elected chamber, using an emergency clause to build a fortress, and
who had put a military police force into the villages of people who disagreed with
him. Both of those sentences are true and the chapter needs both.

---

## Ruling without a budget, 1885

Christiansborg had burned down in 1884, so the two chambers met that winter in
rented rooms in Fredericiagade, in what is now the Eastern High Court. Contemporary
accounts describe the atmosphere as oppressive: two hostile assemblies in a
temporary building, in a state whose parliament no longer had a house.

Venstre would not pass a finance bill carrying the fortification of Copenhagen.
Estrup did not negotiate. On **31 March 1885** he had the royal resolution ready
and sent the Rigsdag home with no budget passed; on **1 April** the government
issued a provisional finance law whose contents it had written itself.

Then it did the same thing the next year, and the next, in almost identical terms,
every year until 1894.

What the laws actually said is worth reading, because it is blander than the
conflict around it and more remarkable for being bland. They authorised the
government to meet the necessary expenses of the state out of the treasury, as the
government judged them. That is the whole of it. There is no rhetoric in a
provisional finance law and no emergency described in it. It is an accounting
instrument, and for nine years it was the legal basis on which a European
constitutional monarchy was administered.

Meanwhile the ordinary business continued, which is the part that startles a
modern reader. Elections were held. Estrup's party lost them, repeatedly and
heavily; Højre never came close to a Folketing majority in these years and never
expected to. The results were published, the members took their seats, the chamber
debated, and the government went on spending money the chamber had refused. Nobody
was prevented from voting. It simply made no difference to who governed, and
everybody could see that it made no difference, which is precisely what turned a
disagreement about a clause into a crisis about the state.

The legal position was stranger than either side's slogans. A provisional law was
by its own nature temporary and had to be laid before the Rigsdag when it
reassembled. It was. On **25 January 1886 the Folketing rejected** the provisional
finance law of 1 April 1885 — and nothing happened. The money had been spent. A
royal resolution the following day dealt with the consequences, and the government
carried on. The constitution provided a procedure for the Rigsdag to say no and no
procedure for the no to mean anything.

That is the exact shape of the thing, and it is worth resisting both easy
descriptions. It was not a coup: no soldiers surrounded the chamber, elections
continued to be held and lost, the courts sat, Estrup submitted his laws for
rejection as the text required. Nor was it lawful government: a clause written for
an administrative gap of weeks was used for nine years to spend money the elected
chamber had refused, and the government had to dissolve parliament repeatedly in
order to keep using it.

What it was, precisely, is a government discovering that a constitution which
provides no remedy provides no restraint, and deciding to find out how far that
went.

---

## The gendarmes

Up to this point the conflict was conducted between men in a building. In 1885 it
came out into the country, and that is what people remembered.

It began with weapons. In impotence at their exclusion from any influence, Venstre
supporters had been organising rifle associations across the countryside —
descendants of the shooting clubs that had grown up beside the Grundtvigian
meeting-houses, and not obviously an armed threat, but not obviously not one
either. On **5 May 1885** the government issued the *Riffellov* by provisional law,
restricting the acquisition and use of rifles and shutting the associations down.

Then came 21 October, which is the next section. Six days after it, on **27
October 1885**, the government issued a provisional law creating a **military
gendarmerie corps** to assist the police anywhere in the kingdom, together with
laws on police expenditure and on criminal-court assessors. On **2 November** a
criminal-law provisorium made it an offence to put about "invented or distorted
facts" in order to render the institutions of the state or the measures of the
government hated or contemptible.

Danish accounts say plainly where the model came from: the far-reaching provisional
laws Germany had issued after two attempts on the Kaiser's life. The country that
had lost Slesvig to Bismarck in 1864 was governed in 1885 on Bismarck's emergency
statute book.

The gendarmes wore light blue and were universally known by it. They were posted
into market towns and villages, and they were loathed with an intensity that
surprised the government, because what they represented was not policing but
supervision: the state stationing armed men among farmers whose offence was how
they voted. At Brønderslev market it turned into a brawl and the gendarmes drew
their sabres; afterwards public meetings were prohibited across Vendsyssel.

It reached into how people bought and sold. Villages boycotted shopkeepers who
took Højre's side and shopkeepers boycotted Venstre customers; a district could
divide over which inn a meeting was held in, and the schoolteacher, the priest and
the parish council chairman found themselves publicly on one side or the other
whether or not they wished to be. This is the same social geography chapter 35
described, put under pressure: the meeting-house and the mission house, the rifle
club and the rectory, now with a political test applied to each.

The prosecutions ran alongside. The Venstre leader Christen Berg was given six
months for physically removing the police chief of Holstebro, who had tried to stop
him delivering an advertised speech. In September 1886 the priest and folk high
school principal **Morten Pontoppidan was sentenced to four months in prison for
saying that the government had broken the constitution** — which, on Venstre's
reading of the constitution, it had. On Constitution Day 1886 there were large
demonstrations across the country.

Some historians have written that Denmark could have gone to civil war in these
years. That is a counterfactual and should be labelled one. What can be said
without speculation is that a country with no rebellion, no barricades and no
political killings spent a decade with a military police force in its villages, its
rifle clubs banned, its opposition editors and clergy in prison, and its budget
written by a government the majority had voted against.

## A wall around Copenhagen

Underneath the constitutional argument was a spending argument, and underneath that
was a wall.

Højre held that Copenhagen must be fortified against a modern army, and after 1864
it was hard to call the anxiety unreasonable. Venstre held that a small state
cannot fortify its way out of its neighbours and that the money belonged to
schools, and it was hard to call that unreasonable either. What made it
irreconcilable was that Estrup would not fund the works by taxing wealth and
Venstre would not vote them from the surplus its own obstruction had created.

So the fortification was built by decree. Work on the land defences ran from 1886,
and the **Vestvold** itself was thrown up between **1888 and 1892**: a single
continuous rampart fourteen kilometres long from Utterslev Mose in the north to
Køge Bugt in the south, broken only where an existing road or railway went through.
It was designed by Lieutenant-Colonel E.J. Sommerfeldt, whose saw-toothed plan with
the guns in protected caponiers made him internationally known, and the work was
directed by Estrup's war minister J.J. Bahnson.

It was the largest construction project in Danish history to that date, and it was
built by hand. About **3.15 million cubic metres** of earth and chalk were moved
with shovels, wheelbarrows and tip-wagons by some two thousand local contractors
and day labourers. Concrete mixing machines did not yet exist, so the concrete was
mixed in large tubs and carried. A railway was laid along the rampart road using
worn-out track cast off by the state railways.

The 1894 settlement stopped further building. In 1909, fifteen years later, it was
decided that the land fortification should be abolished from 1922. When war came
in 1914 a security force of fifty thousand men was called up to hold it, and spent
four years waiting for an attack that never arrived. It was formally abolished on
**1 April 1920**.

The wall that broke the Danish constitution was never fired in anger, and it was
obsolete before the men who dug it had grown old.

## 21 October 1885

There was one act of violence in nineteen years of it, and it came from a man
acting entirely alone.

> **Vignette · Julius Rasmussen, Toldbodvej, 21 October 1885**
>
> He was born on Christianshavn on 5 July 1866, twenty-three days before the
> revised constitution was signed. He was a typographer. He had worked on a Venstre
> paper on Lolland-Falster, where he had joined the local Venstre association and
> taken a seat on its committee, and joined the local rifle association, where he
> acquired a pistol.
>
> Back in Copenhagen, setting type for a printer in Tordenskjoldsgade, he watched
> the enormous welcome given on 2 October 1885 to Christen Berg, who had just been
> sentenced to six months for removing a police chief who had tried to silence him.
> Shortly after that he decided to kill the council president.
>
> On the afternoon of the 21st he waited outside Estrup's house at Toldbodvej 26.
> When Estrup came home from the Folketing he asked him, from a couple of paces,
> *Er De Minister Estrup?* Estrup said yes. He fired. **The bullet struck a coat
> button and lodged in the lining.** Estrup said *Hvad, skyder De paa mig?* — what,
> are you shooting at me? — and the second shot went into the gate behind him.
>
> He made no attempt to escape. At the station he confessed at once and said he had
> done it for freedom's sake. He was nineteen: a small, pale, quiet young man who
> by every account had never hurt anyone in his life.
>
> Estrup went on to his dinner engagement that evening and mentioned it to the
> company only when reporters began arriving to find him, in the middle of a rubber
> of whist.
>
> Julius Rasmussen was sentenced to fourteen years' hard labour and sent to Horsens.
> He died there in his cell four years later, aged twenty-three. At his funeral
> there were wreaths from Venstre associations, from three provincial newspapers,
> from students in Copenhagen, and from the typographers of Aalborg and of Vejle.
>
> Julius Rasmussen, typographer · Toldbodvej 26, Copenhagen · 21 October 1885 · [n]

It is worth being clear about how bad the autumn of 1885 was before he acted.
Contemporaries writing about the case afterwards said that the ministry had never
stood on weaker feet than it did in those weeks, and that the conflict had begun to
leave the political sphere and enter private life — meeting against meeting, family
against family. The youth associations and the rifle clubs were forming. A young
man reading a Venstre paper in October 1885 was reading that his country's
government was governing illegally, that its opponents were being jailed for saying
so, and that nothing whatever could be done about it through any channel the
constitution provided. That was accurate.

Everything he wanted, the two shots made worse. Estrup's popularity rose to heights
it had never reached before; a wave of sympathy went through people who had
detested him the week before; and within a fortnight the government had the
gendarmes, the police laws and the press provisorium, all of them justified by what
had happened outside the gate on Toldbodvej.

## The other opposition

Venstre was not the only opposition, and the other two were arranged along the same
seam.

The Social Democrats entered the Folketing in 1884, with two seats. They were
outside the constitutional argument in the sense that neither reading of the text
did anything for the people they represented, and inside it in the sense that a
Højre that could rule against the elected chamber could rule against anybody.

They did not stay at two seats. The party built outward through the 1890s while
the constitutional struggle went on over its head: unions for the unskilled from
1896, a national federation of trade unions in 1898, and in 1899 the four-month
lockout and the September Compromise of chapter 35 §09. By 1901 the Social
Democrats were a parliamentary party with a press, a union movement and a
programme, and they had achieved all of it in a decade when the elected chamber
they sat in did not control the government.

The religious division ran with the political one, closely enough to be the same
line seen twice. Venstre's rural base was substantially Grundtvigian and made heavy
use of the free-congregation law of 1868, which let a congregation choose and pay
its own priest inside the folkekirke — the legal grandchild of chapter 33's
*sognebåndsløsning*. The hierarchy of the established church sat with Højre. A
parish's meeting-house, its rifle club, its cooperative dairy and its Folketing vote
tended to be the same people; the mission house, the rectory and the manor tended
to be the others.

> **Vignette · Line Luplau, Copenhagen, 15 February 1889**
>
> She was sixty-five, and she had come to this late. For years she had been a
> vicar's wife in Varde, where the poverty of the town's poor moved her to found a
> relief society; and it was working in it that persuaded her that women understood
> social need better than men did, which is what turned her toward political rights.
> She was one of the first women in Denmark — possibly the first — to speak at a
> Constitution Day meeting.
>
> On 15 February 1889 the Kvindevalgretsforeningen, which she had founded with the
> municipal schoolteacher Louise Nørlund, held its first public meeting. Fifteen
> hundred people came. It was the first Danish association with women's suffrage and
> nothing else on its programme, and she chaired it until shortly before her death.
>
> Two decisions of hers belong in this chapter rather than in a history of the
> women's movement. She built it deliberately as a **mixed association**, breaking
> with the women-only principle of the society she came from — which cost her the
> friendship of Matilde Bajer, who resigned her own chairmanship over it. And she
> took her support where the argument actually was: from Social Democrat and Venstre
> men, from left-liberal women, and from the "red" working women of the unions. She
> said she did not care whether she was called a Venstre woman or a Social Democrat
> so long as nobody called her a sympathiser of Højre.
>
> She died in 1891. At her burial the red banners of the trade-union women flew over
> the churchyard. Her daughter Marie painted the group portrait of the campaign's
> first days, and in 1917 — two years after the vote was won — it was hung in the
> Folketing.
>
> Line Luplau, vicar's wife and suffragist · Copenhagen · 15 February 1889 · [f]

Three oppositions, then, and one line: the farmers, the workers and the women were
all on the far side of it from the landowners, the officers and the bishops. That
alignment is why the constitutional struggle was never only constitutional, and why
its resolution changed more than a procedure.

## The settlement of 1894

From about 1890 the moderate wings on both sides began to move toward each other,
and the popular protests thinned as they did. In 1894 they made a bargain.

The provisional finance laws stopped. The further build-out of the fortification
that the army wanted was suspended. And **Estrup resigned** after nineteen years.

What the settlement did not do was answer the question. It ended the emergency and
left the gap in the constitution exactly where it had been: nothing was written
down about whether a ministry answers to the Folketing, and nothing prevented a
future government from reading §25 as Estrup had read it.

The reaction was immediate and it came from Venstre's own side. At the election of
1895 the moderates lost their narrow majority for the settlement, the opposition
Venstre groups combined as the **Venstrereformparti**, and the real leader of it
took the chairmanship of the finance committee and resumed the *visnepolitik* in a
harder form: drain the treasury, stop every piece of reform legislation, and pass
nothing until parliamentary government had been conceded.

His name was J.C. Christensen and he had been a village schoolmaster six years
earlier.

## 1901

What finished it was arithmetic. Højre's share of the Folketing had been falling
for a generation and by the election of 1901 it was derisory — a party holding the
government of the country on the votes of a small fraction of those who voted for
the chamber that mattered. The Landsting franchise still protected it in the other
chamber, but no reading of any clause could make the position look like anything
but what it was.

Christian 9. gave way. He did it without conceding the principle: he never accepted
that the constitution required him to appoint a ministry from the Folketing
majority, and he did not say that he was doing so. He simply appointed one.

The ministry took office on **24 July 1901**. Its head was J.H. Deuntzer, a
professor of law at the University of Copenhagen who had been appointed to his
chair at twenty-seven and who took the foreign ministry as well. Christopher Hage
had finance, Ole Hansen — a farmer — agriculture, and **Viggo Hørup**, the
radicals' symbol and the most effective journalist of the opposition, was made
minister of public works, on the advice of the justice minister, so that every wing
of a very broad party should be inside the tent.

> **Vignette · J.C. Christensen, Stadil, 1901**
>
> He was born in 1856 on a heath farm at Påbøl in west Jutland and began working
> life as a herd boy. He trained as a teacher, and from 1886 to 1901 he taught the
> village school at Stadil, near Ringkøbing.
>
> It was the Provisorietid that made him political. He became chairman of his parish
> council in 1888 and was elected to the Folketing for the Ringkøbing seat in January
> 1890, as a follower of Christen Berg; he held it for thirty-four years. When Berg
> died in 1891 it was Christensen who carried on his fight against the moderates'
> policy of negotiating with Højre, and after the settlement of 1894 collapsed at
> the polls it was Christensen who assembled the Venstrereformparti and, from the
> chair of the finance committee, starved the government until it gave way.
>
> In the ministry of 24 July 1901 he was formally the minister for church and
> education. He was in fact the head of the government, and everyone including
> Deuntzer knew it. Copenhageners did not know what to make of his west Jutland
> manner and decided it was peasant cunning; a cartoonist drew him in 1905 with a
> fox behind his ear and the fox stayed for the rest of his career.
>
> He would be council president from 1905 to 1908, and would be brought down by the
> justice minister sitting three places from him at that first cabinet table.
>
> J.C. Christensen, schoolmaster · Stadil, west Jutland · 1901 · [n]

What the ministry then did is the reason this is a chapter ending rather than a
date. J.C. Christensen carried the great school reform of 1903, which opened the
secondary school to children who had been through the village school and connected
the two systems that had run in parallel since Grundtvig's day. And in May 1903 he
carried a law on parish councils: an elected council of at least four members in
every parish, chosen by the baptised who put themselves on a church electoral roll,
with the priest as chairman by right.

That last one settles something this book left open two chapters ago. Article 80 of
the constitution of 1849 had promised that the constitution of the folkekirke would
be settled by law; a commission appointed in 1853 reported eleven years later and
nothing came of it; and the promise had stood undischarged for fifty-four years.
The first parliamentary government in Danish history put a democratic body into
every parish in the country within two years of taking office. It was expressly
temporary, for six years, pending a final settlement of the church's constitution.
That settlement has still not been made.

That last is worth finishing. P.A. Alberti, minister of justice in Denmark's first
parliamentary government and again in Christensen's own, turned out to have been
running a fraud on a scale that has few parallels in Danish public life. He
confessed in 1908, Christensen's government fell with him, and Christensen was
impeached and acquitted with a rebuke for negligence. The first ministry appointed
by a parliamentary majority contained, from its first day, the man who would
destroy the man who made it.

The change of system did not put parliamentarism into the constitution. It was not
written down until 1953, where this book ends. For fifty-two years the most
important rule in Danish politics was a rule everybody obeyed and nobody had
enacted — which, if you have read the last nine sections, is a very Danish way for
the argument to have ended.

## Coda — Part H

Part H opened in 1814 with a state that had just lost Norway, defaulted on its
debts and been left holding two provinces that belonged constitutionally to
Germany. It closes in 1901 with a small, prosperous, democratic country of about
two and a half million people that has lost the provinces too.

The line through it is not the wars, though there were two and the second was a
catastrophe. It is a long argument about who a Dane is and who gets to decide for
him, conducted in four places at once. In Slesvig it was conducted in language:
which tongue the church and the school used, and whose survey you believed. In the
Rigsdag it was conducted in constitutional law, and turned on a silence in a
document that neither side had noticed until they needed it. In the parishes it was
conducted in institutions — the dairy, the meeting-house, the mission house, the
rifle club — and the answer arrived at there was more radical than anything in
Copenhagen: one member, one vote, whatever the size of the herd. And in the
tenements and on the emigrant ships it was conducted by the people none of the
other three answers included.

The part has an unusual shape for a national history, and it is worth naming.
Denmark's two great constitutional gains, in 1849 and 1901, sit on either side of
its worst defeat, and neither of them was a consequence of it. The June
constitution came out of a European revolution and a war Denmark won; the change of
system came out of a farming economy that had reorganised itself around butter. In
between, 1864 cost two-fifths of the territory and the franchise of the upper
house, and only the first of those was taken by foreigners.

What is carried into Part I is therefore not one thread but four. A parliamentary
convention with no text behind it, which will hold for half a century and then be
written down. A labour settlement signed in 1899 that is still in force. Two
hundred thousand people — or a hundred and seventy thousand — under a government
that will conscript their sons in 1914, and a promise of a vote that Denmark itself
formally surrendered in 1907 and will nevertheless get in 1920. And a woman who
died in 1891 with union banners over her grave, whose association had one item on
its programme, and who would have to wait until 1915.

## Danish terms, by section

**§01 — two chambers, two countries**

- **Højre** and **Venstre** — "the Right" and "the Left". Not parties in the modern
  sense at the start of this chapter and increasingly so by its end. Venstre is the
  party of the farmers and the Folketing; Højre of the landowners, officials and
  the Landsting. Venstre is the ancestor of today's Venstre, Højre of today's
  Konservative.
- *`Folketinget` and `Landstinget` are glossed in chapter 33 — reference, do not
  re-gloss.*

**§02 — the question nobody had answered**

- **parlamentarisme** — here specifically *folketingsparlamentarisme*: the rule
  that a government must have, or at least not have against it, a majority in the
  Folketing. Demanded from 1873, conceded in practice in 1901, written into the
  constitution in 1953.
- **provisorisk lov** — a provisional law, issued by the government under §25 in
  urgent cases when the Rigsdag is not sitting, and laid before it afterwards.
- **finanslov** — the annual finance act. Under §48 it goes to the Folketing first,
  which is the whole of Venstre's constitutional argument.

**§03 — Estrup**

- **konseilspræsident** — council president; the office called prime minister
  after 1918.
- **visnepolitik** — the "withering policy": Venstre's tactic of voting down every
  expenditure not strictly necessary, to starve the government into resignation.
  It filled the treasury instead.

**§04 — ruling without a budget**

- **Provisorietiden** — the years of provisional government. Usually given as
  1885–1894, sometimes as 1877–1894 from the first, much smaller provisional
  finance law. Both datings are in the literature and the difference is real: 1877
  was a technique tested, 1885 was a technique adopted.

**§05 — the gendarmes**

- **gendarm** — a member of the military gendarmerie corps created by provisional
  law on 27 October 1885 to assist the police. Light-blue uniforms, hence *de blå
  gendarmer*.
- **riffelforening** — rifle association. Grown up beside the Grundtvigian
  meeting-houses and closed down by the *Riffellov* of 5 May 1885.

**§06 — a wall around Copenhagen**

- **Vestvolden** — the western rampart, 1888–92: fourteen kilometres from Utterslev
  Mose to Køge Bugt, and the largest Danish building project of its century.

**§09 — the settlement of 1894**

- **Forliget 1894** — the settlement that ended the provisional laws and removed
  Estrup, and answered no constitutional question at all.

**§10 — 1901**

- **Systemskiftet** — "the change of system": the appointment on 24 July 1901 of a
  ministry drawn from the Folketing majority.

## Meanwhile in Europe

**Berlin, 1862–1866.** Prussia's chambers refused Bismarck a budget for army
reform, and Bismarck governed without one for four years on the argument that the
constitution provided no procedure for the case and the state must be administered
regardless — the *Lückentheorie*, the theory of the gap. He was retrospectively
indemnified in 1866 by a parliament that had just watched him win two wars. The
resemblance to Denmark twenty years later is not a coincidence and it was noticed
at the time: the same reasoning from the same kind of silence, and the provisional
laws of October 1885 were modelled on the German emergency statutes. The man who
took Slesvig from Denmark supplied the method by which Denmark was then governed
against its own majority.

**Kristiania, 1884.** Norway got there seventeen years before Denmark, and by the
opposite route. Where the Danish Folketing could only refuse and wait, the
Norwegian Storting impeached the Selmer ministry before the *Riksrett*, removed it,
and forced the king to appoint a government from the parliamentary majority. The
difference is procedural and it is the whole difference: Norway's constitution
contained a mechanism for removing a government and Denmark's did not. Danish
Venstre watched it closely and could not copy it.

## Checkpoints

**Before §04 — where we are.** The 1866 revision produced two chambers returning
two different countries, and the constitution never said which of them a government
answers to. Venstre has demanded parliamentary government since 1873 and has been
voting down expenditure to force it. Estrup has been in office since 1875 and has
tested the emergency clause once, in 1877.

**Before §07 — where we are.** Since 1 April 1885 the country has been governed on
provisional finance laws written by the government and rejected by the elected
chamber. The rifle associations are closed. The fortification of Copenhagen is
being built with money the Folketing refused.

**Before §10 — where we are.** The gendarmes have been in the villages, an editor
and a folk-high-school principal have been in prison, one man has fired two shots
and made everything worse, and the settlement of 1894 has ended the emergency
without answering the question. Venstre has regrouped under a schoolmaster from
Ringkøbing and gone back to starving the treasury.

## Myth-check

**"1901 gave Denmark parliamentary government."**

It gave Denmark the practice and not the rule, and the distinction held for
fifty-two years.

What happened on 24 July 1901 was that a king appointed a ministry from the
Folketing majority. What did not happen was any change to the text. Christian 9.
did not concede that the constitution obliged him; he was careful not to, and his
successors inherited the same unaltered clauses. Parliamentarism was not written
into the Danish constitution until **1953**, which is where this book ends.

So for half a century the most important rule in Danish political life had no legal
existence. It was obeyed because breaking it had been tried, at length, by a
determined and intelligent government with the king and a chamber and two
professors behind it — and had cost that government its majority, its reputation
and finally its office. The rule held because everyone had watched what happened to
the man who tested it.

There is a second, smaller myth attached, and it is the mirror image. That Estrup
simply broke the constitution. He did not simply break it: he used a real clause,
submitted his laws for the rejection the text required, held elections and lost
them and went on. What he demonstrated is something less dramatic and more
uncomfortable — that a constitution which describes a disagreement but provides no
way to settle it is not a constraint on a government willing to sit still. The gap
was the fault. Estrup only stood in it.

## Summary

The revision of 1866 produced a Folketing carried by Venstre and a Landsting
carried by Højre, and from 1873 Venstre demanded that governments be appointed from
the Folketing majority. The constitution did not say whether they should be. Both
sides read the silence honestly and neither could be argued out of it.

Estrup governed for nineteen years from 1875, and from 1 April 1885 he sent the
Rigsdag home each year without a budget and issued a provisional finance law of his
own writing under an emergency clause used once before, in 1853. The elected
chamber formally rejected the first of them in January 1886, and nothing followed,
because the constitution provided a way to say no and no way for the no to matter.

The conflict left the chamber and entered the country: rifle associations closed by
decree, a military gendarmerie in light blue posted into the villages, a press law
against making the government contemptible, and an editor and a folk-high-school
principal in prison. One nineteen-year-old typographer fired two shots at Estrup in
October 1885, and every consequence of it favoured Estrup.

What the money was for was a fourteen-kilometre rampart round Copenhagen, dug by
hand between 1888 and 1892, never fired in anger and abolished in 1920. The
treasury was full the whole time; the argument was never about whether Denmark
could afford it but about who was entitled to decide.

The settlement of 1894 removed Estrup and answered nothing, and on 24 July 1901 a
king who never conceded the principle appointed a ministry from the Folketing
majority anyway. Parliamentarism was not written into the constitution until 1953.

## Visit

**Vestvolden**, from Utterslev Mose to Køge Bugt. Fourteen kilometres of it are
still there and much of it is a park; the caponiers, the moat and the rampart road
are walkable end to end, and the profile Sommerfeldt was famous for is best seen
from above.

**Esplanaden 34–36**, Copenhagen. Toldbodvej 26 in 1885. Estrup came home along
this pavement and the gate is the one the second bullet went into.

**Horsens.** The prison Julius Rasmussen was sent to is now a museum.

**Christiansborg**, Copenhagen. Marie Luplau's *Fra Kvindevalgretskampens første
Dage* has hung in the Folketing since 1917. Line Luplau is standing in the middle
of it.

**Stadil**, near Ringkøbing. The village school where the man who ran Denmark's
first parliamentary government taught until the year he entered it.

**Fredericiagade**, Copenhagen. The Eastern High Court now. The Rigsdag met here
after Christiansborg burned, and it was in these rooms that the two chambers stopped
speaking to each other.
