# Chapter 35 — Industry, cooperatives, emigration and labour, c. 1870–1901

*Draft, ten sections. Prose only; apparatus follows the sections.*

## Two chapters over one span

This chapter and the next cover the same thirty years and divide them by subject
rather than by date. This one is the country: what Danes grew, sold, believed,
joined and left. The next is the politics: a parliament that could not resolve a
disagreement between its two chambers, and thirty years of governing round the
problem.

They are separated because they happened to different people in the same parish at
the same time, and because running them together would produce a chapter in which
a dairy is founded, a government rules by decree, a man emigrates and a
constitution is not amended, all in the same paragraph. The reader needs both.
Reading them in order is the intended way; reading §09 of this chapter beside §07
of the next is the other one.

One warning about the shape. The story here is usually told as a Danish success —
farmers ruined by cheap American grain who reinvented themselves, invented the
cooperative, and sold butter to England. That story is true, and it is a story
about the people who owned land. This chapter tries to keep both halves in view:
the parish that founded the dairy, and the four families in the same parish who
were not asked to join and whose sons went to Nebraska.

## The grain that stopped paying

For most of the century covered by chapter 32, Danish farming had sold grain.
Barley and wheat went out through the ports, the price was set in Europe, and the
enclosure and freehold reforms of the 1780s had produced a countryside of
individual farms geared to producing it.

From about 1875 that stopped working. The American plains had been opened by
railways built on land the federal government gave to the companies, which sold
it on to settlers; Russian grain came west; steam shipping made both cheap to
move. The price of grain in Europe fell and went on falling. A Danish farmer
selling barley in 1885 was competing with land that had cost almost nothing,
worked by people who had arrived on it the year before.

The effect was not even. Large farms and manor estates could absorb it. The
small and middling farms — the ones the reforms of the 1780s and 1790s had made —
were being distanced, and it was among them that something had to change.

What changed was the direction of the trade. If grain would not sell, grain could
be eaten: fed to cows and pigs, and sold again as butter and bacon at a price the
world market had not collapsed. This is the pivot the rest of the chapter turns
on, and it was not a Danish invention. What Denmark did, and what almost nowhere
else did on the same scale, was work out how a farmer with eight cows could take
part in it.

There was a Danish complication on top of the European one. The loss of 1864 had
taken with it the land route south: the direct road into the German market now
crossed a customs frontier at the Kongeå. Denmark's remaining outlet was the sea,
and at the end of the sea was England.

The reversal, when it came, was total. A country that had exported grain for
centuries ended the period **importing** it — buying foreign grain cheaply, feeding
it to Danish animals, and exporting the animals' product. The cheap American
harvest that ruined the Danish grain farmer became the raw material of the Danish
dairy farmer, and the same ships carried it.

Two things outside anyone's control pushed the timing. The continuous cream
separator arrived at the end of the 1870s and solved the technical problem of
handling milk from many suppliers at once — which had been the reason joint
dairies kept failing. And in **1881 Germany banned the import of live cattle.**
Denmark's nearest market for the animal itself closed, and what remained was the
milk.

## Hjedding, 1882

Ølgod is in west Jutland, on poor sandy ground, and in 1882 twenty-six farmers in
the southern part of the parish agreed to build a dairy together and hired a
dairyman to run it. It opened on 10 June.

The story of how they got there begins, in almost every telling, with a young man
nobody can identify. In the winter of 1881–82 he travelled the Varde district
offering lectures on dairying and demonstrations of how to get the best butter out
of the district's milk, and early in 1882 he invited the neighbourhood to
instruction in souring and churning cream at the old inn at Ølgod. Attempts have
been made to work out who he was. That the founding scene of the Danish
cooperative movement opens with an unnamed stranger is worth noticing, and it says
more about how the movement later told its own story than about 1882. The building is still there: about
ninety square metres of red brick, three rooms — a room for the dairyman, a
cooling and kneading room, and the dairy hall with the weighing-in, the separator,
the churn, a steam engine and a water pump.

None of that is why anyone remembers it. Joint dairies already existed; there had
been one at Kaslunde on Funen since 1875 that was very like this. What Hjedding
produced was a set of rules, and the rules went everywhere.

> **Vignette · Niels Hansen Uhd, Ølgod, 1882**
>
> He is a farmer in Ølgod parish, and he is the man who wrote it. The standard
> account of the Danish cooperative movement names the dairyman, J. Stilling-
> Andersen — a capable and energetic man who set up the plant, ran it, and even
> bought milk privately from farms in the district that had not joined. He is in
> the photographs. But the laws of Hjedding Andelsmejeri, which became the model
> for the movement, were drawn up in essentials by Uhd.
>
> He did not compose them from nothing. He took the wording from the recently
> revised rules of the local savings bank and the parish fire-insurance
> association, and changed the subject from money paid in to milk delivered. The
> constitutional document of Danish cooperation is a village insurance club's
> constitution with one noun replaced.
>
> Four things are in it. Every member must deliver all the milk his cows give, and
> deliver it clean, unskimmed and not from a sick cow — adulterate it and you are
> expelled and fined ten kroner a cow. Every supplier is paid the same price for
> the same quantity, whoever he is. Every member has one vote at the general
> meeting **regardless of how many cows he owns.** And all of them stand behind
> the debts of all the others, *alle som én og én som alle*, with everything they
> own.
>
> The Danish summary of the third rule is better than any translation of it: they
> voted *ikke efter høveder, men efter hoveder* — not by heads of cattle, but by
> heads.
>
> Niels Hansen Uhd, farmer · Ølgod parish, west Jutland · 1882 · [n]

The fourth rule is the one that made the other three possible and it is the one
usually left out. Unlimited joint liability is what let a group of small farmers
with no capital borrow enough to buy a steam engine and a separator: the bank was
not lending against the dairy, it was lending against every farm in the parish.
It also meant that a man joining put his land behind his neighbours' judgement.
That is a serious thing to sign, and it explains both the movement's speed and its
carefulness about who was let in.

One consequence of the first rule is rarely stated and follows directly from its
wording. *All* the milk went to the dairy. Butter-making had been done in the
farmhouse, and on most farms it had been done by women — the farmer's wife and the
dairymaids, whose skill decided what the butter fetched. The contract moved that
work out of the house and into a building operated by a paid male dairyman, and it
moved the income with it. The cooperative gave every member one vote and took a
trade away from people who did not have one.

Within twenty years there were more than a thousand cooperative dairies in
Denmark. The same rules were then applied to slaughterhouses, to feed and
fertiliser buying, to egg export, to village shops. It is worth being exact about
what was invented at Hjedding, because it was not cooperation and it was not
dairying. It was a constitution.

## Butter, bacon and the English breakfast

The market was Britain, and Britain was changing in a way that suited Denmark
exactly. British industrial workers were being fed on cheap imported grain, which
freed money for something to put on the bread; British farming had been left
undefended by free trade; and the British working week now ended with a cooked
breakfast that wanted bacon and eggs.

Denmark was four days from London by steamer and could deliver a perishable good
in a condition the customer would accept. But only if it was the same every week,
and that was the real problem. Butter made on four hundred separate farms is four
hundred different products, some of them rancid. Butter made in one dairy from the
milk of four hundred cows is one product, and it can be graded, branded, and sold
forward.

So the cooperative was not only a way of sharing a machine. It was a way of
manufacturing consistency, and consistency was the thing Britain paid for. The
quality clauses in the Hjedding contract — clean pails, no milk from sick cows,
nothing from a newly calved cow before the seventh milking — read like fussiness
and are actually the product specification.

The same logic then ran through the pig, and it ran fast. On 14 July 1887 five
hundred farmers around Horsens founded Denmark's first cooperative pig
slaughterhouse; the first pig was killed on 22 December, with a reporter from
*Horsens Folkeblad* present to describe it being hauled up by one leg. In its first
year the slaughterhouse killed **23,400 pigs and sold 1.1 million kilos of pork.**
Others followed everywhere, the Danish pig was bred toward the shape the English
side of bacon wanted, and in 1902 three of the cooperative slaughterhouses set up a
selling company in England and undertook to ship four hundred bales of bacon a
week between them. By 1914 its successor was shipping seven hundred and fifty
tonnes a week.

The state came in behind the brand. The Lur mark was registered as a butter mark
in 1901 and handed to the government a few years later as the official quality
stamp, because by then a single bad consignment damaged everyone's price and the
industry wanted an authority that could stop it leaving the country. By the end of the century Denmark had
reorganised its agriculture, its transport and part of its law around the
breakfast habits of a foreign working class.

It made the country rich, and it made it dependent in a way that would matter
enormously in 1914 and again in 1940. A national economy built on selling one
perishable thing to one customer across one sea has exactly one serious risk, and
Denmark took it deliberately.

## Mission and meeting-house

Something else was dividing the same parishes, along a line that had nothing to do
with cows and everything to do with who a Dane was.

The revival movements of chapter 32 had produced two answers. The Grundtvigians
took the cheerful one: that a Christian people is a people first, that the word
is spoken and sung rather than enforced, that the school and the song are the
work. The other became **Indre Mission**, whose answer was that most of the
baptised were not saved, and that the business of a Christian was to wake them.

Its origins are less respectable than its later authority. The movement began in
1853 as a **lay** association founded by a Zealand blacksmith, Jens Larsen, who
travelled the country preaching. In 1861 the clergy took it over. At a meeting
at Stenlille the old association was dissolved and reconstituted as the
*Kirkelig Forening for den indre Mission i Danmark*, with a board that filled its
own vacancies and was now dominated by priests. Jens Larsen was excluded from the
thing he had founded.

The man who ran it for the next forty years was Vilhelm Beck, a chaplain from a
clerical family, the real leader from 1861 and the formal chairman only from 1881.
He was a superb speaker who could draw ten thousand people to an open-air meeting.
He was also, by conviction, not a democrat: he held that democracy inside Indre
Mission would split it, and he ran the organisation in a form his biographers
describe as a survival of absolutism.

Hold that beside the previous section. In the same decades, often in the same
parish, Danish farmers were writing constitutions that gave every member one vote
regardless of his wealth, and the mission house at the other end of the village was
being governed on the principle that voting causes schism. Both movements were
built by the same people out of the same revival.

The division was physical. Grundtvigian districts built a *forsamlingshus*, a
meeting-house, and put a rifle club and a folk high school beside it. Mission
districts built a *missionshus*. Two buildings, two circles of families, often two
schools, and — where the split ran deep — sometimes two cooperative societies in
one parish, because men who would not pray together would not audit each other's
accounts either.

Where the line fell had a mechanism, and it was political. Beck refused to involve
Indre Mission in worldly politics at all. In districts with a strong political
life — Salling, much of the Venstre countryside — that refusal was disqualifying,
and Grundtvigianism held the ground. The folk high schools grew there: Askov,
founded in 1865 to replace Rødding, which had been left on the wrong side of the
new border. Chapter 32's school idea comes back with a war in between and forty
kilometres further north.

By Beck's death in 1901 — the year this chapter closes — the association he had
built from a fund to pay one missionary's wages had more than a hundred and fifty
employees, four hundred mission houses, schools and seminaries, and adherents in
every part of the country.

## Leaving

Between 1868 and 1900 about three hundred and nine thousand Danes left for America
and other overseas destinations, out of a population of some two million. It was a
smaller movement than Norway's or Sweden's — Danes were about one in seven of the
Scandinavian total — and the usual explanation is that conditions at home were
better. That is true and it is not much comfort to the three hundred thousand.

We know who they were with unusual precision, and the reason is a scandal. Danish
emigration agents had been swindling their customers, and a law of 1 May 1868
required the Copenhagen police director to license every agent in the country and
authorise every overseas ticket. The particulars were copied into police
registers — ninety volumes, running from 1868 to 1940. Denmark has one of the best
emigration records in Europe because its emigrants were being cheated.

Read against the previous three sections, those registers say something the
cooperative story does not. Of Danish migrants between 1868 and 1900, **more than
four in ten were agricultural labourers and about one in four were urban working
class** — for women, above all, servants. The people who left were not the farmers
who founded the dairies. They were the people the dairy did not ask.

The pull was money. Danish wages in the 1870s were about a third of American ones,
and even in the early 1900s only a little over half. The push was arithmetic: the
population was growing faster than the land could absorb, and a farm passes to one
son.

The flow was not steady. It peaked in 1882 at about eleven thousand four hundred
in a single year, fell to some four thousand two hundred by 1885, and rose again
to a second peak of around nine thousand seven hundred in 1891 and 1892. Nobody
who has looked at those numbers has found a single cause for the shape of them.

## The city outside the walls

Copenhagen's ramparts were given up as a defence line in 1856 and the city was
allowed to build outward. What followed was the fastest change in the way Danes
lived that had happened since the villages were broken up.

The new districts — Nørrebro, Vesterbro, Østerbro — went up as speculative
tenement blocks: four and five storeys, a courtyard, a privy and a pump, one or
two rooms to a family, built fast and let dear. The people filling them were
coming in from exactly the countryside the last three sections have described,
and often from the same families whose other sons had gone to America. The choice
facing a farm labourer's son in 1885 was Nebraska or Nørrebro, and the two were
priced against each other.

What the tenements were like is documented because the doctors and the statisticians
went into them. Two rooms held a family and often a lodger as well, taken in to make
the rent; the water came from a tap in the yard and the privy was there too; and
tuberculosis, which needs crowding and poor light more than it needs anything else,
did what it does in such places. Infant mortality in the new districts ran well
above the countryside the families had come from. Lægeforeningens Boliger, where
chapter 34's Ilia Fibiger raised her foster daughters, had been built in the 1850s
precisely as a demonstration of the alternative, and it stayed a demonstration:
private builders went on putting up blocks around courtyards because that was what
paid.

Danish industry in this period was real but modest, and it is worth saying so
plainly because the national story tends to inflate it. There were shipyards,
breweries, engineering works, a tobacco trade, textiles in Odense; Burmeister &
Wain and Carlsberg are the names that survive. But as a Danish trade unionist told
an international congress in 1899, Denmark was not an industrial country but an
agricultural one whose industry was new and relatively small.

That smallness matters for what comes next. A labour movement in a country of
small workplaces cannot win by shutting down a few great factories, because there
are none. It has to organise very widely and very tightly instead, and that is
exactly what the Danish one did.

## Fælleden, 5 May 1872

In April 1872 the masons of Copenhagen went on strike for shorter hours, and a few
hundred labourers came out with them. The Danish section of the International
Working Men's Association — founded the previous autumn by Louis Pio, his cousin
Harald Brix and the schoolteacher Povl Geleff — had held back from the strike.
When it began to look like ending in defeat, and the defeat like a humiliation for
the new movement, Pio called a public meeting on Nørre Fælled for Sunday 5 May to
raise money.

He announced it in *Socialisten* on 2 May with an article headed *Maalet er fuldt!*
— the measure is full — which ended by telling the employers that they had given
the workers a bitter drink for thousands of years, and that they should take care,
because one more drop would make it overflow.

The government and the police read that as a call to revolution. The police
director, Vilhelm Crone, banned the meeting with posters in the streets, which was
unconstitutional, and in the night before the 5th he arrested all three leaders:
Pio the grand master, Brix the secretary, Geleff the treasurer.

Several thousand workers came to the Common anyway. Police and mounted hussars
went in with drawn sabres, and there was a general brawl. Afterwards it was
reported that **seventy-four hussars and twenty-three policemen had been injured by
stones.** Nobody was killed. There is no return for the workers' injuries, which
tells you who was counting.

The three were tried and convicted the following year of working for the
introduction of a socialist ordering of the state. The sentences are given
variously in the sources as two and a half to three and a half years, three to
five, and four to six; the International was banned outright on 14 August 1872.

The battle of the Common has since become the founding myth of the Danish labour
movement, and the myth is not false so much as differently shaped than the day.
It was not a revolution attempted and crushed. It was a fundraiser for a failing
strike, banned illegally, met with cavalry, and turned by the prosecution that
followed into the thing the prosecution said it was. The Danish state took a small
socialist society with a few thousand members and, by banning it and imprisoning
its leaders for high treason, taught the next generation that the movement was
worth that much trouble.

## 1899

What was rebuilt after 1872 was rebuilt differently: not as one society with a
grand master but as trade unions, a party, and eventually a structure joining
them. The Social Democratic party entered the Folketing. The unskilled organised
separately — Dansk Arbejdsmandsforbund from 1896 — and in 1898 the unions formed a
single national federation, **De samvirkende Fagforbund.** The employers had
already formed theirs.

> **Vignette · Olivia Nielsen, Copenhagen, 1899**
>
> She was born in Helsingør in November 1854, the daughter of a farmhand. She
> married a factory worker at twenty, already pregnant, and had nine children, of
> whom two died as infants. To feed them she worked — as a laundress, and later
> washing bottles. In the 1880s the family moved to Copenhagen because there was
> work there.
>
> Kvindeligt Arbejderforbund had been founded in 1885 for cleaners and
> washerwomen, and from 1890 took in women factory workers. She joined its
> committee in 1891 and was elected its president in 1892. What she did first was
> not rhetoric: she introduced monthly meetings and regular subscriptions, which
> fixed the union's finances, and then brought in outside speakers so that
> laundresses heard from people they would not otherwise meet.
>
> From 1898 she sat for her union in the council of the new national federation —
> a bottle-washer in the room where the Danish labour market was being negotiated.
>
> In 1899, with half the country's industrial workers locked out, the organised
> women of Denmark levied **ten per cent of their own poor earnings** for the men
> shut out of work, on top of what the federation had already assessed them. There
> were about eleven thousand of them: textile workers, tailors, cigar makers,
> bookbinders, newspaper deliverers, shoemakers, corset sewers.
>
> In 1901 she founded the national Kvindeligt Arbejderforbund and led it until she
> died in 1910. In 1909 she was elected to Copenhagen's city council.
>
> Olivia Nielsen, laundress and bottle-washer · Copenhagen · 1899 · [f][n]

The party came with them. Social Democrats entered the Folketing in 1884 — two of
them — and went on gaining seats through the 1890s while the constitutional
struggle of the next chapter was going on over their heads. The movement banned in
1872 as high treason was, within a generation, a parliamentary party with a press,
a network of unions, a cooperative wholesale society of its own, and a leadership
that had concluded that the way to power in Denmark ran through elections.

The confrontation came in the spring of 1899. On 15 May the employers sent the
federation eight demands as the price of calling off a threatened lockout of
thirty thousand men; on the 18th the federation rejected them; on 24 May the
lockout began. It ran for four months and at its height shut out more than half of
all organised workers in Denmark. Measured against the size of Danish industry it
was, as a Danish delegate told a congress in London that summer, the largest
industrial conflict fought anywhere.

Neither side broke the other, which is the important fact about it. What ended it
was the **September Compromise of 5 September 1899**, an agreement between the two
federations in which each recognised the other's right to exist and to organise;
the employers' right to direct and distribute work was acknowledged; and
procedures were fixed for how disputes would be handled and when a conflict could
lawfully be begun.

It is the oldest agreement of its kind in the world and its principles still frame
the Danish labour market. It was reached because both sides had discovered, over
four months, that they were strong enough to hurt each other and not strong enough
to win — which is the same discovery the two chambers of the Rigsdag were failing
to make in the same years, and the subject of the next chapter.

## Nordslesvig under Prussia

Everything above happened north of the Kongeå. South of it, the same thirty years
went differently for the people chapter 34 handed over.

They were not a minority in the modern sense; they were most of the population of
the northern districts, and they behaved like a nation under administration. They
elected Danish members to the Prussian Landtag and the Reichstag and went on doing
it for fifty years. They founded newspapers — *Flensborg Avis*, and *Hejmdal* in
Aabenraa — and read them. They built their own credit associations and their own
assembly houses, and applied the cooperative rules of §03 to a province where
cooperating was itself a political act.

The Prussian state responded with law. Danish was pushed out of the schools by
stages until instruction was in German except for religion; associations were
watched and dissolved; assembly permissions were refused. The pressure became
systematic under the Oberpräsident **Ernst Matthias von Köller**, whose period from
1897 to 1901 gave the policy its Danish name. Danish associations were dissolved in
series. Danish schools were put under pressure that contemporaries called unheard
of. And Danish servants and optants were expelled from the province in numbers —
people who had been born there, sent across a border to a country they had never
lived in.

The instrument that made the expulsions possible was the choice chapter 34
described. An **optant** had kept his Danish citizenship and stayed. He paid
Prussian taxes and obeyed Prussian law and had none of a Prussian's rights,
including the right not to be removed.

> **Vignette · H.P. Hanssen, Aabenraa, 1898**
>
> He was born in 1862 at Nørremølle, a farm on Sundeved — two years before the war,
> on the peninsula where it was lost. He went to school in Germany and then to
> **Askov**, the folk high school founded in 1865 to replace the one left on the
> wrong side of the border. The school Denmark built to replace what it had lost
> educated the man who led what it had lost.
>
> In 1888 he settled at Sønderborg as a journalist, helped found the Vælgerforening
> for Nordslesvig and became its secretary, and in 1893 moved to Aabenraa and
> bought the newspaper *Hejmdal*. He helped set up a school association in 1892 and
> a long list of other associations, all of them for the same purpose, all of them
> constituted with a careful eye on German association law, which he had studied
> precisely so that they could not be dissolved on a technicality.
>
> By 1898 he sits in the Prussian Landtag and the Köller policy is at its height,
> which means his associations are being dissolved anyway. He had been arrested in
> 1895 and charged with high treason and let go again; there would be fines and
> prison sentences later, the worst of them three months.
>
> What he argued for was the thing his own side found hardest. The old policy had
> been protest — refuse, abstain, and let the young men emigrate rather than serve
> in the German army. Hanssen said that was surrender by attrition: that Danishness
> in Nordslesvig was strengthened by staying and by taking the burden, including
> the uniform. In 1914 he would put it as *do your duty and demand your right*, and
> six thousand of those men would not come home.
>
> The other view had a paper too. Jens Jessen edited *Flensborg Avis* and led the
> protest wing, and the two editors disagreed in print for twenty years about
> whether a people under occupation should participate or refuse.
>
> H.P. Hanssen, editor of *Hejmdal* · Aabenraa · 1898 · [-]

And then there were his children.

A child born in Nordslesvig between 1864 and 1898 to Danish citizens living there
was Danish by descent and Prussian by birthplace and was claimed by neither. They
were called *de hjemløse*, the homeless. They had no civil rights of any kind and
were subject to whatever the authorities decided. There were close to **ten
thousand** of them.

The matter was settled in 1907. Germany agreed to recognise the stateless as
German citizens if they applied, and about three thousand six hundred did. The
price, which Denmark paid, was to accept formally that Article 5 of the Peace of
Prague — the promise of a vote, made in 1866, abrogated between Berlin and Vienna
in 1878 — was extinguished for good.

So the last shred of the promise chapter 34 ended on was not taken from Denmark.
It was sold, by Denmark, to buy ten thousand children a nationality; and the
nationality on offer was German.

A boy born at Haderslev in 1890 to parents who had chosen to stay Danish reached
his seventeenth year holding no passport of any country. In 1907 he could become a
German. In 1914 he would be conscripted into the German army as one, and about six
thousand men from these districts would not come back. In 1920 the districts would
vote, in a plebiscite the 1907 agreement had formally given up, and come home.

He did not know any of that. What he knew was that he had been born in a house in a
country that would not have him.

## Danish terms, by section

**§01 — two chapters over one span**

- **andelsbevægelse** — the cooperative movement: dairies, slaughterhouses,
  buying societies and shops owned by their own suppliers or customers, on the
  rules written at Hjedding in 1882.

**§02 — the grain that stopped paying**

- **kapitelstakst** — the officially published average grain price for a district,
  computed since the seventeenth century and still computed today. It is the
  series that would show the collapse of the 1870s, and see figure 1's note on why
  it is not drawn here.

**§03 — Hjedding**

- **andelsmejeri** — cooperative dairy. Owned by the farmers who supply it, paid
  for out of their joint and unlimited liability, and governed one member one
  vote.
- **centrifuge** — the continuous cream separator, from the late 1870s. It made
  milk from many suppliers processable together, which is what joint dairies had
  been failing at.
- **interessentskab** — a partnership in which the partners are personally and
  jointly liable. Hjedding was one, and the liability was the point.

**§04 — butter, bacon**

- **brugsforening** — cooperative shop, on the same rules, buying for its members
  instead of selling for a profit.

**§05 — mission and meeting-house**

- **Indre Mission** — "inner mission", founded 1853 as a lay society and
  reconstituted under clerical control in 1861: a revival movement inside the
  folkekirke aimed at converting the baptised.
- **missionshus** and **forsamlingshus** — the mission house and the meeting-house.
  The two buildings are the movement's social geography made visible; a village
  with both had two of most other things as well.
- **valgmenighed** — an elected congregation: a Grundtvigian body that chose and
  paid its own priest while remaining inside the folkekirke. The legal descendant
  of chapter 33's *sognebåndsløsning*.

**§06 — leaving**

- **husmand** — a cottager, holding too little land to live on it, and working for
  others. The largest single group in the Danish countryside and largely outside
  §03.
- **tyende** — living-in farm and household servants, hired by the year under a
  law that made leaving early a punishable offence. The other group outside §03,
  and heavily represented among the emigrants.

**§07 — the city outside the walls**

- **brokvarter** — the districts built outside Copenhagen's demolished ramparts
  after 1856: Nørrebro, Vesterbro, Østerbro.

**§08 — Fælleden**

- **Internationale** — the Danish section of the International Working Men's
  Association, founded 1871 and banned 14 August 1872.

**§09 — 1899**

- **De samvirkende Fagforbund** — the national federation of trade unions, 1898.
- **Septemberforliget** — the September Compromise of 5 September 1899, the
  agreement that ended the great lockout and still frames the Danish labour
  market: mutual recognition, the employer's right to direct work, and rules for
  how and when a conflict may be begun.

**§10 — Nordslesvig**

- **optant** — *glossed in chapter 34; the consequences arrive here.*
- **de hjemløse** — "the homeless": children born in Nordslesvig between 1864 and
  1898 to Danish citizens living there, holding no citizenship of any country.
- **Køllerpolitikken** — the Köller policy, 1897–1901, named for the Prussian
  Oberpräsident: dissolution of Danish associations, pressure on Danish schools,
  and expulsion of Danish servants and optants.

## Meanwhile in Europe

**Berlin, 1883–1889.** Bismarck gave Germany sickness insurance in 1883, accident
insurance in 1884 and old-age and invalidity pensions in 1889 — the first modern
welfare state, built expressly to draw working men away from socialism. Denmark
produced its own old-age relief in 1891, and the difference is instructive. The
German schemes were contributory insurance, tied to employment and paid for by
employer and worker. The Danish act was tax-financed relief, paid to the deserving
old out of public funds and, crucially, without the loss of civil rights that came
with taking poor relief — which had cost a man his vote since 1849. Denmark took
the political argument and left the German machinery, and the shape it chose in
1891 is recognisably the shape of what it built in the twentieth century.

**The American plains, the same decades.** What the emigrants of §06 found was
land sold to them by railway companies that had been given it, in Iowa, Nebraska,
Minnesota and Wisconsin. Danes did not settle as densely as Norwegians or Swedes
and assimilated faster, partly because there were fewer of them and partly because
they arrived as individuals rather than as congregations. By 1900 the number of
people in the United States who had been born in Denmark or born there to Danish
parents was equal to about fifteen per cent of the population of Denmark.

## Checkpoints

**Before §05 — where we are.** Grain stopped paying from about 1875, Germany shut
the door on live cattle in 1881, and the separator made joint dairying work. In
1882 a parish in west Jutland wrote a set of rules — deliver everything, be paid
alike, one man one vote, and stand behind each other's debts — and within twenty
years there were more than a thousand dairies running on them, selling to Britain.

**Before §08 — where we are.** The same movement that gave every member one vote
was not open to the cottagers and servants who made up much of the countryside,
and it was largely their sons and daughters who left: more than four in ten of the
emigrants were farm labourers. Those who did not leave went to the new tenement
districts outside Copenhagen's demolished ramparts.

**Before §10 — where we are.** A labour movement banned in 1872 was rebuilt as
unions and a party, and in 1899 it fought the largest lockout in the country's
history to a draw and signed an agreement with the employers that is still in
force. All of that happened north of the Kongeå.

## Myth-check

**"The cooperative movement was Danish democracy in economic form."**

It was democratic among its members, and the question it settles quietly is who
counted as one.

Take the rule that everyone praises. One member, one vote, regardless of herd
size, is a genuinely radical provision and it was written by farmers who could
have written it otherwise. But a member was a man who owned cows and land. The
society was an *interessentskab* whose members were jointly liable with everything
they owned, which meant that a man with nothing to pledge could not be admitted
even in principle: he had no security to offer the others and the bank was lending
against the collateral of the whole membership.

So the movement drew its line at the boundary of the freehold. Below it were the
*husmænd*, holding a few acres and working for wages, and the *tyende*, hired by
the year under a servant law that made walking out an offence. In many parishes
those two groups together were most of the population. They did not vote in the
dairy because they were not in the dairy.

That is not a reason to think less of what was built. It is a reason to read §06
next to §03 rather than after it. In the same thirty years in which Denmark
invented a form of economic self-government admired everywhere, more than four in
ten of the people who emigrated were agricultural labourers, and among the women
the largest single group was servants. The cooperative parish and the emigration
figures are the same parish, counted twice.

**A smaller myth, in passing.** That Hjedding was the first cooperative dairy.
Joint dairies had existed for years and Kaslunde on Funen in 1875 was very close to
the later model. What Hjedding did first was write down the rules that everyone
else then copied — which is a better claim than the one usually made for it.

## Summary

Grain stopped paying from about 1875 under American and Russian competition, and
Germany closed its border to live Danish cattle in 1881; Danish farms turned from
selling grain to feeding it, and sold butter and bacon to Britain instead.

The institution that let small farmers do it was written in Ølgod in 1882: deliver
all your milk, be paid the same rate as everyone else, have one vote whatever the
size of your herd, and stand liable for your neighbours' debts with everything you
own. Within twenty years more than a thousand dairies ran on those rules, and the
rules had been copied from the parish savings bank.

The same countryside divided along a religious line at the same time — mission
house against meeting-house, an association run on the principle that voting
causes schism against a movement of folk high schools and rifle clubs — and the
division reached into the schools and sometimes into the dairies.

The people the cooperative did not admit left. About 309,000 Danes went overseas
between 1868 and 1900, more than four in ten of them farm labourers; those who
stayed filled the new tenement districts outside Copenhagen's ramparts. Their
movement was banned in 1872 after the police illegally prohibited a meeting on
Nørre Fælled, rebuilt itself as unions and a party, fought the largest lockout in
Danish history to a standstill in 1899, and signed the September Compromise, which
is still in force.

South of the Kongeå none of it applied. Danish associations were dissolved, Danish
schools pressed and Danish servants expelled under the Köller policy from 1897; and
close to ten thousand children born there to Danish parents held no citizenship at
all until 1907, when Germany offered them one and the price Denmark paid was the
promise of a vote.

## Questions

**Recall.**

1. What four rules were written into the Hjedding contract in 1882, and where had
   the wording come from?
2. What two things outside Denmark's control made the change of 1875–1882
   possible, and in which years?
3. Who were *de hjemløse*, how many of them were there, and what did Denmark give
   up in 1907 to settle their position?

**Causal.**

1. Grain prices fell across all of Europe. Why did Denmark respond with
   cooperatives when other countries with the same problem did not?
2. The unlimited joint liability in the Hjedding rules is usually mentioned last
   and explains most of the rest. Why?
3. The police ban on the meeting of 5 May 1872 was unconstitutional and the
   prosecution that followed was for high treason. Did the state's reaction weaken
   the Danish labour movement or make it?

**Counterfactual.**

1. Suppose Germany had not banned live cattle imports in 1881. Does the Danish
   dairy economy still arrive, and when?
2. Suppose the September Compromise had not been reached in 1899 and one side had
   broken the other. What in the following century looks different?

**Contested.**

1. This chapter argues that the cooperative movement and the emigration are the
   same parish counted twice, and that the movement's democracy was real and
   bounded by the freehold. What evidence would tell against that?
2. The battle of the Common is the founding event of the Danish labour movement,
   and it was a fundraiser for a failing strike that the authorities turned into
   something larger. Does knowing that make it matter less, or differently?

## Visit

**Hjedding Andelsmejeri**, south of Ølgod. The original ninety square metres, kept
as a museum since 1948, with the Maglekilde separator and the churn and the steam
engine that drove both. Free entry; a sign at the door with a telephone number.

**Askov Højskole**, near Vejen. Founded in 1865 to replace Rødding, four kilometres
on the wrong side of the border that had just been drawn.

**A parish with two houses.** Almost any village in Jutland will have both a
*forsamlingshus* and a *missionshus*, usually at opposite ends. Some of the
meeting-houses in Salling are octagonal, on the pattern from Mors.

**Nørrebro and Vesterbro**, Copenhagen. The blocks of the 1870s and 1880s are
still standing and still lived in, with the courtyards visible from the street
doors.

**Nørre Fælled.** The ground of 5 May 1872 is now Fælledparken, and the labour
movement has met there on the first of May ever since.

**Flensborg** and **Aabenraa.** *Flensborg Avis* is still published. In Aabenraa
the offices of *Hejmdal* and the buildings of the Danish associations of the
Köller years are in the old town.
