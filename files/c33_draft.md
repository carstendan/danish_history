## A king with no heir

On 9 January 1848 Christian 8., knowing he would not recover, wrote out what he
wanted his son to do. He asked for three things: that the monarchy be given a
common constitution, that Frederik take counsel from patriotic men rather than
from his friends, and that he contract a suitable marriage before the year was
out. Eleven days later, on the evening of 20 January, the king died of blood
poisoning contracted in a bloodletting his physician had ordered. He was
sixty-one.

The third instruction was the one his son ignored. Frederik 7. was thirty-nine,
had been married twice, had both marriages dissolved, and had no children; in
August 1850 he would marry Louise Rasmussen, a milliner and former dancer, and
make her Countess Danner. The first instruction was carried out eight days after
the old king's death, and it detonated.

The rescript of 28 January 1848 promised a constitutional order for the whole
monarchy: a common estates assembly for kingdom and duchies together, to be
prepared by a gathering of experienced men — eighteen from the kingdom, seven from
Holstein, six from Schleswig, four from the knighthood, with a further eight named by
the king from the kingdom and four from each duchy. The common representation
itself was to have equal numbers from kingdom and duchies, sitting alternately in
each.

Read that arithmetic slowly, because both national movements did. The kingdom held
about 1.4 million people and the duchies about 900,000, and the offer gave them the
same number of seats. To the Copenhagen liberals it meant a German veto over Danish
affairs. To the Schleswig-Holsteiners it meant Schleswig being counted with Holstein
in one breath and governed from Copenhagen in the next. A document intended to
settle the question told both sides they were about to lose.

## What Schleswig legally was

The Ribe letter of 1460 and what the nineteenth century made of it belong to
chapter 19, and the argument about the clause is settled there, not here. What
matters now is the machinery: three territories, two legal orders, and one man
about to die without a son.

Holstein was a fief of the Holy Roman Empire, and after 1815 a member state of the
German Confederation, along with Lauenburg. Its northern boundary was the Eider.
Schleswig was not in the Confederation. It was a fief of the Danish crown, which
produced the strangest arrangement in the monarchy: in Schleswig the king of Denmark
was both the vassal who held the duchy and the lord from whom it was held.

Against that sat the succession. The Kongelov of 1665 is usually described as
allowing the female line to inherit, and the description is right but too loose.
The law was agnatic. It gave the crown to the male descendants of Frederik 3., and
opened the female line only if the male line failed — a fallback nobody needed
for more than a hundred and eighty years. It also laid down that whatever the king held
or acquired should remain undivided under the king of Denmark.

The duchies ran on their own rules. Schleswig and Holstein had been cut and recut
into partial duchies held by collateral branches of the house, each with its own
succession, and the principle in all of them was agnatic and only agnatic: from man
to man. Nothing in 1665 changed that, and 1721 did not settle it either. When
Frederik 4. brought the Gottorp share of Schleswig under the crown and took homage
for it, he did so without introducing the kingdom's law, the kingdom's
administration, or Danish as the language of administration. The territory was
incorporated; the question of whose succession law now ran there was left where it
had been.

By the 1840s that unanswered question had a date on it. The heirs under the
Kongelov, in order, were Christian 8.'s brother Prince Ferdinand, who had no
legitimate children; then his sisters — Juliane Sophie, childless, and Louise
Charlotte, born in 1789 and married to Landgrave Wilhelm of Hesse-Kassel — and
then her children. Which is to say: after Frederik 7. and his uncle the Danish
crown passed through a woman. Under Holstein's law it could not. The same
childless king was therefore about to send his kingdom to one man and at least
part of his duchies to another, and the other was the duke of Augustenborg, whose
father had set down the family's claim in 1812–13 and who had himself fused his
dynasty's interest with the Schleswig-Holstein movement's case for the duchies.

Christian 8. tried to close it with the Open Letter of July 1846, which declared
the Kongelov's succession fully valid for Schleswig and admitted uncertainty for
parts of Holstein. That is chapter 32's ending. It is worth saying plainly here
what it did not do: it asserted the Danish reading, it did not prove it.

**Where this account differs from the usual one.** Most popular treatments give
the dynastic law a paragraph and put the weight on the clash of nationalisms. This
page gives it a section, because 1864 makes no sense without it. Great powers do
not send armies over sentiment; they send them over broken instruments. And there
is a further reason for caution that Danish accounts tend to skip. Working
source-critically half a century later, the historian Kristian Erslev concluded, in
1901 and again in 1915, that the Kongelov's provisions had never been valid in
Schleswig at all — and that the Augustenborg claim was accordingly justified. His
view has not won general assent and the question remains open. But it means the
legal case Denmark went to war for in 1848, and lost a duchy over in 1864, was one
its own best archival scholar later thought was wrong.

## March 1848

On 18 March 1848 the German-minded members of the Schleswig and Holstein estates met
together at Rendsburg and resolved to send the king an ultimatum: a free
constitution for the duchies, Schleswig admitted to the German Confederation, and a
Schleswig-Holstein army under its own officers. Granted in full, that would have
left nothing in common with Denmark but the person of the king. A deputation was
put on a steamer for Copenhagen.

The news reached Copenhagen on the 20th, and it arrived enlarged. A meeting of the
national liberals had been called for the 22nd; it was pulled forward two days. That
afternoon Copenhagen's Borgerrepræsentation debated an address to the king, drafted
by Orla Lehmann, demanding that the old ministry be replaced by men who would give
the country a free constitution and bind Schleswig to the kingdom. There was
resistance at first. After Lehmann spoke there was not.

On the 21st the Borgerrepræsentation walked to Christiansborg with the address, and
a crowd of twelve to fifteen thousand walked with it. They were met at the palace
with the news that the king had already dismissed his ministers that morning. On
the 22nd the new ministry came together — A.W. Moltke, finance minister since 1831,
as premier and still at finance, Bardenfleth also kept on from the old government,
Knuth at foreign affairs, Bluhme from
the central administration and Zahrtmann from the navy, and, as the mark of the
change, the national liberals Monrad, Hvidt and Lehmann himself and Tscherning of
the Friends of the Peasant. Frederik 7. told them he now regarded himself as a
constitutional king. Absolutism in Denmark had lasted since 1660 and ended in two
days, without a barricade or a shot.

Then the thing Copenhagen had already reacted to actually happened. On the night of
23–24 March six men met in Kiel and constituted a provisional government for the
duchies: the lawyers Wilhelm Beseler and Jürgen Bremer for the national liberals,
Theodor Olshausen, Count Reventlow-Preetz for the nobility, Martin Thorsen Schmidt
for the Kiel citizenry, and Prince Frederik of Nør, the Augustenborg duke's younger
brother, as their military man. The proclamation Beseler read outside the town
hall early on the 24th did not claim independence. It claimed
that the will of the sovereign was no longer free and the country was without a
government, and that until the king could decide freely the provisional government
would carry the responsibility. The formula was Reventlow's, and it was why
officials and town councils across the duchies accepted a revolutionary body as
lawful: they were not rebelling, they were rescuing a prisoner.

At seven a special train left Kiel for Rendsburg carrying the Kiel garrison and
armed volunteers under the Prince of Nør. They rang the church bells. The garrison,
taking it for a fire, assembled without its weapons; the commandant judged
resistance hopeless, most of the officers refused to join and were let go, and the
men went over to the rising. Rendsburg was the central fortress of the duchies,
with their arsenal and their treasury, and it changed hands without bloodshed, by
railway timetable.

Two revolutions in one week, in opposite directions, and the second one was
reported before it began.

## The war begins

The provisional government held most of the duchies within days, but not all of
them. In North Schleswig the farming population stayed with the king, and so did the
town of Haderslev; Sønderborg, Aabenraa and Flensburg split down the middle. That
division — a duchy in which loyalty ran north to south rather than along any
administrative line — is the fact the next sixteen years were fought over, and it
was visible in the first fortnight.

Danish troops crossed the Kongeå into Schleswig on 29 March. On 9 April about
eleven thousand men under Hedemann met a Schleswig-Holstein force of some six
thousand under General Krohn at Bov, north of the modern border, and broke it;
more than nine hundred of the enemy were taken prisoner unwounded, and the rest
went south in disorder. Within two days the Danish army stood on the line of the
Schlei and the Dannevirke. Denmark had
apparently won its civil war in a month.

It had not, because the war stopped being a civil war. Prussia and the German
Confederation came in on the insurgents' side, and on Easter Day, 23 April, General
Wrangel attacked the Danish position outside the town of Schleswig. How large a
force he brought is genuinely disputed: one reckoning gives about twenty thousand
Prussians with some three thousand federal troops and four thousand
Schleswig-Holsteiners, others more, and a count of the units actually engaged
less. About twelve thousand Danes held for a day and then fell back north. Their
losses in killed, wounded and captured are given at between eight and nine
hundred; the other side's, at 461.

The following day the rear-guard was caught at Oversø, south of Flensburg, by
Mecklenburg dragoons and Hanoverian infantry, and about fifty men were taken.
Prussian and Schleswig-Holstein troops crossed the Kongeå into Jutland at the start of
May, and Russia, which would not let Prussia into the kingdom proper, made Prussia
take them back. Meanwhile the Danes won a bridgehead in Sundeved and held it — at
Nybøl on 28 May and at Dybbøl on 5 June, against Wrangel's German army — which
delighted Denmark and alarmed everybody else. Under British and Swedish mediation
the fighting was suspended by the armistice of Malmö on 26 August 1848.

That armistice is where the war's real shape shows. It was accepted on 16
September by the Frankfurt National Assembly, which had no power to enforce
anything else (see *Meanwhile in Europe*). The provisional government was wound up on 22
October and replaced by a joint Danish-Prussian administration sitting at Gottorp.
Nobody had conceded anything. Everybody had discovered that the question would be
decided somewhere other than the battlefield.

The truce ran out in the spring. The war resumed in April 1849 and began badly: on
5 April the navy lost the ship of the line *Christian VIII* and the frigate
*Gefion* in Eckernförde Bay, and on the 23rd the army was beaten at Kolding by the
Schleswig-Holsteiners, fighting on their own. Their army went on into Jutland and
laid siege to Fredericia, and on the night of 6 July the garrison came out over
the ramparts and broke the siege. A second armistice followed that summer.

## The soldier's war

Until 1848 the Danish army was recruited almost entirely from one class. Conscription
fell on the peasantry: on farmers, cottagers and their sons, entered in the
*lægdsrulle* parish by parish. Townsmen, burghers, officials and the nobility were
exempt, either by birth or by having bought the exemption with a payment into the
Landmilicefonden or the Søindrulleringsfonden, and they supplied the officers. The
apparatus that did the counting was the one chapter 29 described: the parish
muster roll.

> seven on a good one. The census of 1845 lists him at home with his parents as a
> clog-maker, one of seventeen in the three parishes around the forest. His mother
> had died when he was six, of an infection after a tooth was pulled badly.
>

The war changed who had to go. A temporary ordinance of 23 September 1848 pulled in
men who had been exempt, for the duration; then, after three days of debate in the
constituent assembly in December, the Law on Universal Conscription of 12 February
1849 made it permanent. The cut it made was not between town and country but
between one birth year and the next. Every man born in 1826 or later lost whatever
exemption he had, whether it came from his birth or had been bought with a payment
into the funds, and only established teachers and ordained clergy were spared; every
man born before 1 January 1826 kept his, except in an emergency of war. Every male
was to be entered in the *lægdsrulle* as soon as he was confirmed, the parish priest
reporting the confirmed boys. The obligation was written into the constitution of
5 June.

Two things follow, and both are worth saying because the phrase "universal
conscription" carries neither of them. The first is that a man could still buy his
way out by paying another to serve in his place, and that survived until 1867 —
through this war and the next one. The second is that the institution which now
enrolled every boy in Denmark was the same register that since 1788 had held the
country conscript to his home district. It now held everyone, and held no one in
place: a conscript could move anywhere in the kingdom, his papers following him
from one district's roll to the next, and the tie to the birthplace, which the
state had taken over from the landlords when the *stavnsbånd* ended, went with it.

The list of the dead that is still used was made privately, by Asser Daniel Cohen,
catechist to the Jews of Funen and Lolland-Falster, living in Odense, who had
already published a study of the position of the Jews in Denmark past and present.
His register of the fallen gives 533 for 1848, 1,381 for 1849 and 1,872 for 1850:
**3,786** men, more than the 2,978 Denmark would lose in 1864. A companion volume,
*Krigen 1864 og de Faldnes Minde*, did the same for 1864, and the Rigsarkiv still
names the two as the principal sources for the dead of both wars.

## 5 June 1849

The constituent assembly was elected in October 1848, with a minority of members
named by the king, and sat at Christiansborg through the winter arguing about who a
citizen was. On 5 June 1849 Frederik 7. signed what it produced.

The Junigrundlov gave Denmark a two-chamber Rigsdag — a Folketing and a Landsting,
elected in 1849 on the same qualifications — a government answerable to it, and a
list of rights: freedom of speech, of assembly and of association, an end to
arbitrary imprisonment, an end to censorship. Measured against 28 January 1848 it
was an enormous distance travelled in sixteen months.

Then the qualifications. To vote a man had to be unblemished in reputation, hold
Danish *indfødsret*, be thirty years old, have his own household, not be in another's
service without one, and never have received poor relief that had not been repaid.
The result was that **15 per cent of the whole population** could vote, which is
**72.8 per cent of all men over thirty**. Popular speech named the excluded as the
seven F's: *fruentimmere, folkehold, fattige, fremmede, fallenter, fjolser,
forbrydere* — women, servants, paupers, foreigners, bankrupts, those under
guardianship and the convicted. Women and servants waited sixty-six years; the
foreigners are waiting still.

What the first election looked like is worth having as a number rather than as an
impression. On 4 December 1849 a hundred seats were filled. In forty-three of them
there was no ballot at all, the single candidate being carried by acclamation; in
the fifty-seven that were contested, 37,903 men voted out of 116,553 entitled. A
third of the electorate of a third of the men of a country turned out, in less than
two-thirds of the constituencies, at the first election to the Folketing.

One more clause matters more than any of this, and almost nobody reads it. The
constitution carried an opening reservation that everything concerning the position
of the duchy of Schleswig would wait until peace was concluded. It never came into
force south of the Kongeå.

## A church for the people

In December 1842 the Danish state settled that the children of Danish Baptists
were to be baptised by force. A state church cannot do otherwise: it is
the church a subject is born into, holding the doctrine the state has settled, and
a parent who declines infant baptism is not exercising a preference but breaking
the law. The Baptist cases were still running when the constituent assembly sat.

Article 3 of the Junigrundlov reads that the evangelical Lutheran church is the
Danish *folkekirke* and is as such supported by the state. Article 81 gave the
right to worship according to conviction, and article 84 that a man's choice of
faith affected neither his civil rights nor his civil duties. The word *folkekirke*
had first been used by P. Chr. Kierkegaard in 1841 — the elder brother of the more
famous one — and it was Monrad who wrote the church paragraphs of the government's
draft. The church was to be the people's rather than the state's, in name at least.

The change is easy to undersell, so here is its size. The Lutheran monopoly of
chapter 20's settlement had run since 1536; it ended in 1849, and the
Conventicle Placard of 1741 had already gone, in 1848. The Jews, who had had civil rights since 1814,
now had full religious freedom — which is to say that Denmark's roll of its own war
dead was begun by a man who, when the war began, was still legally a second-class
believer.

What did not happen is equally characteristic. Article 80 promised that the
constitution of the *folkekirke* would be settled by law. Three positions had been
in the room: Mynster wanted the state church to survive under a new name, H.N.
Clausen wanted a synod, Grundtvig wanted the church free of the state altogether,
and the promise was the compromise that let all three sign. It was never kept.
Drafts were made in 1853–54 and five times since, the last in 2011–14; none became
law, and the article is still there, undischarged, today. Grundtvig's real victory came
elsewhere, in the *sognebåndsløsning* of 1855, which let a man choose a priest other
than his parish's — the second loosening of the parish priest's hold, after the end
of the Conventicle Placard that chapter 32's lay preachers had been fined under.

A constitution that excluded eighty-five per cent of the population from the vote
gave every one of them the right to pray as they chose. Both statements are true of
the same document, and a page that reports only the first is not describing 1849.

## Isted, 25 July 1850

Denmark made peace with Prussia on 2 July 1850. The provisional government
declined to be included in it and fought on, reinforced by German volunteers, which
is how the largest battle in Danish history came to be fought after the war it
belonged to was formally over.

The armies met on Isted Hede, north of the town of Schleswig, on 25 July. The Danes
had about thirty-seven thousand men under General Krogh; the Schleswig-Holstein
army about twenty-seven thousand under the Prussian general von Willisen. The
first shots had been fired early on the 24th, and through that day the
Schleswig-Holsteiners were pushed back along the whole line; the main battle began
in the night of the 24th–25th and went on until late afternoon, when the Danes
took the town of Schleswig.
It rained, the smoke did not clear, the ground was cut up with
bog and lake, and the front was too long for anyone to see. Orders arrived late or
not at all. A Danish brigade that was to cut the enemy's retreat was pulled back,
and the Schleswig-Holstein army got away south over the Eider to Rendsburg intact.

Denmark won, and paid more for it than the loser did. The Danish dead numbered 845
and the Schleswig-Holstein dead 534; the wounded are given differently by different
authorities depending on whether prisoners are counted with them, at 2,405 or 2,770
on the Danish side and 1,202 or 2,274 on the other. Whichever set is used, the army
that held the field lost more men than the army that left it. Among the Danish dead
were Colonel Frederik Læssøe, shot at six in the morning leading the advance guard
past Øvre Stolk, and General Schleppegrell, wounded at Stolk and dead the next day
in hospital in Flensburg.

It is possible to see the day from inside it, because a conscript assistant surgeon
called Daniel Bølling wrote to his father two days later. Bølling was a Copenhagen
medical student who had been in the Casino on the evening of 20 March 1848 and had
volunteered on the spot; by 1850 he was in uniform not from enthusiasm but because
the law of February 1849 had caught everyone born from 1826, and he was born in
1827. His ambulance spent the first day in a field south of an inn and the second
in a barn north of Helligbæk, close enough to the fighting that enemy skirmishers
came within cannon-shot. Several hundred wounded lay waiting to be dressed. He
admits, with some care, that the cannonade frightened him until the first casualty
arrived and there was work to do. For two days the staff lived on a cup of coffee
each morning and a little wine and water shared with the wounded.

After the battle, four hundred and ninety-five Danes were buried together in one
grave in the old cemetery at Flensburg.

Isted decided nothing. A Schleswig-Holstein attack at Missunde in September failed,
an assault on Friedrichstadt in October failed, and the rising was finally shut down
not by the Danish army but by Russia and Austria leaning on Prussia. Until the
Winter War of 1939–40 no larger battle was fought on Nordic soil.

## London, 8 May 1852

Eight days after Isted, on 2 August 1850, Britain, Russia, France and
Sweden-Norway signed a protocol in London declaring that the Danish monarchy should
be kept whole under the Danish king. Austria acceded shortly afterwards; Prussia,
which wanted the duchies drawn into Germany, did not sign. The protocol was, above
all, Russia's work. That is the shape of the whole settlement: the powers took the
question out of Danish and German hands and answered it themselves, in Denmark's
favour on the surface and against Denmark underneath.

The instrument that mattered came in May 1852. To get Prussian and Austrian
signatures on it, Denmark had to give the two German powers an undertaking at the
turn of 1851–52, in notes exchanged separately, that it would not incorporate the
duchy of Schleswig into the kingdom — which was what the war had begun over — and
in return the bonds between Schleswig and Holstein were to be loosened, so that
Schleswig was tied no more closely to Holstein than to the kingdom. That promise is
not in the treaty. It was made beside it, in notes that were not a treaty and whose
legal standing was doubtful; the royal proclamation of 28 January 1852, which was
to carry it out, announced a united monarchy with a common constitution for its
common affairs and did not mention incorporation at all.

The Treaty of London of 8 May 1852, between Denmark and Britain, France, Austria,
Prussia, Russia and Sweden-Norway, then settled the succession. Failing male
descendants in the direct line from Frederik 3., the crown of all the king's lands
was to pass to Prince Christian of Schleswig-Holstein-Sonderburg-Glücksburg and to
the issue of his marriage with Princess Louise, born a princess of Hesse, by
primogeniture and from man to man. It was done, the treaty says, with the consent
of the Hereditary Prince and of the king's nearest cognates called to the succession
by the Danish Kongelov — that is, the people who actually had the claim signed it
away — and its stated object was a combination which, excluding women, called the
male line to inherit in all the lands united under the Danish sceptre.

Look at what that means. The dynastic quarrel had been between a Danish law that
allowed a woman to transmit a crown and a German law that did not. Europe resolved
it by adopting the German principle and then finding a man whose *wife* held the
Danish claim. Christian 9. would become king of Denmark because Louise renounced in
his favour. The Danish position had been conceded in the act of being secured.

The Rigsdag duly passed a new succession law in 1853. And the June constitution was
to go on applying in the kingdom only; in the duchies the king kept his absolute
power until constitutional forms could be given them. A country that had given up
absolute monarchy in 1848 had now undertaken to keep it in its own provinces.

## What the constitution could not cover

Denmark ended the war holding the field, holding the duchies, and holding a
guarantee from five great powers. It had a free constitution for the kingdom, an
absolutism it was obliged to preserve in the duchies, a promise it had made in
notes beside the treaty not to incorporate Schleswig, and a designated heir chosen
by foreigners.
Every one of those would still be true in 1863, and three of them are why 1864
happened.

The monument tells the rest. In July 1858 Orla Lehmann — the man in the Casino ten
years earlier — wrote to the sculptor H.W. Bissen proposing a lion for Isted. A
national subscription raised twenty-five thousand rigsdaler, Bissen modelled the
beast in 1859 and 1860, and on 25 July 1862, the twelfth anniversary of the battle,
it was unveiled at the old cemetery in Flensburg before five thousand people, with
H.N. Clausen giving the address. *Isted, 25 July 1850. The
Danish people raised this memorial.* Frederik 7. was advised not to involve
himself, gave nothing, and did not come.

To make room for it a large number of graves were levelled, among them a
Schleswig-Holstein war grave from the Three Years' War itself. It stood for less
than two years. In February 1864, after the retreat from the Dannevirke, it was
taken down, and in 1868 the Prussian king had it set up in Berlin as booty, in the
arsenal and from 1878 outside the cadet school at Lichterfelde. It came back to
Denmark in 1945 and back to Flensburg in 2011.

The cemetery it stands in holds about two thousand Danish soldiers — the dead of Bov
in 1848 and Isted in 1850, and then the dead of Sankelmark and Dybbøl in 1864, in
the same ground, in a town that would be on the other side of a border before the
dead of Isted had lain there fifteen years.

## Danish terms, by section

**§01 — a king with no heir**

- **helstat** — "whole state". The monarchy taken as one unit: the kingdom with
  the duchies of Schleswig, Holstein and Lauenburg, held by one man under different
  laws. A nineteenth-century word for an arrangement nearly four centuries old, and it names
  the thing both national movements wanted to break.

**§02 — what Schleswig legally was**

- **agnatisk og kognatisk arvefølge** — agnatic and cognatic succession.
  Agnatic: from man to man only. Cognatic: allowed to pass through a woman. The
  Kongelov was agnatic with a cognatic fallback if the male line failed; the
  duchies' law had no such fallback. The whole crisis is in that difference.
- **Kongeloven** — the King's Law of 1665, the written constitution of absolute
  monarchy, and the law of succession the crisis turns on (chapter 25).
- **up ewig ungedeelt** — "for ever undivided": a nineteenth-century
  Schleswig-Holstein slogan, A. W. Neuber's of 1841, made from a clause of the
  Ribe charter of 1460 and read as a promise that the two lands would never be
  parted. What the clause meant in 1460 is disputed (chapter 19).

**§03 — March 1848**

- **Martsministeriet** — the March ministry, 22 March to 11 November 1848. The
  first Danish government whose members were chosen for a political position
  rather than to administer the king's will.
- **provisorisk regering** — the provisional government proclaimed at Kiel on 24
  March 1848. It claimed to act *for* the king on the ground that he was no longer
  free, which is why officials who would not have joined a rebellion obeyed it.

**§04 — the war begins**

- **treårskrigen** — the Three Years' War, 1848–50, also the First Schleswig War.
  Both names mislead: the fighting was broken by two long armistices and did not
  stop until January 1851, and it was a civil war inside the monarchy before it
  was a war between states.

**§05 — the soldier's war**

- **lægdsrulle** — the muster roll: a parish-by-parish register of every male
  liable to conscription, with a *lægd* number and an individual number. From
  1788, when the *stavnsbånd* ended, the means by which the state held country
  conscripts to their home district (chapter 29); after 1849 the instrument
  through which every confirmed boy in the kingdom entered the state's books.
- **stillingsvæsen** — substitution. The right to pay another man to serve in your
  place, which survived universal conscription and was abolished only in 1867.

**§06 — 5 June 1849**

- **Grundloven** — the constitution; here the *Junigrundlov* of 5 June 1849,
  Denmark's first.
- **Rigsdagen, Folketinget, Landstinget** — the parliament and its two chambers.
  In 1849 both were elected on the same franchise; the privileged franchise that
  made the Landsting a conservative brake came in 1866 (chapter 34).
- **de syv F'er** — "the seven F's", the popular name for the excluded:
  *fruentimmere, folkehold, fattige, fremmede, fallenter, fjolser, forbrydere*.

**§07 — a church for the people**

- **folkekirke** — "people's church". The evangelical Lutheran church as
  established by article 3 of the 1849 constitution: supported by the state but,
  in name at least, not owned by it. The word was P. Chr. Kierkegaard's; the
  clause was Monrad's.
- **sognebåndsløsning** — "loosing of the parish bond", 1855: the right to attach
  oneself to a priest other than one's own parish priest.

**§08 — Isted**

- **ambulance** — in this period a mobile field hospital, not a vehicle.

**§09 — London**

- **Londontraktaten** — the Treaty of London of 8 May 1852, guaranteeing the
  monarchy's integrity and settling the succession on the Glücksburg line. Not the
  London Protocol of 2 August 1850, which is a separate and earlier instrument,
  and not the notes of 1851–52, which are where the promise about Schleswig
  actually sits — a promise of doubtful legal standing, since the notes were not
  a treaty.

**§10 — what the constitution could not cover**

- **Istedløven** — the Isted Lion, Bissen's monument of 1862 on the old cemetery
  in Flensburg. Danish memorial, Prussian trophy, Copenhagen exhibit and, since
  2011, a monument to reconciliation on its original site: four meanings between
  1862 and 2011, and only the first of them the one it was made for.

## Meanwhile in Europe

**February 1848 — Paris.** Louis-Philippe abdicated on 24 February; within eight
weeks there had been risings in Vienna, Berlin, Milan, Budapest and across the
German states, and Denmark's March days fall inside the same eight weeks. What is
unusual about the Danish case is not that absolutism fell but how. In Paris a king
lost his throne; in Berlin and Vienna troops fired on crowds; in Copenhagen the
king dismissed his ministers before the procession reached the palace and kept his
crown for life. The country with the mildest 1848 is the one that got a war out of
it.

**September 1848 — Frankfurt.** The German National Assembly had claimed
Schleswig-Holstein for Germany, and in September it was
asked to ratify the armistice of Malmö, which handed the duchies back to a joint
administration. It ratified, and in doing so admitted it had no army, no treasury
and no obedience, and that Prussia would make war and peace in the north without
consulting it. Riots followed and two deputies were murdered. The parliament that
had opened in May proposing to unify Germany by consent closed the year having learned that
Schleswig, like everything else, would go to whoever had the troops.

## Checkpoints

**Before §04 — where we are.** A king has died leaving three instructions and no
heir. His son has given away absolute power in two days, and a provisional government
has been proclaimed in Kiel four days after Copenhagen was told it had been. The
Danish and German readings of the succession are about to be tested with soldiers.

**Before §06 — where we are.** The army has won at Bov, lost at Schleswig, held a
bridgehead at Nybøl and Dybbøl, lost at Kolding and broken the siege of Fredericia,
and been stopped twice by armistices, the first arranged in Malmö under British and
Swedish mediation. Conscription has stopped being a peasant obligation. The
constitution of 5 June 1849 has been passed.

**Before §09 — where we are.** Denmark has a constitution that excludes
eighty-five per cent of the population from the vote and grants all of them freedom
of belief, and a reservation on its first page saying Schleswig must wait for peace.
The largest battle in Danish history has been won and has settled nothing. The
peace will be made somewhere else, by other people.

## Myth-check

**"Denmark won the Three Years' War."**

Denmark won the war and lost the peace, and the second half is the half that
matters, because the terms of the peace are the direct cause of 1864.

The military record is mostly Danish. Bov, the sortie from Fredericia, Isted: the
Danish army beat the Schleswig-Holstein army in the great battles when the two met
alone, though it lost to it at Kolding. Against Wrangel's German army it could win
and hold a bridgehead, as at Nybøl and Dybbøl, and no more. What stopped Prussia in
1848 was Russia, which would not have a German power on the Sound, and what ended
the rising in 1851 was Russia and Austria leaning on Berlin. Denmark's decisive
weapon in this war belonged to somebody else.

And the settlement was not a Danish victory dressed as a compromise. It was a
compromise dressed as a Danish victory. The monarchy's integrity was guaranteed —
and Denmark undertook, in notes beside the treaty, not to incorporate Schleswig into
the kingdom. The succession was settled — on a principle excluding women, which was
the German case, in favour of a man who held his claim through his wife. The
constitution stood — in the kingdom only, with absolutism kept in the duchies.
Every Danish war aim was formally conceded and practically reversed.

When the Rigsråd passed the November Constitution in 1863, it broke a promise that
the proclamation of 1852, which was to carry it out, had never mentioned. The trap was built
in 1852, out of a victory.

## Summary

Christian 8. died in January 1848 leaving a childless son and a succession that
Danish and German law answered differently, and the rescript that followed offered
the duchies equal representation with a kingdom half again their size. It satisfied
nobody, which is what a compromise does when the thing being divided is a nation.

In March the national liberals brought their meeting forward two days on reports
from the duchies, and heard that Rendsburg had probably fallen; Frederik 7. gave up
absolute power before the procession reached his palace. On the night of 23–24
March the rising the meeting had been called about actually began, and a
provisional government took Rendsburg by special train without bloodshed.

Denmark beat the Schleswig-Holsteiners at Bov, Fredericia and Isted and lost to them
at Kolding; against Wrangel's German army it could only hold a bridgehead, and
Prussia was stopped by Russia instead. Until the autumn of 1848 the army was filled
from the peasantry alone; the law of February 1849 took every man born from 1826,
but left him able to pay another to go in his place until 1867.

The constitution of 5 June 1849 gave the vote to fifteen per cent of the population
and freedom of belief to all of it, ended the Lutheran monopoly of 1536, and
reserved the position of Schleswig until peace was concluded. It never came into
force south of the Kongeå.

The peace, made in London in 1852, guaranteed the monarchy's integrity, settled the
crown on a prince who held his claim through his wife, kept absolutism in the
duchies, and bound Denmark, in notes beside the treaty, not to incorporate
Schleswig into the kingdom. That is the promise Denmark broke in 1863, and it is
why a war Denmark won produced the war Denmark lost.

## Questions

**Recall.**

1. Who compiled the register of the Danish dead of 1848–50, and what had his
   position been under the law when the war began?
2. Which powers signed the London Protocol of 2 August 1850, and which German power
   refused?
3. What were the qualifications for the vote under the 1849 constitution, and what
   proportion of the population met them?

**Causal.**

1. The rescript of 28 January 1848 was a genuine constitutional concession, and it
   made both national movements angrier. Why?
2. The provisional government declared that the king's will was no longer free
   rather than declaring independence. What did that formula buy it, and from whom?
3. Denmark beat the Schleswig-Holsteiners in the great battles and got a settlement
   it had not won. What made the difference, and where was it decided?

**Counterfactual.**

1. Suppose the reports reaching Copenhagen on 20 March 1848 had been accurate, and
   the meeting had been told nothing had yet happened at Rendsburg. Does absolutism
   still end that week?
2. Suppose the Kongelov of 1665 had been plainly agnatic, with no female fallback
   at all. Does the Schleswig question still arrive in 1848, and in what form?

**Contested.**

1. Did Orla Lehmann create the mood at the Casino by announcing a rising that had
   not begun, or did he only use a mood already there? Hans Vammen argued the
   first, Bjørn Svensson the second. What evidence could settle it, and does the
   answer change what one thinks of March 1848?
2. Kristian Erslev concluded that the Kongelov's succession had never been valid in
   Schleswig and that the Augustenborg claim was therefore good in law. His view has
   not been generally accepted. If he was right, what follows about 1848 and 1864 —
   and should a national history say so?

## Visit

**Flensborg Gamle Kirkegård**, Flensburg. About two thousand Danish soldiers from
1848, 1850 and 1864, including 495 of the Isted dead in one grave. The Isted Lion
stands where it was unveiled in 1862, taken from in 1864 and returned in 2011.

**Isted Hede**, north of Schleswig. Heath and bog between Flensburg and Schleswig,
still legible as ground: the road runs through a neck between the wet places, which
is why the battle happened where it did.

**Fæstningen**, Fredericia. The ramparts the army came out over on the night of 6
July 1849, and Bissen's *Den tapre Landsoldat* of 1858 — the same sculptor as the
Isted Lion and the opposite kind of monument: a private soldier at ease.

**Rendsburg**, on the Eider. The fortress taken by special train on the morning of
24 March 1848. The railway from Kiel is still there.

**Alter Garnisonsfriedhof**, Stade, west of Hamburg. The stone raised by the
under-officers and comrades of four Danish prisoners who died there in 1848 — among
them Morten Jørgensen Aldahl, *dansk Dragon død i sit 23 Aar*. Missed by the officer
sent in the 1880s to register Danish war graves abroad, and rediscovered in 2019.

**Maltrupgård**, near Sakskøbing, Lolland. Where a governess not yet twenty wrote
*Clara Raphael*.
