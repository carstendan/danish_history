Plague and reconquest: Valdemar Atterdag

1340 – 1375 · Valdemar 4. · Helvig · Niels Bugge · Margrete · the Hanse

A man inherits a quarter of Jutland and thirty-five years later leaves a kingdom. In between, half his people die, he sells a province to pay for the rest, and he ends by losing a war to a league of German towns.

Introduction

WHAT THIS PAGE ANSWERSFive questions

  1. How do you buy back a country when you have nothing to buy it with?
  2. The Black Death killed a large part of Denmark's population. How large — and how would we know?
  3. What does a plague do to a country that had already run out of good land?
  4. Why did the richest king Denmark had seen in a century lose a war to a group of trading towns?
  5. He left no surviving son. So why is the next chapter the greatest reign in Danish history?

01 / NARRATIVEA quarter of Jutland

Danish terms in this section

Atterdag
Valdemar 4.'s byname, usually rendered 'again-day' or 'another day'. Its origin is not certain, and it may be a later coinage.
indløsning
Redemption: buying a pledged district back from the creditor holding it. The central activity of this reign.

Chapter 14 ended with Denmark in pieces: no king since 1332, Jutland and Funen held by Gerhard III of Holstein, Zealand by his kinsman Johann, and Skåne, Halland and Blekinge gone to Sweden. Then, in April 1340, a Jutland squire killed Gerhard in his bed at Randers, and the Holsteiners decided that a Danish king would be easier to manage than a Danish rebellion.

The man they produced was Valdemar, youngest son of the deposed Christoffer 2., about twenty, brought up at the court of the Emperor in Bavaria and with no power base whatever. He was married to Helvig, sister of the duke of Schleswig, whose dowry was land in north Jutland. And he was given, as his kingdom, roughly a quarter of Jutland. Everything else stayed in pledge.

Helvig deserves better than the half-line she usually gets, because what happened to her belongs to the reign rather than beside it. She bore Valdemar at least three children, the last of them Margrete, at Søborg castle in 1353. Later tradition has her kept at Søborg for years, apart from her husband; the sources do not prove it. She died at Esrum Abbey, in the north of Zealand, around 1374, and was buried there. The marriage that helped buy the first quarter of Jutland ended with the queen at one end of Zealand while the king governed from the other, and no chronicler thought that worth explaining.

It is worth being clear about how weak that position was. He had no treasury, no army beyond what his own estates could raise, no capital — Copenhagen belonged to the bishop of Roskilde — and no control over the two most valuable things in the realm, which were the Sound and the Skåne herring markets. His predecessors had pledged the kingdom away over fifty years. He proposed to buy it back.

The method never changed and it is the whole reign in one sentence: get money from somewhere, redeem a district, tax that district, use the proceeds to redeem the next one. Where a creditor would not sell, take the castle and pull it down, so that it could not be held against him again. Where a marriage would serve better than a siege, marry. It took thirty-five years and it worked.

02 / NARRATIVESelling Estonia

The first substantial sum came from liquidating an asset, and it is the most revealing decision of the reign.

Northern Estonia had been Danish since Valdemar Sejr's landing in 1219 — chapter 13's great adventure, the province of the Dannebrog story, held for a hundred and twenty-seven years. It was also expensive to garrison, hard to reach, and in permanent friction with the German orders around it. In 1343 the Estonian peasantry rose against their German-speaking landlords in what is called St George's Night, and the Danish administration could not put it down. The Teutonic Order did.

In 1346 Valdemar sold the province to the Teutonic Order for nineteen thousand marks of silver.

Modern Danish accounts sometimes treat this as a humiliation. It was not. It was a man with a liability and a shortage of cash converting the first into the second, and it is the earliest clear example of a pattern this series will see again: Denmark acquires overseas territory in a period of expansion, holds it thinly, finds it costs more than it yields, and sells it. The Danish West Indies go to the United States in 1917 for twenty-five million dollars — the sale of the West Indies, in Part I — and the reasoning is not very different.

What the nineteen thousand marks bought was Danish districts. The province of the crusade paid for the redemption of Jutland.

03 / NARRATIVE1350

Danish terms in this section

Den Sorte Død
The Black Death. The name is not contemporary; people at the time called it simply the pestilence, or the great mortality.
sjælemesse
A mass said for the soul of a named dead person, paid for in advance. The registers of these are the best Danish evidence for the plague.

It reached Denmark in 1349 or 1350 — the standard date is 1350 — and it had taken about thirty-three months to get there from Sicily.

And here the chapter has to slow down and be honest, because this is the point at which Danish history has the least evidence for the most important thing that happened.

England has manorial court rolls that record, village by village, who held a tenancy and when it fell vacant, which is why English plague mortality can be estimated within a range. Denmark has nothing comparable for the fourteenth century. There is no Danish equivalent of the Domesday tradition, the monastic annals are thin and formulaic, and the crown's own records for these years are patchy.

What there is comes down to a handful of things. The Zealand chronicle reports great mortality in the land. Letters mention monasteries short of monks — one at Odense in 1355 says as much explicitly. And there are the soul-mass registers: cathedrals recorded the masses paid for on behalf of named dead people, and at Ribe the number jumped from roughly one a year to seventeen a year. That is a startling multiple, and it has to be read carefully, because a soul-mass was expensive and only the wealthy bought them. Seventeen is not a death toll. It is a signal that among the people rich enough to appear in the record at all, something extraordinary happened in that year.

Then there is the indirect evidence, which is in some ways stronger. In 1354 Valdemar remitted all death sentences in the kingdom — almost certainly because there were not enough people. In 1356 he ordered water mills built across the country; the Zealand chronicle has him saying he did not want the streams running to the sea without first having done the country some good. Mills do work that people used to do, and a king ordering more of them in 1356 is a king who has noticed that labour has become the scarce thing.

And the castle he built at Randers, standing by 1357, was built — so the local tradition runs — of the stone of eleven demolished churches west of the town.

That last fact is the one to hold, if the tradition is right. Chapter 12 spent a section on the stone churches that went up between 1100 and 1250, about thirteen a year, each one a parish deciding it could afford to build in stone. Eleven of them came down for a single building project because there was nobody left to pray in them.

04 / NARRATIVEWhat it did to the land

Danish terms in this section

ødegård
A deserted farm — a holding that has gone out of cultivation and has no tenant. The characteristic feature of the late-medieval Danish countryside.
landgilde
The rent a tenant owed. After the plague, increasingly negotiable, because the landlord's alternative was an empty farm.
senmiddelalderlig agrarkrise
The late-medieval agrarian crisis: the long depression in grain prices and land values that runs from the mid-1300s to about 1500.

The population fell, and it did not come back for a century and a half. What that did to the Danish countryside is one of the largest changes in the country's history and it happened almost entirely without anybody deciding anything.

Farms went empty. The bishop of Roskilde's estates in the second half of the century were full of deserted holdings. Whole hamlets — usually the -torp settlements of chapter 12, the ones founded latest on the worst ground — were simply abandoned, and their fields went back to grass and scrub. Danish field archaeology finds them as low banks under later pasture.

Then the economics inverted. For three centuries land had been scarce and people plentiful, which is why tenancy replaced slavery in chapter 14 and why rents were high and rising. Now people were scarce and land was abundant. Grain prices fell and kept falling. Rents fell with them, because a landlord facing an empty farm and a tenant who could walk to a better one had very little bargaining power. Wages rose.

So the surviving Danish peasant was, in narrowly material terms, better off after 1350 than before it: a larger holding, lower rent, more meat in the diet, more chance of a farm of his own. This is the standard finding across northern Europe and it should be stated without sentimentality, because the mechanism by which it happened was the deaths of a third to a half of the people he knew.

The losers were the people who lived on rent. The Danish nobility's income fell sharply and stayed down, and their response over the next century shaped everything: consolidating scattered holdings into working estates, pushing into cattle rather than grain — cattle need fewer hands and could be driven south to German markets on the hoof — and pressing, politically, for compensation from the crown. The great Danish manorial estates of the Lutheran nobility (Part F) and the agrarian reforms (Part G) grow out of this.

One more thing worth saying plainly. Chapter 14 argued that the expansion had already stopped before 1349 — the good land taken, farms being given up in poor districts, the Great Famine of 1315–17 behind them. The plague did not create the crisis. It arrived into one, and made the reckoning absolute.

Meanwhile in Europe

Everywhere the same arithmetic produced the same fight. Labour became scarce, workers asked for more, and governments legislated to stop them: England's Ordinance and Statute of Labourers in 1349 and 1351 tried to freeze wages at pre-plague levels, and France did much the same. It did not work, and the attempt to enforce it produced a generation of revolt — the Jacquerie in northern France in 1358, the Ciompi in Florence in 1378, the English rising of 1381.

Denmark had no equivalent explosion, which is worth noticing: Danish rural unrest in this period runs through the assemblies, as it did in chapters 13 and 14, and through risings the nobility led, as in this chapter, rather than through the sort of mass movement that frightened Paris and London. Meanwhile the Hundred Years' War went on regardless, and in 1356 the Golden Bull settled how a German emperor was to be elected — which mattered in Denmark, because the counts of Holstein held their lands of that emperor.

05 / NARRATIVEWhat the survivors built

Danish terms in this section

kalkmaleri
Wall painting on lime plaster. Denmark has the largest surviving body of medieval church painting in northern Europe, and most of it is late.
hvælving
Vault. Inserting brick vaults under an existing flat wooden ceiling is the single most common late-medieval alteration to a Danish church.
alterstiftelse
The endowment of an altar, with land or rent, to pay a priest to say masses for the founder's soul in perpetuity.

Something happens to Danish churches after 1350 that is visible in almost every village in the country, and it is the most tangible consequence of the plague that an ordinary visitor can go and look at.

The parish churches of chapter 12 were Romanesque boxes: a nave, a narrower chancel, small windows, a flat wooden ceiling. In the century and a half after the Black Death, most of them were altered in the same way. Brick vaults were inserted beneath the old wooden ceilings. Towers were added at the west end, and porches on the south side. And the fresh plaster on the new vaults was painted.

The great age of Danish kalkmalerier is not the twelfth century but the fifteenth and early sixteenth, and there is more of it surviving in Denmark than anywhere else in northern Europe — largely because the Reformation whitewashed the paintings rather than destroying them, and the nineteenth and twentieth centuries washed them off again. When a Dane thinks of medieval church painting, they are almost certainly thinking of work done a century or more after this page.

Why the sudden money? Partly because rent-receiving institutions consolidated their holdings while land was cheap. But mostly because of what people did with death. The soul-mass of section 03 was one end of a spectrum whose other end was the endowed altar: land or rent given permanently to a church so that a priest would say mass for the founder's soul for ever. Endowments multiplied after 1350, and so did the altars, chapels, guilds and chantries they paid for. A society that had watched a third or a half of its people die in a year spent the following century buying prayers.

The church that received all this was not in good shape to use it. Clergy died at least as fast as everybody else — probably faster, since their work took them to the dying — and the men ordained to replace them were hastily trained. The friars of chapter 14 filled some of the gap. So did a rising appetite for the direct and the vivid: images of the Passion, of death itself, of the Virgin sheltering the faithful under her cloak.

And one Nordic figure belongs here for reasons that turn out to be dynastic as much as religious. Birgitta of Sweden, who died in Rome in 1373 and was canonised in 1391, founded an order whose mother house at Vadstena became the intellectual centre of Nordic religious life for the next century. Her daughter Merete was the woman in whose household a Danish princess named Margrete was brought up in Norway. The most important religious movement in the fourteenth-century North and the woman who would rule three kingdoms come out of the same family.

Meanwhile in Europe

The religious reaction elsewhere was more extreme and much uglier. Bands of flagellants processed between towns scourging themselves in public, until the papacy suppressed them. And across the Rhineland, France and the Empire in 1348–50, Jewish communities were accused of poisoning wells and massacred — in Strasbourg, Mainz, Cologne and dozens of smaller places — in the worst wave of persecution in Europe before the twentieth century.

The Baltic was not exempt. In 1350 the council of Visby arrested nine men as poisoners, recorded their confessions — some of them noted as given without coercion — burned at least one, and wrote to the council of Rostock with what they had confessed, so that the neighbours would know what to look for. Whether the letters are genuine has since been questioned.

Further off, the papacy sat at Avignon until 1377, and from 1378 there were two popes and then three — the Great Schism, which ran for thirty-nine years and did more damage to the church's authority than the plague had.

06 / NARRATIVERedeeming a kingdom

Danish terms in this section

brydning
The deliberate demolition of a castle, so that it cannot be held against the crown again. Valdemar did a great deal of it.
rigsråd
The council of the realm — the bishops and great magnates who advised, and increasingly constrained, the king.

Through the 1340s, the 1350s and into the 1360s the redemptions went on, district by district: north Jutland first, with Helvig's dowry; then Zealand, where Copenhagen was taken in 1343; by 1348 eastern Funen, the southern islands and eastern Jutland; all of Jutland by 1354; western Funen not until 1365. Some were bought. Some were taken. Where a pledge-holder refused to be bought out, Valdemar besieged him, and when the castle fell he had it demolished rather than garrisoned — a policy that cost him a fortress but denied one to everybody else.

The money came from everywhere. Estonia, in 1346. Taxation, pressed hard and resented. Fines and confiscations, which had the useful property of transferring land to the crown at no cost. Vacancies in church appointments, which the crown could milk. And a coinage that got steadily worse, which is the oldest fiscal trick there is and which the chroniclers noticed and disliked.

None of this made him popular. A king who taxes hard in the decade after a plague, debases the coin, and takes castles off the men who hold them is a king with enemies, and the Jutland magnates in particular had done well out of the years without a crown and did not want a strong one back.

07 / NARRATIVEThe road from Middelfart

Danish terms in this section

væbner / ridder
Squire and knight — the two grades of the Danish armed landholding class.
forlig
A settlement or accord, negotiated rather than imposed.

In the middle 1350s Jutland rose against him, and the leader of the rising was Niels Bugge of Hald, one of the greatest landowners in the peninsula.

The revolt was serious enough that Valdemar had to negotiate rather than fight. The settlement came in 1360, after its leader was dead: the peace of Kalundborg promised an annual assembly at Nyborg, which is chapter 14's Danehof being asked for again by the same class for the same reasons.

Vignette · the ferry at Middelfart

In December 1358 Niels Bugge is on his way home from negotiating with the king at Slagelse. His road runs across Funen to the Little Belt, and at Middelfart he takes a boat across to Jutland.

He does not arrive. He and two companions are killed at the crossing, and a couple of local fishermen were later sentenced for it.

What follows tells you more than the killing. Valdemar denied any part in it. The town paid instead — this is the detail — and went on paying: the burghers of Middelfart owed an annual sum for the killing, the Buggespenge, and it was still being paid in 1874. Neither the denial nor the payment settled anything. To the Jutland nobility it was obvious that a king had disposed of the man who had forced him to negotiate, and the death became one of the standing grievances of the reign.

Nobody has ever established who ordered it, or whether anybody did. It is entirely possible that three men were robbed and killed on a winter ferry crossing, which happened, and that the timing did the rest.

Niels Bugge of Hald · the Little Belt crossing at Middelfart · December 1358 · [-]

Niels Bugge matters here for a reason beyond the mystery. The Danish nobility of this period is usually invisible in the narrative — a collective noun that opposes kings. He is a specific man with a specific grievance who organised a province, forced a monarch to the table, got terms, and was killed on his way home. The class that would spend the next three centuries constraining Danish kings is made of people like that.

08 / NARRATIVE1360

By 1360 Valdemar held almost the whole of the old kingdom west of the Sound. What was missing was the richest part of it.

Skåne, Halland and Blekinge had gone over to Magnus Eriksson of Sweden in 1332, and with them the herring markets of chapter 14 and the eastern shore of the Sound. Magnus had bought them, in effect, by paying off the German pledge-holder; the Skåne magnates had accepted him because there was no Danish king to accept instead.

In 1360 Valdemar got them back, and he did it with a combination of purchase, negotiation with disaffected Skåne magnates, a marriage alliance, and an army in the field. Magnus was in trouble at home and in no position to fight for a province whose leading men had already been approached. The transfer was formalised, and the eastern provinces were Danish again after twenty-eight years.

With Skåne came the Sound, and with the Sound came the thing that would pay Denmark's bills until 1857: control of both shores of the entrance to the Baltic. Valdemar did not invent a toll on shipping — that is Erik of Pomerania in 1429, chapter 18 — but he is the man who put both banks back in Danish hands, and everything the Sound Dues later made possible rests on 1360.

Which is exactly why the Hanse could not let it stand.

09 / NARRATIVEVisby, 27 July 1361

Danish terms in this section

Gotland
The large Baltic island, and Visby its walled town — a Hanseatic trading city with a German-speaking merchant elite and a Gutnish countryside.
Korsbetningen
'The cross meadow', outside Visby's east wall, where the dead of 1361 were buried in mass graves.

In the summer of 1361 Valdemar took a fleet to Gotland. He landed on the west coast, and the country people of the island — the farmers of the Gutnish parishes, not the merchants of Visby — came out to meet him.

Vignette · outside the east wall

On 27 July 1361 a militia of Gotland farmers formed up outside Visby and fought a professional Danish army. Between fifteen hundred and eighteen hundred of them died in a few hours. They were buried where they fell, in mass graves, in their armour — which is why we know so much about them.

The graves were excavated in the twentieth century and they are the single richest archaeological find for medieval European warfare anywhere. Mail coifs, mail shirts, coats of plates, gauntlets: armour that is otherwise known mainly from manuscript illustrations, here on the actual bodies. And the bodies say things the chronicles do not. Many are old men, and many are boys. A great many show healed injuries and joint disease from a lifetime of farm work. The wounds are terrible, and a high proportion are to the legs and to the head — where mail did not reach, or where a man already down was finished.

Some of the armour they wore was two generations out of date. They were farmers, wearing whatever their families had, standing in a field against men who did this for a living.

There is no name in this vignette, and the reason is the point: nobody wrote down who they were. What we have instead is more than eleven hundred excavated skeletons that can be measured, aged and read for old injuries — a collective far better evidenced as a collective than any individual in it will ever be.

The farmers of Gotland — no names recorded · Korsbetningen, outside Visby · 27 July 1361 · [n]

The town itself did not fight. Visby's merchants — largely German, and with a great deal to lose — opened the gates, agreed terms and paid. This is worth stating clearly, because the popular Danish and Swedish version has Valdemar sacking and burning Visby, and the most famous embellishment has three great vats filled with the town's gold and silver, later lost at sea. The vats are a nineteenth-century romance. What happened was a surrender, an indemnity, and a massacre that had already taken place outside the walls, of the people the town did not defend.

Visby was a Hanseatic town in a Baltic the Hanse regarded as its own. Taking it, a year after taking Skåne, was the act that turned commercial irritation into war.

10 / NARRATIVELosing to a league of towns

Danish terms in this section

Hansaen
The Hanse: an association of north German trading towns, led by Lübeck, with no king, no army of its own and no territory — and the dominant power in the Baltic for two hundred years.
Køln-konføderationen
The Confederation of Cologne, 1367: the alliance of Hanseatic towns and their partners formed specifically to destroy Valdemar's position.

The first round went to Denmark. A Hanseatic fleet attacked in 1362, was beaten at Helsingborg, and the towns made peace on poor terms in 1365.

The second round did not. In 1367 the Hanseatic towns met at Cologne and formed a confederation against him — Lübeck and the Wendish towns, the Prussian towns, the Dutch, and, crucially, Sweden and the counts of Holstein, who had their own reasons. This was a coalition of everybody in northern Europe with a grievance against Valdemar, and there were a lot of them.

In 1368 they attacked in force. Copenhagen was taken and its castle destroyed. Skanør and Falsterbo fell. Helsingborg held out for a year and then surrendered. Valdemar left the country altogether and spent much of the war in Germany trying to raise support, which did not come.

It is worth pausing on what beat him, because it is genuinely novel in Danish history. He was not defeated by a rival kingdom, or by an emperor, or by a dynastic claimant. He was defeated by an association of towns — an alliance of merchants with no monarch, no capital city and no standing army, that could nevertheless assemble a fleet, sustain a two-year war, and coordinate a coalition across the whole Baltic. Denmark's opponents up to this point had all been the kind of thing a king recognises. This was something else, and the Danish crown would spend the next two hundred years failing to work out how to fight it.

11 / NARRATIVEStralsund, 1370

The peace was made at Stralsund in May 1370, and Valdemar was not there. It was negotiated by his council, and he confirmed it afterwards.

The Hanse took four castles on the Sound — Skanør, Falsterbo, Malmö and Helsingborg — for fifteen years, and with them two-thirds of the revenues of the Skåne markets. Their trading privileges in Denmark were confirmed and extended. And the treaty included a clause stating that no future king of Denmark would be recognised without the towns' consent.

That last provision is the one that gets quoted, and the usual gloss on it — that it was a dead letter — is wrong. It was cashed at the first opportunity there was. Valdemar died five years later, and when Margrete set about getting her five-year-old son elected in 1376 she went to the Hanseatic towns and asked for their consent. They gave it, because the rival claimant was Mecklenburg, and they wanted a Mecklenburg Denmark even less than they wanted a strong Danish one.

So a league of merchant towns wrote a claim on the succession of a European kingdom into a treaty, the kingdom signed it, and the clause was honoured the first time it was tested — by Margrete, who is the subject of chapter 16 and who found it useful.

Set against 1360, this is a hard year. Set against 1340 it is less simple than it looks. In 1340 Valdemar held a quarter of Jutland and the country had no king. In 1370 he held every province of the old realm, and had conceded a large slice of the revenue of one of them for fifteen years, together with privileges to a partner Denmark had to trade with in any case. The castles came back. Historians differ, reasonably, on whether Stralsund is best read as a catastrophe or as an expensive settlement that left the essential achievement standing.

12 / NARRATIVEThe ten-year-old

Danish terms in this section

Margrete
Valdemar's younger daughter, born 1353. The most capable ruler in Danish history, and the subject of the next chapter.
arvehylding
Acclamation of an heir. Denmark remained formally an elective monarchy, which is why the succession in 1375 was not automatic.

Valdemar's son Christoffer died in 1363, of wounds. After that there was no male heir, and everything the reign had assembled depended on two daughters.

Vignette · a wedding

In 1363, in Copenhagen, Valdemar's younger daughter is married. She is ten years old. Her husband is Håkon, king of Norway and son of Magnus Eriksson of Sweden — the same Magnus from whom her father had just taken Skåne, now in enough difficulty at home to need a Danish alliance.

She is sent to Norway to be brought up, in the household of Birgitta's daughter. She has a son at seventeen. Her husband dies when she is twenty-seven.

Nothing about this arrangement was designed to produce a ruler. It was a standard dynastic transaction of a kind that happened to hundreds of aristocratic girls in fourteenth-century Europe, and its purpose was to settle a quarrel between two kings, neither of whom expected the bride to matter. Within thirty years she governed Denmark, Norway and Sweden.

Margrete Valdemarsdatter, aged ten · Copenhagen · 1363 · [-]

Valdemar died at Gurre in October 1375, and there was no obvious successor. Two grandsons had claims: Albrecht, the young duke of Mecklenburg — Valdemar's grandson through the elder daughter Ingeborg, and nephew of the different Albrecht of Mecklenburg who was at that moment king of Sweden — and Oluf, the five-year-old son of Margrete and Håkon. Denmark was an elective monarchy, the council chose, and Margrete — with an energy that surprised everybody — got them to choose her son. She also went to the Hanseatic towns for the consent their treaty entitled them to, and got that too. Chapter 16, Margrete I and the making of the union, is what she did with that.

It is worth ending on what the thirty-five years actually produced, because the last five of them were a defeat and it is easy to let that colour the whole.

In 1340 there was no Danish state. There was a territory that had been a kingdom, held in pieces by four creditors, with no king, no revenue and no capital. Valdemar did not restore Denmark to greatness; he was beaten by a shipping cartel in his last war and he died with the Sound castles in foreign hands. What he did was reassemble the thing itself — put the provinces back under one crown, recover both shores of the Sound, and hand on a realm that was territorially whole for the first time in sixty years.

He did it by selling a province, debasing a coin, taxing a halved population, and killing as many as eighteen hundred farmers in a field on Gotland. Danish tradition remembers him as the king who put the country back together, and that is true. It is worth also remembering what it was assembled out of, and who paid.

Myth-check

"The Black Death killed a third of Denmark" — or a half, depending on who is telling it.
Nobody knows, and the range in the literature runs from about 30% to about 50% because the Danish evidence is genuinely poor. There is no Danish equivalent of the English manorial rolls. What we have is chronicle statements, monasteries reporting shortages, the jump in soul-masses at Ribe from about one a year to seventeen, and the indirect evidence of remitted death sentences, new mills and abandoned farms. A confident percentage for Denmark is a percentage borrowed from somewhere else.
"Valdemar Atterdag sacked and burned Visby, and the treasure went down at sea."
Visby surrendered and paid an indemnity; the town was not stormed. The killing happened outside the east wall, where the island's farmers fought and between fifteen hundred and eighteen hundred died. The three great vats of gold lost overboard are a nineteenth-century romantic invention.
"The plague came to Denmark on a ghost ship that stranded in north Jutland with its crew dead."
A good story, and a travelling one — the same tale is told of Bergen, and of several English ports. Plague reached Denmark by the ordinary Baltic and North Sea shipping routes, from more than one direction, in 1349–50.
"Denmark lost Estonia."
Denmark sold it, deliberately, in 1346, for nineteen thousand marks, in order to fund the redemption of Danish territory. It was an asset disposal, not a defeat — and the same logic will reappear in 1917 with the West Indies.
"Valdemar Atterdag made Denmark great again."
He made it whole, which is a different and in the circumstances more remarkable thing. He also lost his last war to a confederation of trading towns and signed a treaty giving them the Sound castles, two-thirds of the Skåne market revenues, and a stated claim on the choice of his successor.

Summary

IF YOU REMEMBER FIVE THINGSThe page in five

  1. Valdemar was handed about a quarter of Jutland in 1340 and spent thirty-five years redeeming the rest, district by district, funding it by selling Estonia to the Teutonic Order in 1346, by heavy taxation, and by debasing the coinage.

  2. The plague reached Denmark in 1350, thirty-three months after Sicily. Danish mortality cannot be established — the estimates run from a third to a half — and the best direct evidence is a jump in paid soul-masses at Ribe from about one a year to seventeen.

  3. Afterwards land was abundant and people were scarce, which reversed three centuries of economics: farms stood empty, rents and grain prices fell, wages rose, surviving peasants were better off, and landlords were not.

  4. Skåne, Halland and Blekinge were recovered in 1360, putting both shores of the Sound back in Danish hands. Gotland followed in 1361, where between fifteen hundred and eighteen hundred island farmers died outside Visby's wall while the town itself made terms.

  5. The Hanseatic towns formed a confederation in 1367 and beat him. At Stralsund in 1370 they took four Sound castles for fifteen years, two-thirds of the Skåne market revenues, and a stated say in the next royal election.

Questions & discussion

FOUR KINDSWork the material

Recall

Did the facts land?

  • What did Valdemar actually hold in 1340, and what did he do about it?
  • What was sold in 1346, to whom, and what for?
  • Name three kinds of evidence for the plague in Denmark, and say what each can and cannot show.
  • How did Helvig's marriage help Valdemar begin, and what does the record say about how it ended?
  • What happened outside Visby's east wall on 27 July 1361 — and what happened inside it?
  • Name the three main concessions at Stralsund in 1370.

Causal

Why did that follow from this?

  • Why did a plague that killed a third or more of the population make the surviving peasants better off?
  • Why did the same event make the nobility poorer, and what did they do about it over the following century?
  • Why did taking Skåne in 1360 make a war with the Hanse almost unavoidable?
  • Why could an association of towns with no king and no army defeat a European kingdom?
  • Why does 1360 matter more for the next four hundred years of Danish finances than 1370 does for the next fifteen?

Counterfactual

Pull one thread and see what moves.

  • Suppose Valdemar had kept Estonia. Does he still redeem Jutland — and does Denmark end up with a Baltic province to defend in the century of the Teutonic Order's collapse?
  • Suppose the plague had arrived in 1330 instead of 1350, while the country was pledged and kingless. Is there a Denmark for anybody to redeem?
  • Suppose Christoffer had lived and inherited in 1375. Margrete is a Norwegian queen consort and nothing more. What happens to the Nordic union?

Contested

Where historians actually disagree.

  • What was Danish plague mortality? The figures in circulation are extrapolations from better- documented countries. The honest Danish answer is a range so wide it barely constrains anything, and it is worth asking what would count as evidence that could narrow it.
  • Did the agrarian crisis begin before 1349? If the expansion had already stalled — marginal land failing, farms given up, the famine of 1315–17 — then the plague accelerated a decline rather than causing one, and the late-medieval Danish countryside needs a different explanation.
  • Who killed Niels Bugge, and did anyone order it? A king with an obvious motive, ferrymen with an obvious opportunity, and no evidence either way.
  • Was Stralsund a catastrophe or an expensive settlement? The castles were returnable, the succession clause was exercised once and then lapsed with the fifteen-year term, and the alternative was a war Denmark was losing. How much the treaty actually cost depends on what you think the Hanse could have taken anyway.
  • Was the reconquest statesmanship or opportunism? Valdemar exploited a plague, a Swedish crisis and a Holstein succession dispute, and improvised throughout. Whether that is a plan executed or a series of chances taken is not a question the sources can settle.

Sources

LIGHT SOURCINGWhat this is built on

  • Sjællandske Krønike The Zealand chronicle — near-contemporary, brief, and the main Danish narrative for the reign, including the mortality of 1350 and the order for water mills.
  • The soul-mass registers, Ribe Masses paid for named dead people. The closest thing Denmark has to plague statistics, and a record only of those who could afford one.
  • Kong Valdemars Jordebog & later crown accounts The thirteenth-century survey of chapter 13, used as the baseline against which the deserted farms of the fourteenth are measured.
  • The Korsbetningen mass graves, Visby Excavated in the twentieth century; the best archaeological evidence for medieval European warfare that exists, and the reason the men of 1361 are not anonymous.
  • The Stralsund treaty, 1370 The text survives. What its succession clause meant in practice is another matter.
  • danmarkshistorien.dk Aarhus University. Valdemar Atterdag, the Black Death in Denmark, the agrarian crisis and the Hanseatic wars.

Go and look

STILL THEREPlaces you can visit

  • Vordingborg & Gåsetårnet Valdemar's principal castle and the base for the southern wars. The Goose Tower is his, and Danmarks Borgcenter is built over the excavations.
  • Gurre Slotsruin, North Zealand Where he died in 1375. Foundations in a wood, and a site with more legend attached to it than masonry.
  • Sorø Klosterkirke Valdemar Atterdag is buried here, a few metres from Absalon of chapter 13.
  • Gotlands Museum & Korsbetningen, Visby The armour and the skeletons from 1361 are in the museum; the meadow where the graves were is a short walk outside the still-complete medieval wall.
  • Skanör & Falsterbo, Skåne Two of the four castles handed to the Hanse in 1370. Low ruins on the sand, beside the herring ground that made them worth taking.
  • A deserted village Hundreds of Danish ødegårde are visible as banks and hollows under pasture, often near a surviving church that is too large for its parish. Once you know the shape you see them everywhere.
