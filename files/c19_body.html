Schleswig-Holstein and the union's collapse

1460 – 1523 · Christian 1. · Kong Hans · Christian 2. · Sten Sture · Gustav Vasa

A Danish king is elected duke of Schleswig and count of Holstein on conditions, and one clause of those conditions goes off four hundred years later. In between, the Northern Isles are pledged away, an army drowns in a marsh, and Sweden leaves for good.

01 / NARRATIVEA childless uncle, 1459

Danish terms in this section

ridderskabet
The knighthood: the nobility of Schleswig and Holstein, acting as one corporation across both territories. They are the electors here, and the party whose interests the settlement protects.
arveret
Right of inheritance. Danish law let a claim pass through a woman; German practice mostly did not. Which law applied decided who got the duchy.

Adolf 8. of Schauenburg died in December 1459 without children. He had been duke of Schleswig since chapter 18's settlement of 1435, count of Holstein by inheritance, and — the detail that decides everything — he was the maternal uncle of the king of Denmark. He had brought Christian up, had declined the Danish throne himself in 1448 and recommended his nephew for it, and had then spent eleven years making sure the Danish crown did not get his duchy.

With his death the Schauenburg line was extinct in the male line and two bodies of law pointed in opposite directions. By German practice, a fief without male heirs reverted to its overlord — which for Schleswig meant the Danish crown, and for Holstein the Emperor. By Danish inheritance law, a claim could descend through a woman, and the nearest such claimant was Christian 1. himself.

What actually decided it was neither. The knighthood of the two territories — the Rantzaus, the Ahlefeldts, the Reventlows, families who held land on both sides of the Eider and had for generations — met and elected. They had no intention of being divided between a Danish king and an imperial administration, and no intention of losing the privileges they had accumulated while their rulers were weak. They wanted one lord, and they wanted him to sign.

02 / NARRATIVERibe, 5 March 1460

Danish terms in this section

Ribebrevet
The Ribe letter, or Ribe privilege: the terms on which Christian 1. was accepted as duke of Schleswig and count of Holstein.
hertugdømmerne
'The duchies' — the pair, spoken of together from now on, which is itself the point.
Ejderen
The Eider: the river dividing Schleswig from Holstein, and thus the northern boundary of the Holy Roman Empire. Chapter 12's frontier.

At Ribe on 5 March 1460 the knighthood elected Christian 1. duke of Schleswig and count of Holstein, and he accepted their conditions. He undertook to preserve their privileges, to rule the two territories as their own countries rather than as parts of Denmark — Holstein could not be annexed to the kingdom, and Schleswig, though a Danish fief, could not simply be absorbed either — and to keep the peace in them. The knighthood reserved the right to resist him if he broke the agreement, which was standard in charters of this kind and had been in Danish ones since 1282.

And there was a clause promising that these lands would be held in good peace, and that they remain forever together undivided — unde dat se bliven ewich tosamende ungedelt.

What Christian got was a great deal: the wealthiest territory on his southern border, an imperial title, and the end of a two-hundred-year quarrel that had cost his predecessors a duchy, a war and in chapter 14 a king. In 1474 the Emperor Frederick 3. raised Holstein to a duchy, so that the Danish king was now a duke twice over and a prince of the Empire.

What he gave was the thing the series has been watching kings give since 1282: a written guarantee to a nobility that it would keep what it had. The duchies were governed by their own knighthood, in Low German, under their own law, with their own estates. They were attached to the Danish crown at the top and to nothing else.

And it did not even hold in its own terms. In 1490 Christian's sons Hans and Frederik divided the duchies between them — the first of a series of partitions that continued for two centuries and made the map of the region a patchwork of enclaves. Whatever the 1460 promise meant, it plainly did not prevent that.

03 / NARRATIVEWhat one sentence became

Danish terms in this section

slesvig-holstenisme
The nineteenth-century political movement holding that the two duchies formed an indivisible unit with a right to be independent of Denmark. Its historical foundation was the 1460 clause.
Ejderpolitikken
The Danish counter-policy: Denmark to the Eider, with Schleswig incorporated and Holstein let go. The direct cause of the war of 1848.

For three and a half centuries the phrase sat in an archive and nobody thought about it. What happened next is one of the clearest examples in Danish history of a document being reactivated for a purpose it was never written for.

After 1815, Friedrich Christoph Dahlmann — historian, and secretary to the Schleswig-Holstein knighthood — went back to the Ribe letter and argued that the clause proved something specific: that the duchies belonged constitutionally to each other, and therefore not to Denmark. In 1841 a doctor in Aabenraa named A.W. Neuber put it into a poem. Sung at a song festival in 1845, it spread through the duchies on pipe-bowls, handkerchiefs and lithographs in the modern Low German form up ewig ungedeelt, and from there into the rising of 1848, the war that followed, and eventually 1864.

Two things are worth saying about that, and this page will not adjudicate between them. The first is that the modern slogan is not the medieval sentence: up ewig ungedeelt is Neuber's 1841 phrasing of ewich tosamende ungedelt. The second is what the original clause meant. The older reading — territorial indivisibility, the two lands never to be separated — is the one the nineteenth century needed. Most historians now read it more narrowly: either as a promise that the lordship would not be split among heirs, or, as Carsten Jahnke has argued, as a conventional land-peace formula, a promise of internal peace and nothing more. The 1490 partition is a strong argument for the narrow reading.

Either way, the practical consequence of 1460 is not in dispute, and it is the thread this series follows to 1920. A Danish king now ruled a German-speaking territory under German law, joined to a Danish-speaking one under Danish law, both of them separate from the kingdom he was elected to rule. Nothing about that arrangement had an answer to the question that the nineteenth century was going to ask: which nation do these people belong to?

04 / NARRATIVEA dowry never paid

Danish terms in this section

pant
A pledge: property handed over as security for a debt, redeemable on payment. Chapter 14's mechanism, applied here to two archipelagos.
skattland
Tributary land, from chapter 16. Orkney and Shetland had been Norwegian since the Viking Age and came to Denmark through Norway.

In 1468 Christian 1. married his daughter Margaret to James 3. of Scotland. The dowry was set at 60,000 Rhenish florins, which he did not have.

He paid a small part in cash and pledged Orkney against 50,000 of the remainder. The following year, still short, he pledged Shetland against a further 8,000. Both pledges were redeemable — this was a mortgage, not a sale, and Danish and Norwegian sovereigns raised the question of redemption repeatedly over the following centuries.

Nobody ever paid. The islands were annexed to the Scottish crown in 1472, and Norse law and the Norn language faded out of them over the next two hundred years. It is the quietest territorial loss in the whole series: no battle, no treaty of cession, no date on which anyone decided. A king short of cash for a wedding pledged two island groups that had been Norwegian for five hundred years, and the redemption clause was never exercised.

The map at section 08 shows what this looks like. In chapter 16 the western panel filled for the first time and Orkney and Shetland were in it. In 1500 they are simply not coloured, and the run of the Danish realm in the North Atlantic is one archipelago shorter for the rest of the series.

05 / NARRATIVEBrunkeberg, 1471

Danish terms in this section

rigsforstander
Regent — the office Sweden used for fifty years rather than admit either a Danish king or a Swedish one. Sten Sture the Elder held it, on and off, from 1470 to 1503.
unionsvenner
'Union friends': the Swedish party, largely aristocratic and clerical, that preferred a Danish king to a Swedish regent. Sweden's argument in this period is as much internal as national.

Christian 1. had been accepted in Sweden in 1457 and expelled in 1464. In 1471 he came back with a fleet and an army to take it again, and on 10 October he was beaten outside Stockholm, at Brunkeberg, by a Swedish force under Sten Sture the Elder. The king was wounded in the mouth, losing teeth, and shipped home.

Brunkeberg matters more for what was made of it than for what happened. Sten Sture commissioned for Stockholm's Storkyrkan a great wooden Saint George and the Dragon, carved by Bernt Notke of Lübeck and set up in 1489: the saint is Sweden, the dragon is Denmark, and the princess is Stockholm. It is one of the most impressive objects of the northern Middle Ages and it is a piece of political argument in oak and elk-horn. Sweden was learning to tell a story about itself in which Denmark was the antagonist, and it had fifty years of material to work with before the story acquired its ending.

For the next half-century the pattern holds. Sweden is governed by regents of the Sture party; Danish kings are periodically accepted and periodically thrown out; the Swedish council manoeuvres between the two, as chapter 18 saw it learn to do. Kong Hans got the crown back in 1497 after beating Sten Sture at Rotebro, and lost it again in 1501, at which point his queen Christina held Stockholm castle through a siege of many months before surrendering.

Meanwhile in Europe

The 1460s and 1470s are the decades in which the medieval political map stops being medieval. Constantinople had fallen in 1453 and by 1463 the Ottomans were in Bosnia and at war with Venice. England spent the period in the Wars of the Roses, deposing and restoring the same king twice. France under Louis 11. was assembling the territories that made it the strongest monarchy in Europe, and in 1477 the Burgundian state — the richest and most cultured in the north — collapsed when Charles the Bold was killed at Nancy, its lands passing by marriage to the Habsburgs. That marriage is why Denmark's Christian 2. later has an emperor for a brother-in-law.

Meanwhile printing spread from Mainz across the continent in a single generation: Cologne 1466, Lübeck 1475, Odense by 1482. Christian 1.'s university of 1479 belongs to a European wave, and it is the same technology and the same wave that carries Luther's argument north forty years later.

06 / NARRATIVEA fleet, a university, and a bound peasantry

Danish terms in this section

vornedskab
The obligation binding a peasant on Zealand and the islands to remain on the estate where he was born, and to take a farm when the lord required it. It does not apply in Jutland. Abolished in 1702.
orlogsflåde
A standing war fleet, as distinct from ships requisitioned when needed. Hans built one, and it is the ancestor of the navy of chapters 22 and 24.
universitet
Copenhagen's, founded 1479 by papal licence: four faculties, a handful of teachers, and the beginning of a Danish learned class.

Between Brunkeberg and Ditmarschen the Danish state was doing better than its foreign policy suggests, and three developments in particular reach forward.

In 1479 Christian 1. founded a university at Copenhagen, on papal licence obtained during his journey to Rome in 1474. It was small and it struggled. It matters because until then every educated Dane had studied abroad — at Paris, Cologne, Erfurt, Leipzig, Rostock — and the men who bring Lutheranism home in chapter 20 are precisely the ones who went to Wittenberg. A domestic university did not stop that. It did mean there was somewhere for the argument to be had.

Kong Hans, who succeeded in 1481, built a permanent war fleet, hired Scottish and Dutch captains, and used it — against Sweden, and in the war with Lübeck of 1509–12, which Denmark won and which ended a century in which the towns had dictated terms. He also protected the market towns and encouraged Dutch merchants, on the reasonable view that a king with his own ships and his own burghers was less dependent on both his nobility and the Hanse.

And in the same decades the Danish peasantry on Zealand and the islands was formally bound. Vornedskab tied a man to the estate on which he was born: he could not leave without permission, and could be required to take over a vacant farm. It is the endpoint of the process chapter 17 described — deserted farms, landlords needing tenants, an aristocracy building estates and an export trade — and it is the answer to a labour shortage by law rather than by wages. Jutland, where the peasantry stayed free of it, is on the other side of the country from the capital, and the difference in tenure between east and west Denmark outlasts everything else in this chapter.

07 / NARRATIVEHemmingstedt, 17 February 1500

Danish terms in this section

Ditmarsken
Dithmarschen: the marsh country between the Eider and the Elbe, self-governing, without a lord, under its own written law from 1447. Usually called a peasant republic, which overstates it slightly and captures it well enough.
Den Store Garde
The Great Guard, sometimes called the Saxon Guard: a professional mercenary corps of about four thousand under Thomas Slentz.
Dannebrog
The Danish banner. The one carried in 1500 was believed to be very old. It was lost here, and the legend of its miraculous origin is first written down shortly afterwards — an order of events the myth-check returns to.

Ditmarschen had no lord anyone could find. It lay formally under the archbishop of Bremen, who did not govern it; it was run by its own parishes and its own law, codified in Low German in 1447 and printed at Lübeck around 1487. Chapter 17 noted that it had killed Gerhard 6. of Holstein in 1404; it had beaten a Holstein count in 1309 as well. The Emperor had enfeoffed Christian 1. with it in 1473, which changed nothing on the ground.

In February 1500 Kong Hans and his brother Duke Frederik went to collect. The army was the largest and best-equipped that had ever been assembled in the region: the knighthood of both duchies, Danish nobles and levies, artillery, north German nobility, and the Great Guard. Perhaps twelve thousand men in all, with a baggage train sized for the plunder. They took Meldorf on 14 February.

Three days later the column was moving on Heide along a single causeway through the marsh, in February, with thaw and rain. It found the road blocked by an earth bank. The Ditmarschers — perhaps two thousand, under a local man named Wulf Isebrand — attacked the head of the column from both sides, where the flanks could not deploy and the artillery went into the ditch. Then they opened the sluices.

Behind the fighting, thousands of men who could not see what was happening kept pressing forward along the causeway. The Guard broke. The retreat became a rout through flooding marsh, and men in armour drowned in it. Perhaps four thousand of the royal army died; Ditmarschen lost fewer than a hundred. Eleven members of the Ahlefeldt family died on the field, six of the Buchwalds. The knighthood that had elected Christian 1. at Ribe forty years earlier lost a generation in an afternoon.

Vignette · Hans von Ahlefeldt, on the causeway near Hemmingstedt

He was born about 1440 and he is carrying the banner. That is the honour and it is the position at the head of the column, in front of the Guard, on a road eight metres wide with water on both sides.

When the column stops he is at the point where it stops. He is killed there, with six squires around him, and the Dannebrog goes down with him — wrapped around him, according to the account that comes down to us.

The Ditmarschers took it, along with the other banners, and hung it in a church at Wöhrden. It stayed there for fifty-nine years, until Frederik 2. conquered Ditmarschen in 1559 and demanded it back. He got it, and hung it in Slesvig cathedral, where it was reported to be already almost destroyed by damp and age. The last of it seems to have perished around 1660.

So the oldest flag in Europe, if that is what it was, exists now only in descriptions of its decay. What replaced it is the story: that this banner had fallen from the sky in Estonia in 1219, in chapter 13, and that Denmark had been carrying the same cloth ever since.

Hans von Ahlefeldt, knight, standard-bearer · the causeway near Hemmingstedt · 17 February 1500

08 / NARRATIVEDenmark in 1500

Danish terms in this section

købstad
A market town with a royal charter and a monopoly on trade in its district. There were something over eighty of them, and most were tiny.
herremand
A nobleman holding a manor — the class that had assembled its estates out of chapter 17's deserted farms and was now exporting oxen.

Set beside the map of 1397, this is a smaller thing pretending otherwise. The three kingdoms are still coloured, but Sweden is held for four years out of a possible sixty. Two archipelagos have gone. What has been added is at the bottom of the map: two duchies, attached to the person of the king rather than to the kingdom, with their own nobility and their own law.

Underneath, the country was recovering. The population was growing again after a century and a half of decline; deserted farms were being retaken; the ox trade of chapter 18 was running south in tens of thousands of head. Danish towns were small — Copenhagen perhaps ten thousand people, Malmø and Aalborg a few thousand each, most of the eighty-odd market towns well under a thousand — but they were growing, and their burghers were beginning to matter politically, which is the precondition for chapter 20.

And the church stood at its greatest extent. It held something like a third of the land, its bishops were great magnates who sat in the council and commanded castles, and the parish churches were being vaulted and painted as fast as parishes could pay. Every crisis of the next forty years runs through the fact that the richest institution in Denmark was one that the crown did not control and the nobility could not inherit from.

Meanwhile in Europe

The generation on either side of 1500 is the one everything is dated from. Columbus crossed in 1492 and Vasco da Gama reached India in 1498, which within thirty years moved the pepper trade from Venice to Lisbon and began the slow shift of European wealth to the Atlantic seaboard — a shift that runs directly through the Sound, and eventually makes Amsterdam the busiest payer of Danish tolls. Printing had reached Odense by 1482. In 1494 the French invasion of Italy began forty years of war that turned the papacy into one Italian power among several.

Then, in October 1517, an Augustinian friar in Wittenberg published objections to the sale of indulgences, and by 1519 the argument had escaped into print in a way no previous heresy had managed. In the same year Charles of Habsburg — the brother-in-law of Denmark's Christian 2. — was elected Holy Roman Emperor, inheriting Spain, the Low Countries, Austria and the American silver. Every Danish decision in the next twenty years is taken inside that frame.

09 / NARRATIVEChristian 2., and the woman who kept the accounts

Danish terms in this section

borgerskab
The burgher class of the market towns. Christian 2. governed with them against the nobility, which is why he is remembered so differently by different people.
Sigbrits regnskaber
The accounts kept by Sigbrit Villumsdatter, who ran the Sound toll and much of the royal finances without holding any office at all.

Christian 2. succeeded his father in 1513. He had governed Norway for his father since 1506, he married Elisabeth of Habsburg in 1515 — sister of the future Charles 5. — and he was, by any measure, the most capable and the most alarming Danish king of the period.

His programme was consistent. He legislated for the towns and for the peasantry against the interests of the nobility and the higher clergy: town law and land law issued in 1521–22 that attacked vornedskab, restricted the nobility's private jurisdiction, protected peasants from arbitrary eviction, regulated trade in the towns' favour, and set up a court where subjects could sue their lords. He brought in Dutch farmers to Amager and Dutch merchants to Copenhagen. He wanted a Danish trading company on Dutch lines.

He was also vindictive, and had the run of a state with no institutional check except a council he despised. In 1517 he had Torben Oxe, a nobleman and the governor of Copenhagen castle, executed after a trial the council refused to countenance — the charge was connected with the death of Dyveke, the king's mistress. Whatever the truth of it, the lesson the nobility took was that this king would kill them by procedure.

Vignette · Sigbrit Villumsdatter in Copenhagen

She arrived from Bergen, Dutch-born, the mother of Dyveke; and when Dyveke died in 1517 she did not go away. She stayed, and she took over the royal finances.

She held no office. There was no office for her to hold. She managed the Sound toll accounts, advised on trade policy, corresponded with Dutch merchants, and by the end was the person the king listened to about money — a middle-aged commoner woman, foreign, in a court of noblemen whose entire claim rested on birth. The Swedish and Danish nobility loathed her and said so in language that has not improved with age. When the council wanted to describe everything wrong with the reign, they described her.

She went into exile with Christian in 1523, and after that the record thins out. She is thought to have died in the Netherlands, possibly around 1532, and there is a persistent story that she was burned as a witch which has nothing solid behind it.

What she demonstrates is not a scandal but a fact about the reign: Christian 2. built his government out of people who owed everything to him and nothing to the council, and the burgher and Dutch connections were the point, not a weakness. It is also why the account of him that survives was written by his enemies.

Sigbrit Villumsdatter, keeper of the king's accounts · Copenhagen · 1517–1523

10 / NARRATIVEStockholm, November 1520

Danish terms in this section

Det Stockholmske Blodbad
The Stockholm bloodbath, 8–9 November 1520. In Swedish, Stockholms blodbad.
kætteri
Heresy. A charge that mattered here for a legal reason: an amnesty for political offences does not cover a crime against the church.
lejde
Safe-conduct, or the guarantee of amnesty on surrender. Its violation is the heart of the matter.

Christian 2. invaded Sweden in 1520 with a professional army and a claim that had been dormant for two decades. The Swedish regent, Sten Sture the Younger, was wounded on the ice of Lake Åsunden in January and died in February. His widow, Kristina Gyllenstierna, held Stockholm.

She held it until September, and surrendered on terms: a general amnesty for everything done during the resistance. On 4 November Christian was crowned king of Sweden in Stockholm by Gustav Trolle, the archbishop of Uppsala, whom the Sture party had deposed and imprisoned and whose castle they had demolished.

On 7 November, at the end of three days of feasting, the doors were closed. Trolle laid a formal complaint before the king: his deposition and the destruction of his castle had been an offence against the church, and those responsible were heretics. The point of the framing is exact — the amnesty covered rebellion, and heresy was not rebellion. A tribunal sat, and on 8 and 9 November the executions were carried out in Stortorget, the great square below the castle.

How many died is disputed, and the disagreement is more informative than a number would be. Gustav Trolle, who had laid the complaint, said fifty or sixty. Jürgen Homuth, the German officer who did the work, said eighty-two — the traditional figure, and the count of a man who had reason to know how many he had despatched. Olaus Magnus, a canon of Linköping who watched it, put it first at somewhat over ninety and then, in a note he wrote by hand in the margin of a copy of Saxo's Gesta Danorum — a volume the Swedish national library acquired only in 2018 — at over a hundred and twenty, adding that he beheld it all with horror.

Part of the spread is a question of what is being counted. The nobles were beheaded with the sword; the burgomasters and councillors of Stockholm were hanged from a gallows put up in the square; and the second day's victims were servants, hangers-on and bystanders, one of them reportedly executed for weeping and one fetched out of his barber's shop. A tally kept by the headsman and a tally kept by a horrified eyewitness are not counting the same event.

Two bishops, Vincens of Skara and Matthias of Strängnäs, were beheaded, which shocked Europe. So were fourteen members of the council, burgomasters, councillors of Stockholm, and ordinary burghers, some of them apparently for protesting. Sten Sture's body was dug up and burned. Kristina Gyllenstierna and a group of noblewomen were sent to prison in Denmark.

Vignette · Kristina Gyllenstierna, Stockholm castle, September 1520

Her husband has been dead since February. She has been holding Stockholm since, with the city council, against a besieging army and a fleet, and she is negotiating the surrender herself.

What she gets is written: a general amnesty, for everyone, for everything. On that basis she opens the gates.

Ten weeks later she is standing in the castle listening to a churchman explain that an amnesty for rebellion does not cover heresy, and watching the men who signed the surrender with her walk out into the square. She is imprisoned in Denmark, in the Blue Tower, with her mother-in-law and other noblewomen; her mother-in-law dies there. She is released in 1524, after Christian has himself been deposed, and lives in Sweden until 1559 — long enough to see the country she surrendered become a separate kingdom under the man whose father was killed in the square.

Kristina Gyllenstierna, widow of the regent of Sweden · Stockholm castle · September 1520

Among the dead was Erik Johansson, a councillor. His son Gustav Eriksson Vasa had been a hostage in Denmark, had escaped, and was in Dalarna within months raising the mining districts — the same country, and much the same argument, as Engelbrekt's in chapter 18. By 1521 he was regent. On 6 June 1523 he was elected king of Sweden, and the Kalmar Union was over.

11 / NARRATIVE1523: two kings leave

Danish terms in this section

opsigelsesbrev
The letter of renunciation, from chapter 18. The Jutland nobility used the instrument of 1439 again, on 20 January 1523.
landflygtig
In exile. Christian 2. spent eight years abroad and twenty-seven in Danish prisons, which is longer than he reigned.

The end came from the south, not from Sweden. The Jutland nobility, with the bishops behind them, renounced their allegiance on 20 January 1523 and offered the crown to the king's uncle, Frederik, duke of Schleswig and Holstein — the surviving half of the 1490 partition, and a man the knighthood of the duchies had every reason to prefer.

Christian had an army, a fleet and the towns, and did not fight. On 20 April 1523 he sailed from Copenhagen with his family, Sigbrit, and the archives, for the Netherlands and his imperial brother-in-law. He spent eight years trying to raise a force, invaded Norway in 1531, was captured in 1532 under a safe-conduct that was not honoured — the symmetry with Stockholm did not escape contemporaries — and spent the next twenty-seven years in captivity at Sønderborg and Kalundborg. He died in 1559.

So the union ends with a Danish king in exile, a Swedish king newly crowned, and a Holstein duke on the Danish throne. It is worth asking what actually died. As a working three-kingdom monarchy the union had been finished since 1448, as chapter 18 argued; between then and 1520 a Danish king governed Sweden for eleven years out of seventy-five. What ended in 1523 was the claim, and the claim's end was made permanent by the manner of it — because after Stortorget, Swedish separateness had a founding atrocity to point at, and no Swedish politician who wanted a career could argue for a Danish king again.

Frederik 1. signed a håndfæstning that went further than any before it: he undertook not to make war without the council's consent, confirmed the nobility's jurisdiction over their tenants, and promised to persecute heretics — a clause he would spend his reign not honouring. He renounced the Swedish claim in 1524. And the country he took over had a king in exile with a real following among the burghers and the peasants, a church at the height of its wealth and the start of its unpopularity, and a set of new books arriving from Wittenberg.

Myth-check

"Dannebrog fell from the sky at Lyndanisse in 1219."
Chapter 13 argued the legend was probably formed around 1500, and this is where the loop closes. No source within two hundred years of 1219 mentions it. The story appears in writing in the 1520s, and the accounts that carry it are written in the decades after the royal banner — believed ancient, carried by Hans von Ahlefeldt — was lost at Hemmingstedt in 1500 and hung in a Ditmarschen church. A flag needs a miraculous origin most urgently at the point where the physical object has been taken by peasants. That is an argument about probability, not a proof; but the sequence is hard to look away from.
"Up ewig ungedeelt is what Christian 1. promised in 1460."
The 1460 words are dat se bliven ewich tosamende ungedelt. The modern slogan is A.W. Neuber's 1841 rendering, popularised at a song festival in 1845 after F.C. Dahlmann had rediscovered the clause and given it a constitutional meaning it had not carried before. What it meant in 1460 is genuinely disputed: undivided lordship, or simply internal peace. The strongest argument against the territorial reading is that Christian's own sons partitioned the duchies in 1490 and nobody said a word.
"The Stockholm bloodbath was Danish cruelty."
It was ordered by a Danish king and it was a violation of terms of surrender. It was also brought about by a Swedish archbishop's complaint, tried by a court containing Swedes, and aimed at one faction of the Swedish aristocracy on behalf of another. The heresy framing was a legal device to get round the amnesty, and it was needed precisely because the amnesty was binding. Calling it Danish villainy and calling it a Swedish civil war are both true, and the first version is the one that built a nation.
"Christian 2. was a tyrant." or "Christian 2. was the people's king."
Both, and the reputation has been rewritten at least twice. He legislated for burghers and peasants against the nobility, and the peasants of the Count's Feud in chapter 20 fought to bring him back. He also executed Torben Oxe against his council's judgment and presided over Stortorget. Danish histories written under the nobility called him a tyrant; nineteenth-century national liberals rediscovered him as a moderniser; twentieth-century work has him as both, and unusually able.
"Denmark sold Orkney and Shetland to Scotland."
They were pledged, not sold — mortgaged against a dowry Christian 1. could not pay, 50,000 florins on Orkney in 1468 and 8,000 on Shetland in 1469, redeemable in principle. Scotland annexed them in 1472 and no redemption was ever made, though Danish and Norwegian governments raised the question for centuries afterwards.

Summary

IF YOU REMEMBER FIVE THINGSThe page in five

  1. At Ribe on 5 March 1460 the Schleswig-Holstein knighthood elected Christian 1. duke and count on conditions: their privileges preserved, the duchies not annexed to Denmark, and a promise that the lands remain ewich tosamende ungedelt. One king now held three territories under three different laws.

  2. That clause was rediscovered after 1815 by F.C. Dahlmann and turned into the slogan up ewig ungedeelt by a poem of 1841. What it meant in 1460 is disputed; the 1490 partition of the duchies by Christian's own sons is the strongest evidence against the reading the nineteenth century needed.

  3. Orkney was pledged to Scotland in 1468 against 50,000 florins of an unpayable dowry and Shetland in 1469 against 8,000. Redeemable in principle, never redeemed, annexed by Scotland in 1472.

  4. On 17 February 1500 a royal army of about twelve thousand was destroyed at Hemmingstedt by perhaps two thousand Ditmarschers who blocked a causeway and opened the sluices. Around four thousand died, the Holstein nobility lost a generation, and the royal Dannebrog was taken.

  5. Christian 2. reconquered Sweden in 1520 and, days after his coronation, executed something over eighty people in Stockholm on a heresy charge devised to defeat an amnesty. Gustav Vasa raised Dalarna, was elected king on 6 June 1523, and the union was finished; Christian himself was renounced by the Jutland nobility in January 1523 and sailed into exile in April.

Questions & discussion

FOUR KINDSWork the material

Recall

Did the facts land?

  • Who died in 1459, and why did his death create a problem?
  • What did Christian 1. promise at Ribe, and what did he receive?
  • On what terms did Orkney and Shetland leave, and in what years?
  • What was the ground at Hemmingstedt, and what did the Ditmarschers do with it?
  • What legal device got round the Stockholm amnesty, and who supplied it?
  • Who took the Danish throne in 1523, and where did he come from?

Causal

Why did that follow from this?

  • Why did the Schleswig-Holstein knighthood want a single lord rather than two?
  • Why did a medieval land-peace clause become a nineteenth-century national slogan — what changed in between, in the world rather than in the document?
  • Why did Hemmingstedt matter politically, given that Ditmarschen was small and remote?
  • Why did Christian 2. govern with burghers and Dutchmen rather than with his council, and why did that end as it did?
  • Why did the executions of November 1520 make a Swedish restoration impossible rather than more likely?

Counterfactual

What if it had gone otherwise?

  • If Adolf 8. had left a son, does Denmark ever acquire Holstein — and is there a Schleswig-Holstein question at all?
  • If Christian 1. had found 60,000 florins, what does the North Atlantic realm look like in 1900?
  • If Christian 2. had kept the terms of the Stockholm amnesty, could the union have survived?

Contested

Where the argument still is

  • What did ewich tosamende ungedelt mean in 1460? Undivided lordship, or a land-peace formula, as Carsten Jahnke argues. The nineteenth-century reading is the one with the least support and the most consequences.
  • Was the Stockholm bloodbath a planned decapitation of the Sture party or an escalation? The speed of the tribunal argues for planning; the number of burghers killed argues for something that ran away from its authors.
  • How many died at Stortorget? Fifty to sixty by the archbishop who laid the charge, eighty-two by the executioner, over a hundred and twenty by an eyewitness canon writing in the margin of a book. None of them is a register, and they are not all counting the same thing.
  • Christian 2.'s reputation has been rewritten at least twice, and the rewritings track Danish politics more closely than they track new evidence.
  • Was the Dannebrog legend formed around 1500? The dating of its first appearance is secure; the causal link to the lost banner is an inference, and a good one.

Sources

LIGHT SOURCINGWhat this is built on

  • WORKED FROM Charters, law codes, chronicles and the objects.
  • — the Ribe letter, 5 March 1460 The text survives and is short. It is a charter to a nobility, drafted by people who wanted their privileges written down — which is what it is mostly about, whatever the nineteenth century made of one clause.
  • — Hans Svaning (c. 1500–1584) The main narrative source for the Ditmarschen campaign and the lost banner. He wrote two generations later as the crown's historian, appointed in 1553 by Christian 3., whose father Frederik was among the commanders who lost the battle. Denmark had no formally appointed royal historiographer until Niels Krag in 1594.
  • — the Dithmarschen Landrecht, 1447 and c. 1467–80 A self-governing peasant community's own written law, printed at Lübeck in the 1480s. It exists because they had no lord to make law for them.
  • — Olaus Petri and the Swedish chronicle tradition The Swedish accounts of 1520 are near-contemporary and were written under and for Gustav Vasa's regime. That does not make them wrong; it makes the emphasis predictable.
  • — Christian 2.'s land law and town law, 1521–22 The best evidence for what he was trying to do, and the reason the nobility removed him. Repealed immediately after his fall.
  • — danmarkshistorien.dk Aarhus University. Ribebrevet, Christian 1., Kong Hans, Christian 2. and the Kalmar Union.
  • WHERE THE ARGUMENT STANDS Named as positions in a historiography, not as books this page has read.
  • — Carsten Jahnke, on the 1460 clause The modern case that ewich tosamende ungedelt is a land-peace formula rather than a promise of territorial indivisibility. The older reading has four hundred years of political weight behind it, which is a reason for caution in both directions.

Go and look

STILL THEREPlaces you can visit

  • Ribe The oldest town in Denmark, and where the 1460 agreement was made. The cathedral of chapter 12 is the building the parties would have walked past.
  • Dusenddüwelswarf, Hemmingstedt 'Thousand devils' mound', near Epenwöhrden in Dithmarschen, with a monument. Flat drained farmland — which is exactly the point, and worth standing in.
  • Storkyrkan, Stockholm Bernt Notke's Saint George and the Dragon, commissioned by Sten Sture after Brunkeberg. Stortorget, where the executions were carried out, is immediately outside.
  • Sønderborg Slot, Als Where Christian 2. spent the first seventeen years of his captivity, and where Abraham Brodersen was beheaded in chapter 17. Now a museum of the border region.
  • Gottorp Slot, Slesvig The ducal seat on the German side of the modern border, and the physical centre of everything the Ribe letter set up.
  • St Magnus Cathedral, Kirkwall, Orkney Norse architecture in a Scottish town: the clearest surviving evidence that these islands were once governed from Bergen and pledged from Copenhagen.
