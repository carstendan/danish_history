1848: constitution and the First Schleswig War

1848 – 1852 · Frederik 7. · Orla Lehmann · D.G. Monrad · Wilhelm Beseler · Mathilde Fibiger · A.D. Cohen

A king dies leaving no heir and a succession that Danish and German law answer differently. Eight weeks later absolutism has ended in Copenhagen without a shot, a provisional government has taken a fortress by railway timetable, and the two halves of the monarchy are at war. Denmark wins the war. The peace is written in London, and it is the trap that 1864 springs.

Introduction

WHAT THIS PAGE ANSWERSFive questions

  1. Christian 8. left three instructions and no grandson. Which of the three could not be carried out, and what did that do to the monarchy?
  2. Slesvig and Holstein were held by the same man under two different legal orders. What were they, and why did a childless king make the difference fatal?
  3. The meeting that ended Danish absolutism was called on a report of a rising that had not yet begun. Does that change what happened in March 1848?
  4. Before 1849 the army was recruited from one class. What changed, where exactly was the line drawn, and what could a man still buy his way out of?
  5. Denmark won every battle it fought alone and lost every point in the settlement. Who decided the peace, and what did they require in writing?

01 / NARRATIVEA king with no heir

Danish terms in this section

helstat
"whole state". The monarchy taken as one unit: the kingdom with the duchies of Slesvig, Holstein and Lauenburg, held by one man under different laws. A nineteenth-century word for a four-century-old arrangement, and it names the thing both national movements wanted to break.

On 9 January 1848 Christian 8. sat up in bed at Amalienborg and wrote out, in a hand already going, what he wanted his son to do. He asked for three things: that the monarchy be given a common constitution, that Frederik take counsel from patriotic men rather than from his friends, and that he contract a suitable marriage before the year was out. Eleven days later, on the evening of 20 January, the king died of blood poisoning following a bloodletting on his arm. He was sixty-one.

The third instruction was the one his son ignored. Frederik 7. was thirty-nine, had been married twice, had both marriages dissolved, and had no children; within two years he would marry Louise Rasmussen, a milliner and former dancer, and make her Countess Danner. The first instruction was carried out within the week, and it detonated.

The rescript of 28 January 1848 promised a constitutional order for the whole monarchy: a common estates assembly for kingdom and duchies together, to be prepared by a gathering of experienced men — eighteen from the kingdom, seven from Holstein, six from Slesvig, four from the knighthood, with a further eight named by the king from the kingdom and four from each duchy. The common representation itself was to have equal numbers from kingdom and duchies, sitting alternately in each.

Read that arithmetic slowly, because both national movements did. The kingdom held about 1.4 million people and the duchies about 900,000, and the offer gave them the same number of seats. To the Copenhagen liberals it meant a German veto over Danish affairs. To the Schleswig-Holsteiners it meant Slesvig being counted with Holstein in one breath and governed from Copenhagen in the next. A document intended to settle the question told both sides they were about to lose.

02 / NARRATIVEWhat Slesvig legally was

Danish terms in this section

agnatisk og kognatisk arvefølge
agnatic and cognatic succession. Agnatic: from man to man only. Cognatic: allowed to pass through a woman. The Kongelov was agnatic with a cognatic fallback if the male line failed; the duchies' law had no such fallback. The whole crisis is in that difference.

The Ribe letter of 1460 and what the nineteenth century made of it belong to chapter 19, and the argument about the clause is settled there, not here. What matters now is the machinery: three territories, two legal orders, and one man about to die without a son.

Holstein was a fief of the Holy Roman Empire, and after 1815 a member state of the German Confederation, along with Lauenburg. Its northern boundary was the Eider. Slesvig was not in the Confederation. It was a fief of the Danish crown, which produced the strangest arrangement in the monarchy: in Slesvig the king of Denmark was both the vassal who held the duchy and the lord from whom it was held.

Against that sat the succession. The Kongelov of 1665 is usually described as allowing the female line to inherit, and the description is right but too loose. The law was agnatic. It gave the crown to the male descendants of Frederik 3., and opened the female line only if the male line failed — a fallback nobody expected to need for a hundred and eighty years. It also laid down that whatever the king held or acquired should remain undivided under the king of Denmark.

The duchies ran on their own rules. Slesvig and Holstein had been cut and recut into partial duchies held by collateral branches of the house, each with its own succession, and the principle in all of them was agnatic and only agnatic: from man to man. Nothing in 1665 changed that, and 1721 did not settle it either. When Frederik 4. brought the Gottorp share of Slesvig under the crown and took homage for it, he did so without introducing the kingdom's law, the kingdom's administration, or Danish as the language of administration. The territory was incorporated; the question of whose succession law now ran there was left where it had been.

By the 1840s that unanswered question had a date on it. The heirs under the Kongelov, in order, were Christian 8.'s brother Prince Ferdinand, who had no legitimate children; then his sisters — Juliane Sophie, childless, and Louise Charlotte, dead in 1824, married to Landgrave Wilhelm of Hesse-Kassel — and then her children. Which is to say: after Frederik 7. the Danish crown passed through a woman. Under Holstein's law it could not. The same childless king was therefore about to send his kingdom to one man and at least part of his duchies to another, and the other was the duke of Augustenborg, whose father had been assembling the historical case since 1812 and whose claim became an integrated part of Schleswig-Holsteinism from 1844.

Christian 8. tried to close it with the Open Letter of July 1846, which declared the Kongelov's succession fully valid for Slesvig and admitted uncertainty for parts of Holstein. That is chapter 32's ending. It is worth saying plainly here what it did not do: it asserted the Danish reading, it did not prove it.

Where this account differs from the usual one. Most popular treatments give the dynastic law a paragraph and put the weight on the clash of nationalisms. This page gives it a section, because 1864 makes no sense without it. Great powers do not send armies over sentiment; they send them over broken instruments. And there is a further reason for caution that Danish accounts tend to skip. Working source-critically half a century later, the historian Kristian Erslev concluded, in 1901 and again in 1915, that the Kongelov's provisions had never been valid in Slesvig at all — and that the Augustenborg claim was accordingly justified. His view has not won general assent and the question remains open. But it means the legal case Denmark went to war for in 1848, and lost a duchy over in 1864, was one its own best archival scholar later thought was wrong.

03 / NARRATIVEMarch 1848

Danish terms in this section

Martsministeriet
the March ministry, 22 March to 11 November 1848. The first Danish government whose members were chosen for a political position rather than to administer the king's will.
provisorisk regering
the provisional government proclaimed at Kiel on 24 March 1848. It claimed to act for the king on the ground that he was no longer free, which is why officials who would not have joined a rebellion obeyed it.

On 18 March 1848 the German-minded members of the Slesvig and Holstein estates met together at Rendsburg and resolved to send the king an ultimatum: a free constitution for the duchies, Slesvig admitted to the German Confederation, and a Schleswig-Holstein army under its own officers. Granted in full, that would have left nothing in common with Denmark but the person of the king. A deputation was put on a steamer for Copenhagen.

The news reached Copenhagen on the 20th, and it arrived enlarged. A meeting of the national liberals had been called for the 22nd; it was pulled forward two days. That afternoon Copenhagen's Borgerrepræsentation debated an address to the king, drafted by Orla Lehmann, demanding that the old ministry be replaced by men who would give the country a free constitution and bind Slesvig to the kingdom. There was resistance at first. After Lehmann spoke there was not.

Vignette · Orla Lehmann, the Casino theatre, 20 March 1848

He is thirty-seven, a lawyer, the best speaker in the country and the man who six years earlier had put the phrase Danmark til Ejderen into general circulation. The hall is full. Somewhere south of him a deputation from Rendsburg is on the water, and the reports coming in from Kiel that day are alarming and unverified.

He tells the hall what he has heard. Their president has just given them the despatches from Kiel; he can add that in all probability the fortress of Rendsburg is already in enemy hands.

It is not. There is no rising yet. The provisional government will not be formed until the night of the 23rd, and Rendsburg will not be taken until the morning of the 24th — four days after this speech. Lehmann then answers the objection directly, and it is the most revealing passage in the evening: is this anything but rumour, ask the sensible people, and would it not be wiser to wait until one can see clearly?

He does not wait. Voices asking the meeting to hold off until the Rendsburg deputation actually arrives are swept aside. The resolutions pass in what everyone present afterwards described as a revolutionary mood.

Orla Lehmann, lawyer and national liberal · the Casino theatre, Copenhagen · the evening of 20 March 1848 · [-]

On the 21st the Borgerrepræsentation walked to Christiansborg with the address, and a crowd of twelve to fifteen thousand walked with it. They were met at the palace with the news that the king had already dismissed his ministers that morning. On the 22nd the new ministry came together — A.W. Moltke as premier, Bardenfleth at justice, Knuth at foreign affairs, Scheel-Plessen for the duchies, Bluhme at trade, and, as the mark of the change, Monrad at church and education and Tscherning at war. Frederik 7. told them he now regarded himself as a constitutional king. Absolutism in Denmark had lasted a hundred and eighty-eight years and ended in a week, without a barricade or a shot.

Then the thing Copenhagen had already reacted to actually happened. On the night of 23–24 March six men met in Kiel and constituted a provisional government for the duchies: the lawyers Wilhelm Beseler and Jürgen Bremer for the national liberals, Theodor Olshausen, Count Reventlow-Preetz for the nobility, Martin Thorsen Schmidt for the Kiel citizenry, and Prince Frederik of Nør, the Augustenborg duke's younger brother, as their military man. The proclamation Beseler read from the town hall steps at a quarter to six in the morning did not claim independence. It claimed that the will of the sovereign was no longer free and the country was without a government, and that until the king could decide freely the provisional government would carry the responsibility. The formula was Reventlow's, and it was why officials and town councils across the duchies accepted a revolutionary body as lawful: they were not rebelling, they were rescuing a prisoner.

An hour or so later a special train left Kiel for Rendsburg carrying the Kiel garrison and fifty armed citizens under the Prince of Nør. They rang the fire bell. The garrison came out of the fortress unarmed to deal with the fire, the officers were offered free passage, and almost every soldier joined the rising. Rendsburg was the largest garrison in the Danish composite state and it changed hands without a casualty, by railway timetable.

Two revolutions in one week, in opposite directions, and the second one was reported before it began.

Meanwhile · February 1848 — Paris.

Louis-Philippe abdicated on 24 February; within eight weeks there had been risings in Vienna, Berlin, Milan, Budapest and across the German states, and Denmark's March days fall inside the same eight weeks. What is unusual about the Danish case is not that absolutism fell but how. In Paris a king lost his throne; in Berlin and Vienna troops fired on crowds; in Copenhagen the king dismissed his ministers before the procession reached the palace and kept his crown for life. The country with the mildest 1848 is the one that got a war out of it.

04 / NARRATIVEThe war begins

Danish terms in this section

treårskrigen
the Three Years' War, 1848–50, also the First Schleswig War. Both names mislead: the fighting stopped each winter, and it was a civil war inside the monarchy before it was a war between states.

The provisional government held most of the duchies within days, but not all of them. In Nordslesvig the farming population stayed with the king, and so did the town of Haderslev; Sønderborg, Aabenraa and Flensborg split down the middle. That division — a duchy in which loyalty ran north to south rather than along any administrative line — is the fact the next sixteen years were fought over, and it was visible in the first fortnight.

Danish troops crossed into Slesvig on 29 March. On 9 April about eleven thousand men under Hedemann met a Schleswig-Holstein force of some six thousand under General Krohn at Bov, north of the modern border, and broke it; roughly a sixth of the enemy were taken prisoner and the rest went back to Rendsburg. Within two days the Danish army stood on the line of the Slien and the Danevirke. Denmark had apparently won its civil war in a month.

It had not, because the war stopped being a civil war. Prussia and the German Confederation came in on the insurgents' side, and on Easter Day, 23 April, General Wrangel attacked the Danish position outside the town of Slesvig. How large a force he brought is genuinely disputed: some accounts give thirty-two thousand men and seventy-four guns, while a reckoning from the units actually engaged puts the Danes against nearer seventeen thousand. About twelve thousand Danes held for a day and then went back to Als. Their losses in killed, wounded and captured were 816; the other side's, 461.

The following day the rear-guard was ambushed at Oversø, south of Flensborg, by Mecklenburg dragoons and Hanoverian infantry, and fifty men were taken. Then the Danes won again — at Nybøl on 28 May and at Dybbøl on 5 June — which delighted Denmark and alarmed everybody else. Russia had no intention of letting Prussia cross the Kongeå into the kingdom proper. Under British and Swedish mediation the fighting was suspended by the armistice of Malmö on 26 August 1848.

That armistice is where the war's real shape shows. It was ratified on 16 September by the Frankfurt National Assembly — a parliament whose first act of foreign policy had been to claim Schleswig-Holstein for Germany, and which now voted to accept terms it had no power to enforce. The provisional government was wound up on 22 October and replaced by a joint Danish-Prussian administration sitting at Gottorp. Nobody had conceded anything. Everybody had discovered that the question would be decided somewhere other than the battlefield.

05 / NARRATIVEThe soldier's war

Danish terms in this section

lægdsrulle
the muster roll: a parish-by-parish register of every male liable to conscription, with a lægd number and an individual number. Built as part of the apparatus of the stavnsbånd (chapter 29), and after 1849 the instrument through which every confirmed boy in the kingdom entered the state's books.
stillingsvæsen
substitution. The right to pay another man to serve in your place, which survived universal conscription and was abolished only in 1867.

Until 1848 the Danish army was recruited almost entirely from one class. Conscription fell on the peasantry: on farmers, cottagers and their sons, entered in the lægdsrulle parish by parish. Townsmen, burghers, officials and the nobility were exempt, either by birth or by having bought the exemption with a payment into the Landmilicefonden or the Søindrulleringsfonden, and they supplied the officers. The apparatus that did the counting was the one chapter 29 described: the muster roll built in the 1780s to keep track of men who were tied to the soil.

Vignette · Morten Jørgensen, dragoon, Stade, September 1848

He was born on 8 June 1825 at Aldal in Rold parish, in Rold Skov in Himmerland, the son of a husmand. The family lived off small plots, kitchen gardens and clogs: they cut beech billets and carved them, five pairs on an ordinary day and seven on a good one. The census of 1845 lists him at home with his parents as a clog-maker, one of seventeen in the three parishes around the forest. His mother had died when he was six, of an infection after a tooth was pulled badly.

He is called up in December 1847, before there is a war to fight, because conscription takes farmers and cottagers and he is of that estate. At the barracks at Randers he is given a horse, a sabre, a carbine, a red uniform, a helmet, and — by the cavalry's habit of naming men after the places they come from — the name Aldahl. His pay is fourteen skilling a day.

On 23 or 24 April 1848, in the confusion of the retreat from Slesvig, he is wounded and taken at Oversø. The Hanoverians march their prisoners to Stade, west of Hamburg, where seven hundred Danes are quartered in the barracks and decently treated. Morten Jørgensen dies there in early September, in his twenty-third year.

His death is entered in the muster roll, and underneath it someone has written, neatly, Viborg 53 X 222. That is where the replacement came from. Four or five weeks later the vacant horse and saddle were filled from the fifty-third lægd of Viborg amt, arrival roll X, number 222 — Jens Peter Sørensen, born 1825 at Højslev near Skive, who survived the war. Nothing else of Morten Jørgensen survives: not a letter, not a photograph, not a uniform button.

Morten Jørgensen Aldahl, dragoon, 5th Dragoon Regiment, 3rd squadron, no. 19 · Stade, kingdom of Hanover · September 1848 · [n]

The war changed who had to go. A temporary ordinance of 23 September 1848 pulled in men who had been exempt, for the duration; then, after three days of debate in the constituent assembly in December, the Law on Universal Conscription of 12 February 1849 made it permanent. The cut it made was not between town and country but between one birth year and the next. Every man born in 1826 or later lost whatever exemption he had, whether it came from his birth or had been bought with a payment into the funds; every man born before 1 January 1826 kept his. Every male was to be entered in the lægdsrulle as soon as he was confirmed, the parish priest reporting the confirmed boys. The obligation was written into the constitution four months later.

Two things follow, and both are worth saying because the phrase "universal conscription" carries neither of them. The first is that a man could still buy his way out by paying another to serve in his place, and that survived until 1867 — through this war and the next one. The second is that the institution which now enrolled every boy in Denmark was the same register, built for the stavnsbånd, that had once existed to stop men leaving.

Nobody in the state counted the dead. The list was made privately, by Asser Daniel Cohen, catechist to the Mosaic congregations of Funen and Lolland-Falster, a bookseller in Odense who had already published, at his own expense, the first history of the Jews in Denmark. His register of the fallen gives 533 for 1848, 1,381 for 1849 and 1,872 for 1850: 3,786 men, more than the 2,978 Denmark would lose in 1864. His son continued the work after that war. It is still the principal name register for both.

06 / NARRATIVE5 June 1849

Danish terms in this section

Grundloven
the constitution; here the Junigrundlov of 5 June 1849, Denmark's first.
Rigsdagen, Folketinget, Landstinget
the parliament and its two chambers. In 1849 both were elected on the same franchise; the privileged franchise that made the Landsting a conservative brake came in 1866 (chapter 36).
de syv F'er
"the seven F's", the popular name for the excluded: fruentimmere, folkehold, fattige, fremmede, fallenter, fjolser, forbrydere.

The constituent assembly was elected in October 1848, with a minority of members named by the king, and sat at Christiansborg through the winter arguing about who a citizen was. On 5 June 1849 Frederik 7. signed what it produced.

The Junigrundlov gave Denmark a two-chamber Rigsdag — a Folketing and a Landsting, elected in 1849 on the same qualifications — a government answerable to it, and a list of rights: freedom of speech, of assembly and of association, an end to arbitrary imprisonment, an end to censorship. Measured against 28 January 1848 it was an enormous distance travelled in seventeen months.

Then the qualifications. To vote a man had to be unblemished in reputation, hold Danish indfødsret, be thirty years old, have his own household, not be in another's service without one, and never have received poor relief that had not been repaid. The result was that 15 per cent of the whole population could vote, which is 72.8 per cent of all men over thirty. Popular speech named the excluded as the seven F's: fruentimmere, folkehold, fattige, fremmede, fallenter, fjolser, forbrydere — women, servants, paupers, foreigners, bankrupts, the certified insane and the convicted. Six of those seven categories would take between sixty-six and a hundred and sixty years to clear.

What the first election looked like is worth having as a number rather than as an impression. On 4 December 1849 a hundred seats were filled. In forty-three of them there was no ballot at all, the single candidate being carried by acclamation; in the fifty-seven that were contested, 37,903 men voted out of 116,553 entitled. A third of the electorate of a third of the men of a country turned out, in less than two-thirds of the constituencies, at the first free election in Danish history.

One more clause matters more than any of this, and almost nobody reads it. The constitution carried an opening reservation that everything concerning the position of the duchy of Slesvig would wait until peace was concluded. It never came into force south of the Kongeå.

Vignette · Mathilde Fibiger, Lolland, December 1850

She is nineteen when she takes the post and twenty when the book comes out. In October 1849 she goes to Lolland as governess to a forest ranger's family at Maltrupgård near Sakskøbing, which is one of the very few kinds of paid work an unmarried woman of her class can do without disgrace. She is a colonel's daughter. She has no vote, no capacity to hold property in her own right after marriage, and no prospect of either.

In the ranger's house she writes twelve letters from an invented woman called Clara Raphael to an invented friend, and sends the manuscript to Johan Ludvig Heiberg — the most powerful literary figure in Denmark, and the husband of the actress who is chapter 32's vignette. He places it with Reitzel, writes a long and carefully soothing preface, and it appears in December 1850 with 1851 on the title page, in eight hundred copies. Within a year it has drawn some twenty-five newspaper pieces and ten pamphlets. It is the first time a woman in Denmark has publicly demanded equality, and her phrase for it, Damernes Emancipation, enters the language.

Heiberg sends her the fee: 120 rigsdaler, about what a schoolmistress with her own small school earned in a year. She asks him to hold back twenty of it and give it to the Committee for the Dependants of the Fallen.

When her anonymity breaks she is cut by respectable society, the Heibergs close the door, and she loses the post. In 1866 she becomes the first woman employed by the Danish state, as a telegraphist.

Mathilde Fibiger, governess · Maltrupgård, near Sakskøbing, Lolland · December 1850 · [f]

Eighteen months after the constitution that excluded her, a twenty-year-old woman gave a sixth of her first fee to the widows of the war it had been written during.

07 / NARRATIVEA church for the people

Danish terms in this section

folkekirke
"people's church". The evangelical Lutheran church as established by article 3 of the 1849 constitution: supported by the state but, in name at least, not owned by it. The word was P. Chr. Kierkegaard's; the clause was Monrad's.
sognebåndsløsning
"loosing of the parish bond", 1855: the right to attach oneself to a priest other than one's own parish priest.

On 27 December 1842 the Danish state issued a placard directing that the children of Danish Baptists be baptised by force. A state church cannot do otherwise: it is the church a subject is born into, holding the doctrine the state has settled, and a parent who declines infant baptism is not exercising a preference but breaking the law. The Baptist cases were still running when the constituent assembly sat, and they did more than anything else to shape what came out of it.

Article 3 of the Junigrundlov reads that the evangelical Lutheran church is the Danish folkekirke and is as such supported by the state. Article 81 gave the right to worship according to conviction, and article 84 that a man's choice of faith affected neither his civil rights nor his civil duties. The word folkekirke had been coined by P. Chr. Kierkegaard in the 1840s — the elder brother of the more famous one — and it was Monrad who put it into the draft. Denmark is the first country in the world whose church calls itself the people's rather than the state's.

The change is easy to undersell, so here is its size. The Lutheran monopoly of chapter 20's settlement had run since 1536; it ended in 1849, and the Conventicle Edict of 1741 went with it. The Jews, who had had civil rights since 1814, now had full religious freedom — which is to say that Denmark's roll of its own war dead had been compiled, in 1848, by a man who was still legally a second-class believer while he compiled it.

What did not happen is equally characteristic. Article 80 promised that the constitution of the folkekirke would be settled by law. Three positions had been in the room: Mynster wanted the state church to survive under a new name, H.N. Clausen wanted a synod, Grundtvig wanted the church free of the state altogether, and the promise was the compromise that let all three sign. It was never kept. A commission appointed in 1853 reported eleven years later and nothing came of it, and the article is still there, undischarged, today. Grundtvig's real victory came elsewhere, in the sognebåndsløsning of 1855, which let a man choose a priest other than his parish's — the legal end of the arrangement chapter 32's farm servants had been fined for ignoring.

A constitution that excluded eighty-five per cent of the population from the vote gave every one of them the right to pray as they chose. Both statements are true of the same document, and a page that reports only the first is not describing 1849.

Meanwhile · September 1848 — Frankfurt.

The German National Assembly had claimed Schleswig-Holstein as its first act of foreign policy, and in September it was asked to ratify the armistice of Malmö, which handed the duchies back to a joint administration. It ratified, and in doing so admitted it had no army, no treasury and no obedience, and that Prussia would make war and peace in the north without consulting it. Riots followed and two deputies were murdered. The parliament that opened the year proposing to unify Germany by consent closed it having learned that Schleswig, like everything else, would go to whoever had the troops.

08 / NARRATIVEIsted, 25 July 1850

Danish terms in this section

ambulance
in this period a mobile field hospital, not a vehicle.

Denmark made peace with Prussia on 2 July 1850. The provisional government declined to be included in it and fought on, reinforced by German volunteers, which is how the largest battle ever fought on Danish ground came to be fought after the war it belonged to was formally over.

The armies met on Isted Hede, north of the town of Slesvig, on 25 July. The Danes had about thirty-seven thousand men under General Krogh; the Schleswig-Holstein army about twenty-six thousand eight hundred under the Prussian general von Willisen. Fighting began at two in the morning and did not stop until seven the following evening. It rained, the smoke did not clear, the ground was cut up with bog and lake, and the front was too long for anyone to see. Orders arrived late or not at all. A Danish brigade that was to cut the enemy's retreat was pulled back, and the Schleswig-Holstein army got away south over the Eider to Rendsburg intact.

Denmark won, and paid more for it than the loser did. The Danish dead numbered 845 and the Schleswig-Holstein dead 534; the wounded are given differently by different authorities depending on whether prisoners are counted with them, at 2,405 or 2,770 on the Danish side and 1,202 or 2,274 on the other. Whichever set is used, the army that held the field lost more men than the army that left it. Among the Danish dead were Colonel Frederik Læssøe and General Schleppegrell, hit leading his division at Øvre Stolk and dead the next morning in Flensborg.

It is possible to see the day from inside it, because a conscript assistant surgeon called Daniel Bølling wrote to his father two days later. Bølling was a Copenhagen medical student who had been in the Casino on the evening of 20 March 1848 and had volunteered on the spot; by 1850 he was in uniform not from enthusiasm but because the law of February 1849 had caught everyone born from 1826, and he was born in 1827. His ambulance spent the first day in a field south of an inn and the second in a barn north of Helligbæk, close enough to the fighting that enemy skirmishers came within cannon-shot. Several hundred wounded lay waiting to be dressed. He admits, with some care, that the cannonade frightened him until the first casualty arrived and there was work to do. For two days the staff lived on a cup of coffee each morning and a little wine and water shared with the wounded.

Three days after the battle, four hundred and ninety-five Danes were buried together in one grave in the old cemetery at Flensborg.

Isted decided nothing. A Schleswig-Holstein attack at Missunde in September failed, a siege of Frederiksstad in October failed, and the rising was finally shut down not by the Danish army but by Russia and Austria leaning on Prussia. It was the last time a Danish army would face a roughly equal opponent, and the largest battle fought on Nordic soil until the Winter War.

09 / NARRATIVELondon, 8 May 1852

Danish terms in this section

Londontraktaten
the Treaty of London of 8 May 1852, guaranteeing the monarchy's integrity and settling the succession on the Glücksburg line. Not the London Protocol of 2 August 1850, which is a separate and earlier instrument, and not the notes of 1851–52, which are where the binding promise about Slesvig actually sits.

Eight days after Isted, on 2 August 1850, Prussia and Austria signed a protocol in London accepting that the integrity of the Danish monarchy was a European necessity. They signed it because Russia required them to. That is the shape of the whole settlement: the powers took the question out of Danish and German hands and answered it themselves, in Denmark's favour on the surface and against Denmark underneath.

The instrument that mattered came two years later. To get Prussian and Austrian signatures on it, Denmark had to give an undertaking at the turn of 1851–52, in notes exchanged separately, that the duchy of Slesvig would not be bound more closely to the kingdom than Holstein was. That promise is not in the treaty. It was made beside it, and the Danish public was not told how far its government had committed itself; the royal proclamation of 28 January 1852 announced a monarchy divided into three parts and said nothing about what had been signed abroad.

The Treaty of London of 8 May 1852, between Denmark and Britain, France, Austria, Prussia, Russia and Sweden-Norway, then settled the succession. Failing male descendants in the direct line from Frederik 3., the crown of all the king's lands was to pass to Prince Christian of Schleswig-Holstein-Sonderburg-Glücksburg and to the issue of his marriage with Princess Louise, born a princess of Hesse, by primogeniture and from man to man. It was done, the treaty says, with the consent of the Hereditary Prince and of the king's nearest cognates called to the succession by the Danish Kongelov — that is, the people who actually had the claim signed it away — and its stated object was a combination which, excluding women, called the male line to inherit in all the lands united under the Danish sceptre.

Look at what that means. The dynastic quarrel had been between a Danish law that allowed a woman to transmit a crown and a German law that did not. Europe resolved it by adopting the German principle and then finding a man whose wife held the Danish claim. Christian 9. would become king of Denmark because Louise renounced in his favour. The Danish position had been conceded in the act of being secured.

The Rigsdag duly passed a new succession law in 1853. And under Russian pressure it was settled that the June constitution would continue to apply in the kingdom only; the duchies were to keep the form of government they had, which was absolutism. A country that had abolished absolute monarchy in 1848 was now required by treaty to maintain it in two of its own provinces.

10 / NARRATIVEWhat the constitution could not cover

Danish terms in this section

Istedløven
the Isted Lion, Bissen's monument of 1862 on the old cemetery in Flensborg. Danish memorial, Prussian trophy, Copenhagen exhibit and, since 2011, a monument to reconciliation on its original site: four meanings in a hundred and fifty years, none of them the one it was made for.

Denmark ended the war holding the field, holding the duchies, and holding a guarantee from five great powers. It had a free constitution for the kingdom, an absolutism it was obliged to preserve in the duchies, a promise it had made in private not to bring Slesvig closer, and a designated heir chosen by foreigners. Every one of those would still be true in 1863, and three of them are why 1864 happened.

The monument tells the rest. In July 1858 Orla Lehmann — the man in the Casino ten years earlier — wrote to the sculptor H.W. Bissen proposing a lion for Isted. A national subscription raised twenty-five thousand rigsdaler, Bissen modelled the beast in 1859 and 1860, and on 25 July 1862, the twelfth anniversary of the battle, it was unveiled at the old cemetery in Flensborg before five thousand people and four ministers, with H.N. Clausen giving the address. Isted, 25 July 1850. The Danish people raised this memorial. Frederik 7. was advised not to involve himself, gave nothing, and did not come.

It was set on ground where a Schleswig-Holstein war grave had been, and that grave was levelled to make room. Two years later the Prussians took the lion to Berlin as booty and stood it outside their cadet school. It came back to Denmark in 1945 and back to Flensburg in 2011.

The cemetery it stands in holds about two thousand Danish soldiers — the dead of Bov in 1848 and Isted in 1850, and then the dead of Sankelmark and Dybbøl in 1864, in the same ground, in a town that would be on the other side of a border before the men from the first war had been dead fifteen years.

Myth-check

"Denmark won the Three Years' War."
Denmark won the war and lost the peace, and the second half is the half that matters, because the terms of the peace are the direct cause of 1864.

Summary

IF YOU REMEMBER FIVE THINGSThe page in five

  1. Christian 8. died in January 1848 leaving a childless son and a succession that Danish and German law answered differently, and the rescript that followed offered the duchies equal representation with a kingdom half again their size. It satisfied nobody, which is what a compromise does when the thing being divided is a nation.

  2. In March the national liberals brought their meeting forward two days on a false report that Rendsburg had fallen, and Frederik 7. gave up absolute power before the procession reached his palace. Four days later the rising the meeting had been called about actually began, and a provisional government took Rendsburg by special train without firing a shot.

  3. Denmark beat the Schleswig-Holsteiners at Bov, Nybøl, Dybbøl, Fredericia and Isted, and never beat the Prussians, who were stopped by Russia instead. Until February 1849 the war was fought by peasants alone; the law of that month took every man born from 1826, but left him able to pay another to go in his place until 1867.

  4. The constitution of 5 June 1849 gave the vote to fifteen per cent of the population and freedom of belief to all of it, ended the Lutheran monopoly of 1536, and reserved the position of Slesvig until peace was concluded. It never came into force south of the Kongeå.

  5. The peace, made in London in 1852, guaranteed the monarchy's integrity, settled the crown on a prince who held his claim through his wife, required that absolutism be preserved in the duchies, and bound Denmark in unpublished notes never to draw Slesvig closer than Holstein. That is the promise Denmark broke in 1863, and it is why a war Denmark won produced the war Denmark lost.

Questions & discussion

FOUR KINDSWork the material

Recall

Did the facts land?

  • In what order did these happen: the Casino meeting, the fall of the Danish ministry, the formation of the provisional government at Kiel, and the seizure of Rendsburg fortress?
  • Where did the conscription law of 12 February 1849 draw its line, and what could a man still do to avoid serving?
  • What were the qualifications for the vote under the 1849 constitution, and what proportion of the population met them?

Causal

Why did it happen that way?

  • The rescript of 28 January 1848 was a genuine constitutional concession, and it made both national movements angrier. Why?
  • The provisional government declared that the king's will was no longer free rather than declaring independence. What did that formula buy it, and from whom?
  • Denmark won every engagement it fought alone and got a settlement it had not won. What made the difference, and where was it decided?

Counterfactual

What if it had gone otherwise?

  • Suppose the reports reaching Copenhagen on 20 March 1848 had been accurate, and the meeting had been told nothing had yet happened at Rendsburg. Does absolutism still end that week?
  • Suppose the Kongelov of 1665 had been plainly agnatic, with no female fallback at all. Does the Slesvig question still arrive in 1848, and in what form?

Contested

Where do historians disagree?

  • Did Orla Lehmann create the mood at the Casino by announcing a rising that had not begun, or did he only use a mood already there? Hans Vammen argued the first, Bjørn Svensson the second. What evidence could settle it, and does the answer change what one thinks of March 1848?
  • Kristian Erslev concluded that the Kongelov's succession had never been valid in Slesvig and that the Augustenborg claim was therefore good in law. His view has not been generally accepted. If he was right, what follows about 1848 and 1864 — and should a national history say so? ---
