Bronze Age: amber, sun and the long road south

c. 1,700 – 500 BCE · bronzealderen · Montelius periods I–VI

A country with no copper and no tin becomes one of the richest bronze-using regions in Europe — by selling fossil tree resin to the Mediterranean. This is the first time Denmark gets wealthy from being in the way of other people's trade, and it will not be the last.

Introduction

WHAT THIS PAGE ANSWERSFive questions

  1. How does a country with no metal ore end up drowning in bronze?
  2. What was actually being traded, how far, and who carried it?
  3. Why do we know what these people wore and how they cut their hair, when we barely know what Neolithic Danes looked like at all?
  4. What did they believe, and how can anyone claim to know?
  5. Were they local? — a question that is currently the subject of a genuine and unresolved scientific feud.

01 / NARRATIVEA country with no ore

Danish terms in this section

bronzealderen
The Bronze Age, 1,700–500 BCE; divided into ældre (1,700–1,100) and yngre (1,100–500).
bronze
An alloy, roughly nine parts copper to one part tin — neither of which occurs in Denmark.
depotfund
Hoard: a group of objects deliberately deposited together, usually in a bog.
rav
Amber — fossilised resin of a Baltic conifer, washed up on the west Jutland coast.

Start with the central fact, because everything else in this chapter follows from it. There is no copper in Denmark. There is no tin. There never was. Every single gram of bronze in the Danish Bronze Age was carried here from somewhere else.

And there is a great deal of it. The Nordic Bronze Age is, by volume of metal deposited, one of the richest in Europe — swords, axes, spearheads, neck-rings, belt plates, razors, tweezers, horns, the lot. A region on the far northern edge of the metal-using world, with no ore at all, somehow accumulated more bronze per head than many places that were sitting on mines.

This is the shape of Danish economic history in miniature, and it recurs so often in this series that it is worth naming now. Denmark has almost no raw materials. What it has is position, one or two things nobody else has much of, and the willingness to trade. In this chapter the commodity is amber. Later it will be the Sound, then grain, then butter and bacon, then shipping. The pattern is already fully formed by 1,500 BCE.

02 / NARRATIVEAmber: what Denmark had to sell

Amber is the fossilised resin of conifers that grew around the Baltic some forty million years ago. It erodes out of submarine deposits and washes up after westerly storms, above all on the west coast of Jutland. It is warm to the touch, it floats in salt water, it holds a static charge when rubbed, it sometimes contains a perfectly preserved insect, and it burns with a sweet smell. To a Bronze Age society it was not a bauble. It was uncanny.

Chapter 3 saw it carved into small animals for local use. Now it goes south — and it goes a very long way. Baltic amber turns up in the shaft graves at Mycenae, in Egypt, and in the cargo of the Uluburun ship that sank off the Turkish coast around 1,300 BCE with copper ingots, tin, ivory, glass and, among all that, beads of northern amber.

Coming the other way: copper from the Alps, from the Carpathians and Slovakia, later from further afield; tin from Cornwall and from the Erzgebirge. The metal arrived as ingots and as finished objects, and Danish smiths then recast it into forms that are unmistakably local. The Nordic Bronze Age has its own style — spirals, above all, endlessly and confidently — and objects made here are identifiable anywhere.

What Denmark was buying with amber, in other words, was not just metal but membership. For twelve hundred years this small northern region was plugged into a continental system that reached the Mediterranean, and its art, its religion and its social structure show it.

Meanwhile in the world

The Egtved girl was buried around 1,370 BCE. Tutankhamun died around 1,323 BCE. They are near contemporaries, and the objects in her oak coffin were made while the New Kingdom was at its height and Mycenaean Greece was building its citadels.

Around 1,200 BCE that whole eastern Mediterranean world came apart in the Late Bronze Age collapse — palaces burnt, Mycenae abandoned, trade networks broken, writing lost in Greece for four centuries. Denmark, at the far end of the supply chain, does not collapse. The Late Bronze Age here is if anything richer than the Early. But the pattern of contacts shifts southwards and eastwards towards central Europe, and the Mediterranean connection thins.

Meanwhile in Europe

The closest parallel to the Trundholm sun chariot is not Danish at all. The Nebra sky disc, found in Saxony-Anhalt and dated to around 1,600 BCE, is a bronze disc inlaid with gold showing sun, moon and a cluster of stars usually read as the Pleiades. Two societies four hundred kilometres and two centuries apart, both making gold-on-bronze models of the sky.

Stonehenge was complete and already old. In central Europe the Únětice culture and its successors controlled the copper routes; in Britain the Wessex culture buried its dead with gold and — as it happens — with Baltic amber. The Bronze Age is the first period in this series in which Denmark is demonstrably part of a single European conversation.

03 / NARRATIVEThe mound landscape

Danish terms in this section

gravhøj
Burial mound or barrow — the defining monument of the Early Bronze Age.
tørv
Turf: cut sods of grassland, the material the mounds were built from.
al
Iron pan — a hard, iron-rich crust that forms in sandy soil and can seal a mound watertight.

If the Neolithic filled Denmark with stone, the Early Bronze Age filled it with earth. Round mounds — gravhøje — went up in enormous numbers between about 1,500 and 1,100 BCE. Something like twenty thousand still stand as visible monuments; the original number was very much larger, with estimates commonly running to fifty thousand or more.

They are sited to be seen. Along ridges, on skylines, beside the routes people actually moved along — which is why the Bronze Age is still legible from a car window in a way that no other prehistoric period is. Drive the ridge road in Jutland and the bumps on the horizon are, mostly, this chapter.

They were built of turf, stripped from the surrounding grassland. A large mound consumed the sod of many hectares, which was a serious agricultural sacrifice — and in the sandier districts the stripped ground did not recover. Some of the earliest heathland in west Jutland was created by building monuments on top of the pasture.

That turf then did something the builders could not have intended. Water percolating through the mound leached iron downwards, where it precipitated into a hard crust — al, iron pan — that sealed the mound's core. Inside the seal, oxygen was excluded, the contents stayed permanently waterlogged, and organic material did not rot.

04 / NARRATIVEThe oak coffins

Danish terms in this section

egekistegrav
Oak-coffin grave: a body laid in a hollowed oak trunk, sealed inside a mound.
snoreskørt
The corded string skirt worn by the Egtved girl — woven at the waist, hanging in free cords.
bælteplade
Belt disc: a large decorated bronze plate worn at the waist by women.

The result is the single most extraordinary body of evidence in Danish prehistory. In a few dozen mounds, the dead were laid in coffins hollowed from split oak trunks — and in those where the iron pan sealed properly, the wood, the woollen clothing, the leather, the hair, the skin, the contents of a birch-bark bucket and the flowers laid beside the body all survived for three and a half thousand years.

Borum Eshøj was opened in 1875, Muldbjerg in 1883, Trindhøj, Skrydstrup in 1935. But the one everybody knows was found in February 1921.

The farmer who stopped digging

On 24 February 1921 a farmer named Peter Platz was levelling a burial mound on his land at Egtved, west of Vejle. His plough or spade struck something hard, and it turned out to be a coffin of oak.

What he did next is the reason we have anything. He stopped, left it in the ground, and wrote to the National Museum. The museum came, lifted the coffin whole, and took it to Copenhagen unopened — so that it could be excavated indoors, slowly, with the contents recorded in place rather than falling apart on a windy Jutland field.

Inside was a girl of sixteen to eighteen. Her hair was blonde and cropped short. She wore a short woollen tunic with elbow-length sleeves and, below it, a knee-length skirt made of twisted woollen cords hanging free from a woven waistband — an object so unexpected that it was widely disbelieved when first published, and has since been found on Bronze Age figurines across the region. At her waist was a large decorated bronze belt disc. She had a bronze awl, an earring, a comb of horn at her belt.

By her feet were the cremated bones of a child of five or six. Beside her lay a birch-bark bucket that had held a fermented drink of wheat, honey, bog myrtle and cowberry — beer, roughly, or mead. And laid on the coffin was a sprig of yarrow, in flower.

Yarrow flowers in summer. So the burial was in summer, of a specific year that dendrochronology later fixed at 1,370 BCE. A single wildflower dates the funeral to a season, three and a half thousand years on.

Peter Platz, farmer · Egtved, 24 February 1921

Egtved is the most famous, but it is not alone, and the others fill out the picture. Borum Eshøj near Aarhus — one of the largest mounds in the country — was dug in the 1870s and produced three oak coffins: a man of perhaps fifty or sixty with his hair and beard preserved, a woman of similar age with an elaborate hairnet, and a young man in his twenties. A family group, or presented as one. The Muldbjerg mound in west Jutland, opened in 1883, held a man buried with a sword, a woollen cloak and a round cap of astonishing workmanship.

And the Skrydstrup woman, found in 1935, had been laid out with her hair dressed in an elaborate construction built up over a pad and held with a fine hairnet — a style that would have taken another person a long time to build and could not have survived a working day. Whatever she was, she was arranged.

What these graves give us is texture that no amount of pottery typology can. We know that Bronze Age Danes wore wool, and therefore that sheep-keeping had become central. We know how the cloth was woven and how the garments were cut. We know that women wore their hair long and netted, or short; that men were clean-shaven and carried folding stools, razors and tweezers; that both sexes wore a great deal of bronze. We know they were buried with food and drink, and with flowers.

We also know they were not, by our standards, old. The Egtved girl was perhaps eighteen. The Skrydstrup woman seventeen or eighteen. These were the honoured dead of a wealthy society, and they died young.

05 / NARRATIVEThe sun

Danish terms in this section

Solvognen
The Trundholm sun chariot — a gilded bronze disc drawn by a horse, on wheels.
helleristning
Rock carving; in Denmark mostly on Bornholm, far commoner in Sweden and Norway.
ragekniv
Razor — a small bronze blade, often engraved with ships, horses and sun images.

A thing made, and then killed

Someone modelled it in wax first: a horse, small and long-necked, and a disc, and six wheels. The wax was packed in clay, the clay fired until the wax ran out, and molten bronze poured into the space where the wax had been. One face of the disc was then covered in gold foil and worked over with spirals — the same spirals that run across every confident Danish object of the period. It is skilled, expensive, unhurried work.

Then somebody broke it, and carried it out onto a wet place in north-west Zealand, and laid it on the surface, and left it. It was not buried. The bog simply grew over it. The breaking was deliberate: the offering was of a thing killed, not a thing stored.

It stayed there for roughly three thousand three hundred years. Trundholm Mose was drained in the nineteenth century, and in September 1902 a smallholder named Frederik Willumsen took a heavy meadow plough across it for the first time in the bog's existence. The horse came up on the first pass. He turned at the end of the field, came back, and the gilded disc came up on the second.

The find is a horse drawing the sun, mounted on six wheels so that it can be pulled. The gilded face has a rayed rim; the plain face does not. Pull it one way and the golden sun travels right, across the day sky. Pull it back and the dark sun travels left, through the underworld. It is a model of how the universe works, and you operate it by hand.

In 1996 an amateur with a metal detector found another piece of a wheel. A systematic survey in 1998 recovered more. Ninety-six years after the plough, the object was still not complete.

Frederik Willumsen · Trundholm Mose, September 1902

The sun chariot is not an isolated curiosity. It is the clearest surviving statement of a religion that can be read, in fragments, all over Nordic Bronze Age art — on razors, on rock carvings, on the great bronze horns.

The reconstruction generally credited to Flemming Kaul of the National Museum goes roughly like this. The sun crosses the day sky drawn by a horse, from left to right. In the evening it is received by a ship and carried down; at night it travels back through the underworld from right to left, helped by a fish or a snake; in the morning it is lifted out again. The evidence is the consistent direction of travel in hundreds of engraved images, and the consistent pairing of sun, horse, ship, fish and snake. It is an interpretation, not a text, and it is argued about. But it is a serious attempt to read a religion off a corpus of pictures, and it explains why ships are the commonest image in northern Bronze Age art in a region where most of the pictures are nowhere near the sea.

06 / NARRATIVESound and spectacle

Among the strangest survivals of the period are the lurer — long curved bronze horns, up to about two metres, cast in sections in the lost-wax method, with a mouthpiece and a decorated disc at the bell. Around three dozen are known from Denmark, more from the rest of Scandinavia. They are almost always found in pairs, mirror images of one another, deposited in bogs. Many still play, and their range is roughly that of a natural trumpet.

Two horns, tuned, played together, by two people standing in the open. Add the sun chariot, the gold, the mounds on the skyline, the bronze glinting on the person of anybody important, and the picture is of a society that took public ceremony extremely seriously — and had the surplus to pay for it.

Rock carvings, so abundant in Sweden and Norway, are sparse in Denmark for the simple reason that Denmark has almost no exposed rock. Bornholm, which does, has them. But the same imagery — ships above all, with their crews shown as vertical strokes — appears engraved on bronze razors right across the country, and on the great slabs of the Kivik grave in Skåne.

07 / NARRATIVESwords, chiefs and travel

Danish terms in this section

fuldgrebssværd
Full-hilted sword — hilt and blade cast as a decorated whole, the high-status form.
høvding
Chieftain — the word normally used, though what the office actually was is unknown.
foldestol
Folding stool: a portable seat, and one of the clearest status objects of the period.

Thousands of bronze swords are known from Denmark. They are the defining male object of the period as the belt disc is the female one, and they raise the obvious question: were they weapons or were they display?

The answer, from wear analysis, is both. Microscopic study of edges has found notches, chips and resharpening patterns consistent with blade meeting blade — not on every sword, and not on the finest, but on enough of them that the fighting was real. Bronze is soft; a bronze sword that has been used shows it. Meanwhile the most elaborate fuldgrebssværd, cast in one piece with spiral ornament running over the hilt, look like objects meant to be seen at a distance by people who were not going to be allowed to touch them.

Which points at the shape of the society. Nobody knows what political units existed here — there are no texts, no names, no boundaries. What the material shows is marked and hereditary inequality: a small number of people buried with gold, imported goods and metalwork of a quality that implies specialist smiths working to order, and a large number buried with very little or not visibly buried at all. The usual term is chiefdom, and the usual model is a landscape of competing local centres, each controlling a stretch of coast and a share of the flow of metal, with Voldtofte the clearest Danish example.

One object gets at it better than the swords. From an oak-coffin grave came a folding stool of ash with a seat of otter skin — a portable chair, a thing you bring so that you can sit while others stand. Folding stools of very similar design were being used in Egypt and the Mycenaean world at the same date. Whether the idea travelled or was invented twice, somebody in Bronze Age Jutland was buried with the north European version of a throne.

How much did people move? The Copenhagen school associated with Kristian Kristiansen has argued for a genuinely mobile Bronze Age — warrior elites, itinerant smiths, marriage alliances across hundreds of kilometres, and long-distance travel as a normal part of an aristocratic life. Much of the isotope evidence adduced for that picture is the same evidence now under dispute in section 09, which is one reason the strontium argument matters beyond two famous women. But the objects, at least, are not in doubt. Amber left, metal arrived, and styles moved with them.

08 / NARRATIVEThe farm behind it

Danish terms in this section

treskibet langhus
Three-aisled longhouse: two rows of internal posts, with people at one end and cattle at the other.
uld
Wool — the new textile, and the reason sheep numbers rise sharply.

All of this rests on farms, and the farms improve through the period.

The great innovation is the treskibede langhus, the three-aisled longhouse, which appears in the later Bronze Age and then dominates Danish rural building for the next two thousand years. Two rows of internal posts carry the roof, freeing the walls from load-bearing and allowing much longer buildings. One end housed people, the other end cattle — which is warmer for everyone and keeps the manure where you want it. Excavations at Bjerre in Thy have recovered whole farm layouts with their fields.

Wool is the other change. Sheep had been present since chapter 3, but it is in the Bronze Age that woollen cloth becomes the standard clothing material, and the quantities implied are substantial — the Egtved girl's outfit alone represents a great deal of spinning. Textile production on this scale is a household industry, and it is overwhelmingly likely to have been women's work, which makes it one of the larger economic activities of the period and one of the least visible.

Cattle, barley, wheat, some flax; oxen for traction; horses, which arrive properly in this period and carry enormous prestige — as the sun chariot makes clear. And, increasingly through the period, land under permanent management rather than shifting cultivation.

09 / NARRATIVEFire, urns and hoards

Around 1,100 BCE the burial rite changes. Bodies are burnt.

Cremation replaces inhumation almost completely, and the ashes go into pottery urns, often inserted into the flank of an existing mound rather than under a new one. Some urns are given a miniature version of the full grave goods — a tiny bronze sword bent to fit. The great mound-building stops.

This is a loss for archaeology and it matters for how the period reads. From 1,100 BCE we stop having bodies, clothing and hair, and we go back to having metalwork. Fortunately there is a lot of it, because the other change in the Late Bronze Age is a marked increase in depotfund — hoards placed in bogs, lakes and streams.

That practice, which chapter 3 watched begin with polished flint axes, now reaches its peak. Almost everything we have from the period comes from deposits rather than from settlements, because bronze in circulation gets recycled: a broken sword is raw material. Only the metal that was deliberately taken out of circulation and given to the water survives. Our picture of the Danish Bronze Age is therefore a picture of what people thought worth sacrificing, which is not at all the same as a picture of what they owned.

Certain places concentrate wealth to a degree that suggests something like a chiefly centre. Voldtofte on south-west Funen is the standard example: a settlement area with imported goods, gold, and enormous quantities of material, in a district that looks like it was running something.

10 / NARRATIVEWere they local? A scientific feud

Danish terms in this section

strontium
An element taken up through food and water; its isotope ratio varies by geology and is fixed in teeth as they form.
baseline
The reference map of local strontium values against which a person's signature is compared.
jordbrugskalk
Agricultural lime — spread on Danish fields for a century, and itself containing strontium.

In 2015 a team led by Karin Frei of the National Museum published a study of the Egtved girl's teeth, hair, nails and wool. Strontium is absorbed through food and drink and locked into tooth enamel as it forms in childhood; hair records the last months of life, a centimetre or so per month. Comparing her signature against a published map of Danish strontium values, they concluded that she had not grown up at Egtved at all — most likely in the Black Forest — and that in her final months she had travelled a long way and back again. A 2017 study reached a similar conclusion for the Skrydstrup woman.

It was a spectacular result, it made international news, and it fitted a broader picture of a mobile, interconnected Bronze Age Europe that archaeologists were building at the time.

In March 2019 two geologists from Aarhus University, Erik Thomsen and Rasmus Andreasen, published in Science Advances an argument that the whole thing rested on a broken measuring stick. Danish farmers have been spreading lime on their fields for about a century. Lime contains strontium. Their measurements showed that streams and ponds in farmed areas have low isotope signatures and high strontium concentrations, while water in woods and unfarmed ground has high signatures and low concentrations — and only around five or six per cent of Denmark is now unaffected by agriculture or urbanisation. Sample the unfarmed places near Egtved, they said, and the local values match the girl perfectly well. She could have grown up within ten kilometres of her own burial mound.

The National Museum responded the same day, maintaining its position. Robert Frei and colleagues at Copenhagen published a counter-study arguing that lime-derived strontium is retained in the upper soil and does not reach surface water. Thomsen and Andreasen replied with measurements from a GEUS-run test field at Voulund, where the exact quantity and date of liming was known, showing strontium demonstrably leaving the topsoil. Frei replied to that. They replied to the reply.

The exchange has now run for a decade across Science Advances, Science of the Total Environment, Frontiers in Ecology and Evolution, the Danish popular science press and the national newspapers, and it is not resolved. Both sides are competent, both are in good journals, and both are quite plainly annoyed.

The honest position for this page is that we do not know where the Egtved girl grew up. What the dispute has established beyond doubt is something more interesting than the answer: that a whole generation of provenance studies across Europe depends on baseline maps whose reliability is itself a live research question. If Thomsen and Andreasen are right, the problem is not confined to Denmark.

11 / NARRATIVEThe end

Around 500 BCE the Bronze Age stops, and the reasons are external.

The climate deteriorates — cooler and wetter across northern Europe, which shortens the growing season and pushes settlement about. The trading system that had brought metal north for twelve hundred years reorganises: the Mediterranean world had already been through its own collapse, and in central Europe the Hallstatt and then La Tène worlds are working iron.

And iron changes the terms of trade completely. Copper and tin are rare, and they made Denmark dependent. Iron is not rare. It can be got out of Danish bogs, in the form of the iron-rich nodules chapter 5 will call myremalm, by anybody with fuel and patience. A society whose elite had defined itself by controlling access to imported metal now faced a world where the useful metal was under the meadow.

The bronze does not disappear; it becomes ceremonial and then rare. The mounds stay on the skyline. But the long southern connection thins, the deposits change character, and the country turns inward for a while.

It is worth marking what ends here, because it is more than a metal.

For twelve hundred years a small northern society had been plugged into a system that reached the Mediterranean, and had organised itself around that connection: an aristocracy whose position rested on access to imported bronze, a religion whose central image was carried on objects made from it, and a landscape of mounds built to be seen from the routes the trade moved along. When the metal stopped arriving in quantity, all of that lost its footing at once.

What survived was the ground. The mounds stayed where they were, and by the Iron Age they were already ancient — unexplained bumps on the skyline, occasionally dug into for later burials by people who had no idea who was inside. The Danes of chapter 5 walked past them without knowing. So did the Vikings of chapter 10, who sometimes buried their own dead in them. So, most likely, do you.

Twenty thousand of them are still standing. They are the largest surviving structure of the period and the only part of this chapter that was never lost, never rediscovered and never reinterpreted. Somebody built each one, out of turf stripped from pasture that took a generation to recover, so that a particular person would be visible from the road. Three thousand years later they still are.

Myth-check

"Vikings wore horned helmets."
They did not — but somebody here did. The two horned bronze helmets found in a bog at Veksø on Zealand are Bronze Age, around 900 BCE, roughly seventeen centuries before the Viking Age, and almost certainly ceremonial rather than military. Nineteenth-century artists and opera designers transferred them to Vikings, and there they have stayed.
"The lur is a Viking instrument."
Also Bronze Age, and by a similar margin. The horn on Danish butter packaging is about two thousand years older than the longships it is meant to evoke.
"Bronze Age Denmark was a poor periphery."
It was a periphery, and it was rich. More bronze was deposited here than in many regions with their own mines. Being on the edge of a system is not the same as being outside it.
"The Egtved girl came from southern Germany."
Widely reported as established fact in 2015 and still on many museum labels. It is contested, the counter-argument is serious, and the dispute is ongoing. "We don't know" is the accurate answer.
"The burial mounds are Viking graves."
The commonest thing said about the bumps on the Danish skyline, and wrong by two thousand years. Most are Bronze Age. Vikings did occasionally bury people in them, which does not help.

Summary

IF YOU REMEMBER FIVE THINGSThe page in five

  1. Denmark has no copper and no tin, yet the Nordic Bronze Age is among the richest in Europe. The bill was paid in amber, traded south in relays as far as Mycenae and Egypt.

  2. Tens of thousands of burial mounds went up between about 1,500 and 1,100 BCE, sited to be seen from a distance. Around twenty thousand still stand.

  3. In a few of them, iron pan sealed the mound and preserved oak coffins with clothing, hair and flowers intact — Egtved, Skrydstrup, Borum Eshøj, Muldbjerg. Nothing else in Danish prehistory comes close.

  4. The Trundholm sun chariot models a religion in which a horse draws the sun across the day sky and a ship carries it back through the night — an interpretation built from hundreds of engraved images.

  5. Whether the Egtved girl was local or a foreigner is genuinely unresolved, and the argument about it has called into question strontium provenance studies far beyond Denmark.

Contested

Where researchers actually disagree.

  • The strontium dispute has run for a decade between the National Museum and Aarhus University. Read the shape of it: what would it take to settle, and why has publishing more papers not settled it?
  • Kaul's reading of the sun's journey is built from the direction of travel in engraved images. How much confidence should a reconstruction of a religion carry when there is no text?
  • A museum that has staked a famous exhibit on a contested interpretation has an obvious interest in that interpretation. How should institutions handle it when their headline object becomes the subject of a live scientific dispute?
  • Was the Egtved girl's burial an honour or something less comfortable? She was eighteen; a cremated child lay at her feet. What readings are available, and which does the evidence actually support?

Go and look

STILL THEREPlaces you can visit

  • Nationalmuseet The sun chariot, the Egtved girl and her coffin, the Skrydstrup woman, the Veksø helmets and a wall of lurs. One room, and most of this page.
  • Solvognens Fundsted, Odsherred The find spot in Trundholm Mose, now marked with a monument and open year-round. Mounds visible on the horizon in several directions.
  • Egtved The mound has been reconstructed on the spot, with a small exhibition. West of Vejle.
  • Madsebakke, Bornholm Denmark's best rock carvings — ships, sun wheels and footprints on bare granite.
  • Any ridge road in Jutland Genuinely. The mounds were sited to be seen from the route, and they still are.
