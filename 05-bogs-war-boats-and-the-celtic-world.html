Bogs, war-boats and the Celtic world

c. 500 BCE – 1 CE · førromersk jernalder · the Pre-Roman Iron Age

The poorest-looking period in Danish prehistory is also the one that produces the first villages, the first permanent fields, the first army we can count — and the first Danish faces anyone has ever looked into.

Introduction

WHAT THIS PAGE ANSWERSFive questions

  1. What happens to a society when the metal it depended on importing is suddenly available in the meadow behind the house?
  2. Why does a period with far less treasure than the Bronze Age represent a step forward rather than a collapse?
  3. Who put people in the bogs, and why — and how honest can we be about not knowing?
  4. How did a Celtic silver cauldron from south-eastern Europe end up in a Himmerland bog?
  5. When does the area we now call Denmark first appear in somebody's writing?

01 / NARRATIVEA colder, wetter world

Danish terms in this section

førromersk jernalder
Pre-Roman Iron Age, c. 500 BCE–1 CE; also called the Celtic Iron Age.
subatlantisk
The Subatlantic — the cooler, wetter climate phase beginning around 600–500 BCE.

The Bronze Age ended in bad weather. From around 600–500 BCE the climate across northern Europe turned cooler and wetter — the phase Danish palaeoecology calls Subatlantic — and the growing season shortened. Bogs grew. Land that had been workable became marginal.

At the same time the long southern connection that had carried metal north for twelve centuries reorganised itself, and the flow of bronze into Denmark dried to a trickle. The archaeological record responds immediately and dramatically. Graves become poor. Imports nearly vanish. Gold, which the Bronze Age had in quantity, disappears almost entirely. By the standard measure of buried treasure, the early Pre-Roman Iron Age is the poorest period in Danish prehistory since farming began.

It would be easy, and wrong, to read that as collapse. What actually happens over the next five hundred years is that Denmark acquires villages, fenced and organised; permanent field systems; stalled livestock; iron in quantity; and, at the end of it, an army capable of crossing water in formation. The society gets stronger while its graves get plainer. That mismatch is the interpretive problem of this chapter, and it is a useful corrective to a discipline that necessarily measures wealth by what people put in the ground.

02 / NARRATIVEIron out of the meadow

Danish terms in this section

myremalm
Bog iron — nodules of iron oxide that precipitate in wet ground where iron-rich groundwater meets air.
skaktovn
Shaft furnace: a clay-built stack in which ore and charcoal are burned together.
luppe
The bloom — the spongy mass of iron and slag taken from the furnace, then forged.
trækul
Charcoal, the fuel; producing it consumed very large quantities of wood.

Chapter 4 ended with the observation that iron changes the terms of trade. Here is what that means in practice.

In western Jutland, where groundwater carrying dissolved iron seeps into bogs and meadows and meets the air, the iron precipitates out as brown nodules in the peat. This is myremalm, bog iron, and it can be dug with a spade. Roast it to drive off water, build a clay shaft furnace, pack it with charcoal and ore, blow air through with bellows for a day, and you get a luppe — a lump of iron and slag which a smith then hammers out.

The ore is poor by any modern standard, the process is slow, and it consumes an enormous amount of wood. But the crucial point is that no part of it requires a trade route. A farming community in Jutland could make its own iron, from its own bog, with its own charcoal, and never ask anyone's permission.

And notice where it is. Bog iron forms where iron-rich groundwater seeps into wet ground — which in Denmark means the sandy outwash country west of the old ice margin, the poor half of the country that chapter 1 watched being laid down by meltwater and chapter 3 found filling with Single Grave barrows. The line has decided the soils, the archaeology, and a cultural frontier. Now it decides where the metal is. For the first time in this series, the light western soils are worth more than the heavy eastern ones.

The scale eventually becomes industrial. In parts of western and central Jutland, excavation has uncovered production sites with hundreds and in some cases thousands of individual furnace pits, each used once and abandoned — though the largest of these belong to the following centuries rather than to this chapter. Iron production on that scale reshapes the woodland around it, because every furnace charge means charcoal, and charcoal means coppiced or felled trees in quantity.

The consequences are structural. Bronze had been an elite monopoly — you needed distant contacts to get it, which is precisely why controlling it conferred power. Iron is a local resource, and a better one: harder, cheaper, and once you can make it in quantity, it goes into ploughshares, sickles, axes and knives rather than only into swords and neck-rings. Agricultural productivity rises. So does the number of people.

Something in the political order of the Bronze Age does not survive this. Whatever the chiefs of chapter 4 had been doing, the basis of it was gone.

03 / NARRATIVEThe village behind the fence

Danish terms in this section

landsby
Village — and this is the period in which the Danish village is invented.
digevoldingsagre
'Celtic fields': permanent plots divided by low earth banks built up from clearing stones and soil.
båseskillerum
Stall partitions in the byre end of a longhouse — the first direct evidence of housed cattle.
Hodde
An excavated village in west Jutland, c. 100 BCE, the fullest plan we have.

Chapter 3's farms were scattered. Chapter 4's were larger but still dispersed. Now, for the first time, people group their houses together and put a fence around the lot.

At Grøntoft in west Jutland, excavation found a settlement of the third century BCE where several farms sat inside a common enclosure — the earliest known village of this kind in Denmark. At Hodde, excavated by Steen Hvass in the 1970s, a village of around 100 BCE was uncovered in full: something like two dozen farmsteads inside a substantial fence, each with its own longhouse and its own small yard, laid out around an open space. One farm is conspicuously larger than the others, with more cattle stalls and a fence of its own.

That last detail is worth pausing on. A village is a decision. It means the households have agreed where the boundary runs, who is inside it, and — since the fence has gates — who is not. And a bigger farm inside a shared fence is the archaeological signature of a first among equals: someone who has more, in a community that can see exactly how much more.

At Borremose in Himmerland the arrangement is defensive. A village sits on an island in a bog, approached by a single paved causeway, behind a rampart and ditch. Whatever the fence at Hodde was for, the works at Borremose were for keeping people out.

Grøntoft is instructive because it was not built once. The excavations traced a settlement that moved repeatedly around its own small territory over generations, rebuilding on fresh ground — and that in its later phases the fence came first and the houses were fitted inside it. The community was planning at the level of the whole village before individual families built.

The houses themselves are three-aisled longhouses of the type chapter 4 introduced, typically twelve to twenty metres long, with living quarters at one end and cattle at the other. A village of Hodde's size therefore held perhaps a hundred and fifty to two hundred people. That is small enough that everyone knew everyone, and large enough that decisions had to be made somehow.

Fields and cattle

Around the villages lie digevoldingsagre — the field systems English calls Celtic fields, though they have nothing to do with Celts. Small rectangular plots, divided by low banks of earth and stone built up over generations of clearing, they represent permanent tenure of particular pieces of ground rather than shifting cultivation. Under the west Jutland heath, where later ploughing never reached, they survive in enormous spreads, visible from the air and now from airborne laser survey.

Inside the longhouses, the byre end acquires båseskillerum — partitions between individual stalls. This is the first hard evidence that cattle were housed over winter, and it comes with an unexpected gift: you can count the stalls. A house with room for twelve animals tells you something specific about a household's wealth, and comparing houses across a village tells you about its social structure. It is one of the few places in prehistory where the evidence is close to quantitative.

Housing cattle also means collecting manure, and manure means the same fields can be cropped year after year. The Celtic fields and the cattle stalls are the same innovation seen from two ends.

04 / NARRATIVEHjortspring: the oldest army

Danish terms in this section

krigsbytteoffer
War-booty offering: the captured equipment of a defeated army, destroyed and deposited in water.
ringbrynje
Mail — interlinked iron rings, here among the earliest examples known anywhere.
lindebast
Lime bast — the inner bark, twisted into cord and used to sew the hull planks together.

Nineteen metres, sewn together

Start with how it was made, because that is the part that ought to be famous.

Five planks of lime: one for the bottom, two broad ones a side, worked thin. No nails — the seams were sewn, with cord twisted from lime bast, and caulked. Carved blocks closed each end, their long projecting horns rising above and below the waterline. Ten thwarts seated two paddlers each; the paddles were maple, shaped individually. Finished, the whole vessel weighed about 530 kilograms — nineteen metres of boat that its own crew could lift and carry up a beach.

It was built to cross open water with about twenty men in it, and around 350 BCE it did, to Als, in company with three or four others. The people who came in it lost. The victors holed it, weighted it with stones and sank it in a small lake three kilometres inland, along with everything its crews had carried.

Two thousand two hundred years later, in the 1880s, peat-cutters working that bog kept striking pieces of old wood. It dried well and it burned well. Substantial parts of the oldest plank-built boat in northern Europe went into the kitchen stoves of Als before anyone thought to ask what it was. Word reached the National Museum only in 1920, and in 1921–22 a museum conservator named Gustav Rosenberg excavated the entire bog, all forty-five by fifty metres of it, to recover what was left.

The conservation nearly finished what the stoves had started. Treated with alum and glycerine, the wood cracked and disintegrated over the following decades, and by the 1960s the find was in crisis. It was rescued by re-treating everything with polyethylene glycol — the technique developed for the Swedish warship Vasa — and went back on display in the 1980s.

Gustav Rosenberg · Hjortspring Mose, Als, 1921–22

The boat is remarkable, but the boat is not the find. The find is what was in and around it.

Rosenberg recovered 138 iron spearheads and 38 more of bone and antler; around fifty wooden shields; swords; and fragments of mail, which are among the earliest known anywhere in Europe. The spear shafts of ash had been deliberately broken, some driven point-down into the bottom of the lake. The boat had been holed and sunk with stones.

Add it up and you have the equipment of a force of roughly eighty to a hundred men, fighting on foot, with standardised gear — not a war-band of individuals with whatever they owned, but something organised, equipped to a pattern, and led. They arrived on Als by sea, probably in four boats. They lost. The victors took everything they had, destroyed it, and gave it to a small sacred lake three or four kilometres inland.

This is the oldest of the great Scandinavian war-booty offerings, and it will not be the last: the practice runs on through chapter 6 at Illerup Ådal and Nydam on a far larger scale. Caesar, writing about Gaul in the 50s BCE, describes something very similar among the Gauls — captured goods heaped up at consecrated places, which nobody dares to steal from. Whatever this ritual was, it was not peculiar to Denmark.

One more thing. In 1999 a society on Als launched a full reconstruction, Tilia Alsie, built from lime trunks that had to be fetched from Poland because Denmark no longer grows trees that size. It handles open water in bad weather, and with a strong crew it has covered nearly fifty kilometres in five hours. A day's travel is on the order of a hundred kilometres. The army that attacked Als could have come from a very long way away.

Meanwhile in Europe

This is the Celtic centuries. The La Tène culture spread across much of temperate Europe from around 450 BCE; Celtic-speaking groups sacked Rome around 387 BCE and Delphi in 279, and settled as far east as central Anatolia. Denmark is not Celtic, but it sits at the northern edge of a Celtic-influenced world and takes styles, objects and probably ideas from it.

Meanwhile Rome grows. By the time the Hjortspring army was defeated on Als, Rome was fighting Carthage. By the end of this chapter it has annexed Gaul, reached the Rhine, and is about to become the neighbour that defines the next four hundred years of Danish history.

05 / NARRATIVEThe ordinary dead

Danish terms in this section

urnegravplads
Urn cemetery: a flat burial ground of cremations in pottery vessels, without mounds.
brandgrav
Cremation grave.
fibula
Brooch — the commonest grave good, and the archaeologist's best dating tool for the period.

Before the bogs, it is worth knowing how people were normally buried, because otherwise the bog bodies get mistaken for the standard practice. They are the opposite of it.

The rule in this period is cremation, continuing the practice that began in chapter 4. The body is burnt, the bones collected, and the remains placed in a pottery urn set in a flat grave. There is no mound. The cemeteries are large — hundreds of graves at some sites, used over centuries — and they sit close to the settlements they served.

What goes in with the dead is very little: often a brooch, sometimes a knife, a pin, a small pot. Occasionally nothing identifiable at all. Compared with the oak coffins of chapter 4, with their swords and gold and folding stools, this looks like a society that had either become far poorer or stopped believing that the dead needed equipping.

Which of those it was is genuinely uncertain, and it matters. The equipment in a grave is a statement made by the living about the dead, and a society can change its mind about what statements are appropriate without changing its actual distribution of wealth at all. The Hodde evidence — one farm markedly larger than its neighbours, with more cattle stalls — suggests inequality was present and visible in life. It simply was not being expressed at the graveside.

Towards the end of the period that changes again. A handful of burials in northern Jutland stand out sharply from the rest, with wagons, imported bronze vessels and weapons. Whatever restraint had governed burial for four hundred years is loosening, and the direction it loosens in points straight at chapter 6, where a new elite will bury itself with Roman silver.

So: hundreds of urns in flat cemeteries, plain and orderly, is the normal Danish death in this period. A hanged man laid carefully in a bog with his eyes closed is not.

06 / NARRATIVEThe bog people

Danish terms in this section

moselig
Bog body — a corpse preserved by the acid, cold and oxygen-free conditions of a raised bog.
sphagnum
Bog moss; its decay releases the acids and tanning agents that preserve skin and hair while dissolving bone.
Mosefolket
P.V. Glob's 1965 book, which made the Danish bog bodies world-famous.

And then there are the faces.

Raised bogs are acidic, cold and anaerobic, and the sphagnum moss that builds them releases compounds that tan skin like leather. Bone dissolves; skin, hair, nails, stomach contents and clothing survive. A body that goes into such a bog and is not disturbed can come out two thousand years later looking as though it went in last week.

Denmark has produced more of these than almost anywhere. In 1950 two brothers cutting peat at Bjældskovdal near Silkeborg uncovered a man so well preserved that they telephoned the police. He is now called the Tollund Man, and his head — eyes closed, mouth slightly pursed, stubble on the chin, a plaited leather rope still round his neck — is the single most reproduced image in Danish archaeology. His last meal, recovered from his gut, was a porridge of barley and seeds. He had been hanged, and then laid in the bog in the position of someone asleep, with his eyes and mouth closed.

Two years later, and thirty kilometres away, peat cutters found the Grauballe Man, dated to around 290 BCE. His throat had been cut from ear to ear. The Elling Woman, from the same bog as Tollund, was hanged, and her long hair was dressed in an elaborate plait. The Haraldskær Woman had been pinned into the peat with wooden hooks at the knees and elbows and stakes across the body, and shows signs of strangulation.

The details are what stay with people. The Tollund Man wore nothing but a pointed cap of skin and a hide belt — he had been stripped, or had never been dressed for this. The Elling Woman's hair was worked into a plait nearly a metre long, tied in a complicated knot. The Grauballe Man's hands are unworn: whatever he did, it was not heavy field labour.

It also helps to remember that people were not the only thing going into the bogs. The same wet places received pottery full of food, joints of meat, whole animals, wooden vessels, tools, and — as sections 04 and 08 of this page show — boats, weapons and imported silver. Deposition in water was the standard Danish way of dealing with the sacred, and had been since chapter 3. The bodies belong to a practice, not an aberration.

What was being done, and to whom? There are three standing answers, and they are not mutually exclusive: religious sacrifice; judicial execution; and the disposal of people considered unsuitable for normal burial. Tacitus, writing about the Germanic peoples around 98 CE — a century after this chapter closes, and from a considerable distance — reports that cowards and shirkers were pressed down into bogs under hurdles. That has been quoted in support of the execution reading for two hundred years, but it is a Roman moralist describing foreigners he never visited, and it should carry roughly the weight one would give a modern newspaper explaining a distant country's customs.

The care in some of the depositions cuts the other way. Somebody closed the Tollund Man's eyes and mouth. Somebody arranged him. Whatever had just been done to him, it was not done carelessly.

07 / NARRATIVENaming the dead

Bog bodies present a difficulty that no other archaeological material does: they look like people we might know, and so the temptation to identify them is almost irresistible. Danish archaeology has a long and instructive record of giving in to it.

The queen who wasn't

On 20 October 1835, two men digging a boundary ditch through a bog near Haraldskær, west of Vejle, found the body of a woman. She was naked, her long hair was intact, and she was held down in the peat by branches laid across her. They thought she had died recently, and she was taken by cart to the hospital in Vejle, where the district physician examined her.

The historian Niels Matthias Petersen then proposed, in an article of 1836–37, that she was Queen Gunhild of Norway — lured to Denmark by Harald Bluetooth with a promise of marriage and murdered in a bog instead, according to the sagas. The find spot was ten kilometres from Jelling. The identification was accepted. King Frederik VI paid for a fine coffin, and the woman was given a Christian royal burial in Sankt Nicolai Church in Vejle, where the coffin still stands.

A young archaeologist from Vejle disagreed. J.J.A. Worsaae — the same man who would later argue with Steenstrup about oyster shells in chapter 2 — had studied bog finds in England and knew that preserved bodies in peat were not unique and not necessarily recent. In 1842 he published a demolition in Historisk Tidsskrift. Petersen had claimed the woman wore costly clothing; Worsaae could find no evidence of it, and Petersen was eventually obliged to admit he had never examined the garments. There were no rich ornaments, nothing indicating high status. She was, Worsaae concluded, an ordinary woman of the earliest Iron Age.

Radiocarbon dating in 1977 put her at around 490 BCE. Worsaae had been right by more than fourteen hundred years.

And she is still called Queen Gunhild. The name has survived the disproof by nearly two centuries, which tells you something about the relative strength of evidence and of a good story.

Haraldskær Mose, 1835 · J.J.A. Worsaae, 1842 · radiocarbon, 1977

The pattern repeated. When the Grauballe Man came out of the ground in 1952, a local woman identified him confidently as Red Christian, a peat-cutter of the district who had gone missing drunk in 1887. It took radiocarbon dating to establish that he had been in the bog for twenty-two centuries.

What made the bog bodies famous beyond archaeology was P.V. Glob's book Mosefolket in 1965 — written partly as a letter to schoolgirls who had asked him about the Tollund Man, and translated as The Bog People. It reached readers who would never open an excavation report, and it drew the attention of poets, notably Seamus Heaney, whose bog poems put these Danish dead into English literature.

Which raises a question this page cannot settle. These are the bodies of people who were, on any reading, killed. They are displayed in glass cases and photographed for schoolbooks. The Egyptian and North American material of comparable status is now, in many museums, quietly no longer on show.

08 / NARRATIVEThe Celtic connection

The greatest object of the Danish Iron Age is not Danish.

In 1891, peat-cutters in Rævemose near Aars in Himmerland found a large silver vessel, taken apart and stacked. Reassembled, the Gundestrup cauldron is about sixty-nine centimetres across, made of some nine kilograms of silver, its plates worked in high relief with a crowded and utterly unfamiliar imagery: a horned figure sitting cross-legged holding a torc and a serpent; a giant dipping a man head-first into a vat; elephants; warriors blowing long animal-headed war trumpets; wheels, bulls, and gods larger than the humans around them.

Almost none of it is northern. The horned figure is generally identified with the Celtic Cernunnos; the torcs and the carnyx trumpets are Celtic; but the silversmithing technique, the punchwork, and several of the animals point to Thracian workshops in what is now Bulgaria or Romania. The usual conclusion is that it was made somewhere in south-eastern Europe between roughly 150 BCE and the turn of the era.

How it got to a bog in Himmerland, nobody knows. Loot, gift, trade, dowry, or the property of someone who had travelled — all have been argued. What is certain is that it was carefully dismantled and deposited, exactly as Danish communities had been depositing valuable things in wet ground since chapter 3. A foreign object, treated according to a local rule that was by then three thousand years old.

It is not alone. The two ornate wagons from Dejbjerg in west Jutland, found in the 1880s, are ceremonial vehicles with bronze fittings in a style that is Celtic or Celtic-derived. Imported bronze cauldrons, brooches and glass appear across the country in small numbers through the later part of the period.

The right way to read all this is that Denmark was not Celtic and was not conquered by anyone, but it was connected — a northern participant in a European world whose centre of gravity lay several hundred kilometres south, taking what it wanted and using it for its own purposes.

09 / NARRATIVEDenmark enters the written record

Everything in this series so far has been mute. Around the end of this chapter, for the first time, somebody writes something down.

The earliest candidate is Pytheas of Massalia, a Greek from Marseille who around 325 BCE made a voyage into the northern ocean, described a land called Thule six days' sail beyond Britain, and reported on coasts where amber was gathered. His own book is lost; we have him only in fragments quoted, often sceptically, by later writers. Whether he saw Jutland is unprovable.

The second is not in doubt as an event, only as a location. Around 120 BCE, Roman sources record, a people called the Cimbri left their homeland, joined by the Teutones and others, and moved south. They destroyed a Roman army at Noreia in 113 BCE. At Arausio in 105 they inflicted one of the worst defeats in Roman history — the casualty figures given by ancient authors are enormous and probably inflated, but the shock was real, and it contributed directly to the army reforms of Gaius Marius. Marius then destroyed the Teutones at Aquae Sextiae in 102 and the Cimbri at Vercellae in 101. The survivors were enslaved.

Roman writers place the Cimbri's homeland in the far north, and from antiquity onwards the name has been connected with Himmerland in northern Jutland, and the Teutones with Thy. The similarity of the names is genuine and old — the Roman geographers already located a Cimbric peninsula in the north.

But the connection should be held loosely. Name resemblance is weak evidence; Danish archaeology shows no clear trace of a mass departure around 120 BCE, and it is quite possible that Roman authors attached a vaguely known northern name to a migration whose origin they did not actually know. Nineteenth- and twentieth-century Danish and German nationalism found the identification extremely attractive, which is another reason to be careful with it.

What can be said without hedging is this: from around 100 BCE the literate Mediterranean world knows that people live up here, and has a name for some of them. Denmark stops being purely prehistoric — not because Danes write, but because somebody else does.

Meanwhile in the world

The centuries of this chapter are among the most crowded in world history. It opens around the time of the Buddha and Confucius, and the founding of the Roman Republic and Athenian democracy. Alexander reaches the Indus in 326 BCE. China is unified under the Qin in 221 and the Han dynasty follows. Carthage is destroyed in 146. Rome becomes an empire in the decades after this chapter closes.

None of this is visible from a Jutland village — but the last of it will be, within a generation.

10 / NARRATIVERome arrives

In the decades either side of the turn of the era, Roman armies push to the Rhine and then to the Elbe. In 9 CE three legions are destroyed in the Teutoburg Forest and the Roman frontier settles back on the Rhine and the Danube for good.

Denmark is never conquered, never occupied and never seriously threatened. It lies several hundred kilometres beyond the furthest Roman post. But a permanent, wealthy, insatiable market with a standing army now sits within reach of a boat and a few weeks' travel, and it wants things the north can supply — hides, furs, amber, cattle and, most consequentially, men.

The flow of imported goods into Denmark, which had been a trickle for five hundred years, becomes a flood. And that is the Roman Iron Age (6).

What that meant on the ground took a century or so to become visible, and it did not look like conquest. It looked like better goods.

A bronze vessel of a quality no local smith could make. Glass, which nobody here had seen. Silver. Coins, useless as money and unmistakable as proof that their owner had reached something enormous. The people who could get hold of these things could do something their grandparents could not: they could put a permanent distance between themselves and their neighbours.

Look back at what this chapter has described and the change is stark. Villages behind a common fence, where one farm is slightly larger. Cemeteries of plain urns, hundreds of them, with a brooch apiece. An army whose equipment was standardised enough that the victors could destroy it as a single category. This was a society with visible inequality and a strong preference for not displaying it at the graveside.

Within a few generations of the turn of the era that restraint is gone, and a man on Lolland is buried with a Roman silver dinner service. Nothing was conquered and nothing was imposed. A market opened several hundred kilometres to the south, and the people best placed to reach it stopped pretending to be like everybody else.

Myth-check

"The Haraldskær woman is Queen Gunhild."
Disproved in 1842 and again by radiocarbon in 1977: she died around 490 BCE, roughly fourteen centuries before Gunhild. The coffin Frederik VI paid for is still in the church in Vejle, and the name is still in use.
"Bog bodies were all human sacrifices."
Sacrifice, execution and irregular burial are all live explanations and the evidence differs from body to body. Anyone who tells you confidently which it was is going beyond what we know.
"Celts lived in Denmark."
Celtic-style objects did. The Gundestrup cauldron was probably made in south-eastern Europe; the Dejbjerg wagons are Celtic-influenced. There is no evidence for Celtic-speaking populations in Denmark, and 'Celtic Iron Age' is a label about contacts, not inhabitants.
"The Cimbri were Danes who beat the Romans."
The Cimbri were real and their campaign was real. That they came from Himmerland rests largely on the resemblance of names, is not supported by any clear archaeological signal, and was a nineteenth-century national favourite for obvious reasons. Treat it as possible, not established.
"The Iron Age was a decline from the Bronze Age."
Fewer imports and poorer graves; more villages, more fields, more cattle, more iron and more people. Buried treasure is a measure of what a society sacrificed, not of how well it was doing.

Summary

IF YOU REMEMBER FIVE THINGSThe page in five

  1. Bog iron ended Denmark's dependence on imported metal. Any community with a bog, charcoal and patience could make its own — which destroyed the basis of Bronze Age elite power and raised agricultural output.

  2. The village is invented here: fenced settlements like Grøntoft and Hodde, permanent Celtic fields, and longhouses with stalls you can count to estimate a household's cattle.

  3. The Hjortspring deposit of about 350 BCE is the equipment of a defeated army of eighty to a hundred men, with northern Europe's oldest plank-built boat — destroyed and given to a lake by the victors.

  4. The bogs preserved faces. Tollund, Grauballe, Elling and Haraldskær are the first Danes we can look at, and we do not know whether they were sacrificed, executed or simply excluded.

  5. Around 100 BCE the Cimbri and Teutones enter Roman history, and with them, possibly, the Danish peninsula. It is the first time this place appears in anybody's writing — written by somebody else.

Questions & discussion

FOUR KINDSWork the material

Recall

Did the facts land?

  • What is myremalm, and what do you need besides ore to turn it into iron?
  • What was found at Hjortspring besides the boat, and what does it add up to?
  • Why do bog bodies preserve skin and hair but not bone?
  • Where was the Gundestrup cauldron probably made, and how do we know it wasn't made here?
  • Who were the Cimbri, and what is the evidence connecting them to Jutland?

Causal

Why did that follow from this?

  • Why does a locally available metal undermine an elite, when an imported one sustained it?
  • Why does housing cattle over winter lead to permanent fields?
  • Why would a victorious community destroy captured weapons rather than use them?
  • Why is a period with poor graves not necessarily a poor period — and what would you need to measure instead?

Counterfactual

Pull one thread and see what moves.

  • Suppose Denmark had no bog iron. How long does dependence on imported metal last, and what does that do to the society of the Roman Iron Age (6)?
  • Suppose the Hjortspring victors had kept the weapons instead of sinking them. What would we know about Iron Age warfare, and what would we not?

Contested

Where researchers actually disagree — and where ethics enters.

  • Sacrifice, execution, or exclusion? Take the Tollund Man specifically: what in the evidence supports each reading, and what would settle it?
  • Tacitus is routinely quoted on Germanic bog punishments. He was writing a century later, about people he had not visited, with a moralising purpose. How much weight should a near-contemporary outside source carry when there is nothing else?
  • Bog bodies are the remains of people who were killed, displayed in glass cases. Museums have quietly withdrawn comparable human remains from display elsewhere. What distinguishes these — and is the distinction principled or just familiar?
  • The Cimbri identification was politically useful to nineteenth-century nationalism. How should a national museum present a claim that is possible, unproven, and flattering?
