Gold, catastrophe, and a people with a name

c. 400 – 750 · germansk jernalder · folkevandringstid and Vendel period

More gold went into the Danish ground in this period than in any other. It went in during the worst two centuries anyone here had ever lived through — and out the other side came something that could organise a canal, a rampart and a town, and that had a name.

01 / NARRATIVEThe gold century

Danish terms in this section

germansk jernalder
Germanic Iron Age, c. 400–750; the earlier half is the Migration Period.
folkevandringstid
'The time of the wandering of peoples' — the Migration Period, c. 400–550.
guldbrakteat
Gold bracteate: a thin gold disc stamped on one side and worn as a pendant.
solidus
The late Roman gold coin; the raw material for much Scandinavian gold work.

Chapter 6 left the western Roman Empire coming apart. For Denmark, the immediate effect of that collapse was not impoverishment. It was gold.

As the empire bought off, paid and was plundered by the peoples on its edges, enormous quantities of gold moved north and west — as tribute, as mercenary pay, as loot. Late Roman gold solidi reach Scandinavia in numbers, and northern goldsmiths melt them down and make something else. The fifth and sixth centuries produce more gold objects in Denmark than any other period before or since.

The quantities are hard to pin down, because so much has been melted down over the centuries. But Lotte Hedeager's figure for surviving Migration-Period gold from Denmark alone is over fifty kilograms — more than the country has produced from the entire Bronze Age, the entire Viking Age (which ran on silver) and the entire Middle Ages combined. It arrives in a period of about a hundred and fifty years, and much of it goes straight into the ground.

The characteristic object is the guldbrakteat: a thin disc of gold, stamped from one side with a die so the image stands out in relief, with a loop for suspension. Over a thousand are known across northern Europe, more than two hundred of them carrying runes.

They began as imitations. Late Roman emperors distributed gold medallions bearing an imperial portrait, and the first bracteates copy that layout closely — a head in profile, a legend around the rim. But within a couple of generations the copying stops being copying. The emperor's head becomes a different sort of figure, often over a horse, accompanied by birds; the legend becomes runes in a northern language; and the whole object turns into something with no Roman equivalent at all.

What that something is has been debated for a century. The consensus reading is religious: the figure is a god or a god-like ruler, the birds are his, the horse is his, and the object is an amulet. Which brings us to what a bracteate found in a Jutland field in 2020 turned out to say.

02 / NARRATIVEVindelev

A sixtieth birthday present

In December 2020, Ole Ginnerup Schytz went out with a metal detector he had been given for his sixtieth birthday. It was the first time he had used it. He was walking a field belonging to Jørgen Antonsen, a friend since childhood, on the family farm at Vindelev — eight kilometres north-east of Jelling, in east Jutland.

What came up, from about ten centimetres down in the ploughsoil, was gold. Twenty-three objects in all, weighing just under eight hundred grams: four Roman medallions from the fourth century, a gold mount from a sword scabbard, and a set of bracteates including several of extraordinary size — one of them nearly fourteen centimetres across, the largest ever found anywhere, the size of a saucer.

Vejlemuseerne excavated the site properly in August 2021 and established that the hoard had been buried inside a longhouse, in a village, around fifteen hundred years ago. Morten Axboe, who has spent a career on Scandinavian gold, was asked to rate it against other Danish finds: on a scale of one to ten, he said, it was a twelve.

Ole Ginnerup Schytz and Jørgen Antonsen · Vindelev, December 2020

Then, in 2023, it got better. The National Museum's runologist Lisbeth Imer and the linguist Krister Vasshus published a reading of one of the bracteates — thirty-four runes, eight words, worn and hard to make out, written without spaces in a language fifteen hundred years old.

Part of it reads: he is Odin's man.

It refers to the portrait on the disc — some unnamed king or magnate, possibly called Jaga or Jagaz. And it is the oldest occurrence of the name Odin anywhere in the world, by about a century and a half.

Two things follow. First, the gods of Norse mythology, which most people meet through Icelandic texts written down in the thirteenth century, were being named in Denmark in the early 400s — eight hundred years earlier. Second, and more revealing, the sentence is about legitimacy. A ruler is being described as belonging to a god. That is a claim about the right to rule, and it is the earliest such claim we have from this country.

Imer said the runes were the hardest inscription she had faced in twenty years, and that she had not seen anything so well cut, or so long, from this period since the Golden Horns.

03 / NARRATIVEThe Golden Horns

Danish terms in this section

Guldhornene
The Golden Horns of Gallehus — two gold drinking horns of c. 400 CE.
Kunstkammeret
The royal art chamber in Copenhagen, the ancestor of the National Museum.
urnordisk
Proto-Norse — the language of the inscription on the shorter horn.

They are the most famous objects in Danish history, and nobody alive has seen them.

Found twice, lost once

On 20 July 1639 a girl named Kirsten Svendsdatter was walking near Gallehus, by Møgeltønder in south-west Jutland, and caught her foot on something in the ground. It was a horn of gold, nearly seventy-six centimetres long, decorated in bands with figures, animals and riders. It went to Christian IV, who sent her a skirt.

The king wanted it made into a drinking cup in honour of antiquity. A courtier, Joachim Gersdorff, pointed out that it had almost certainly been a drinking horn already, and that all it needed was a screw to close the narrow end. One was made, engraved with the date. The historian Ole Worm recorded that in 1640 people were drinking out of it at Nykøbing castle. It held about a litre and a quarter.

Ninety-five years later, on 21 April 1734, a smallholder called Erik Lassen was digging clay a few paces from the same spot and found the second horn. It was shorter, about fifty centimetres, and heavier — some 3.7 kilograms. Together the two horns held nearly seven kilograms of gold. Lassen handed his to the count at Schackenborg, who sent it on to the king, who awarded him 200 rigsdaler.

The shorter horn carried a runic inscription in Proto-Norse, and it is one of the most quoted sentences in Danish: ek hlewagastiz holtijaz horna tawido — I, Hlewagastiz, Holt's son, made the horn. A maker signing his work, around the year 400.

On the night of 4 May 1802 the horns were stolen from the Kunstkammer in Copenhagen by a goldsmith and convicted forger named Niels Heidenreich, who melted them down and sold the gold. He was caught the following year. Police recovered five pounds of melted metal at his sister's house; most of it had already been worked into other things and dispersed around the city. Two earrings he had given to a Copenhagen woman were never traced by the police at the time, and are probably the last surviving gold of the horns — they are now in a museum on Funen.

Kirsten Svendsdatter, 1639 · Erik Lassen, 1734 · Niels Heidenreich, 1802

The theft did something nobody could have predicted. Within weeks, a young writer named Adam Oehlenschläger published a poem called Guldhornene — about the gods giving the horns to a people who failed to understand them, and taking them back. It is conventionally treated as the opening work of Danish Romanticism, and it made the Golden Horns into a national symbol far more powerful than they had been as objects in a cabinet.

So the horns exist now only as drawings — careful ones, made before 1802 — and as reconstructions based on them. What Denmark actually possesses is an idea of them. There is something almost too neat about a country whose most famous prehistoric objects survive only as a description and a poem about their loss, and chapter 31 will pick the thread up when it deals with the Golden Age.

04 / NARRATIVE536: the year the sun failed

In the year 536 the sun dimmed. Contemporaries around the Mediterranean described a veil over the sun for a year or more, giving light without warmth. Tree rings across the northern hemisphere record the coldest decade in something like two thousand years. The cause is now generally taken to be one or more massive volcanic eruptions, followed by a second in 540, throwing enough material into the stratosphere to shut down summers.

Then, in 541, the Justinianic plague — the first documented pandemic of bubonic plague — began working through the Mediterranean world and beyond.

Scandinavia was hit exceptionally hard, and the reason is latitude. A society farming at the northern edge of the possible has no margin. A run of failed harvests here is not hardship; it is famine. The archaeological signal is dramatic: across parts of Sweden and Norway, settlements are abandoned wholesale and not reoccupied, and in Denmark too the settlement pattern is disrupted and some districts thin out sharply.

It has been argued — and it is a suggestion rather than a demonstration — that this is where the Norse idea of the fimbulvetr comes from: the great winter of three years without summer that precedes the end of the world in myths written down seven centuries later. If so, it is a memory of an actual event, carried orally for twenty generations.

Now put that beside the gold. The deposition of gold in the Danish ground peaks in exactly this window. The Vindelev hoard was buried around then; so were many others.

The obvious reading is that people were giving their most valuable possessions to the gods on a scale never seen before or since, because the sky had gone wrong and the crops were failing. An alternative is more prosaic: that gold was buried for safekeeping in a time of violence and its owners did not survive to retrieve it. Both may be true of different hoards. What is not in doubt is that Denmark's richest period of gold deposition coincides precisely with its worst catastrophe.

Meanwhile in Europe

The western Roman Empire ended formally in 476. Its provinces became kingdoms: Franks in Gaul, Visigoths in Spain, Ostrogoths and then Lombards in Italy. The eastern empire, ruled from Constantinople, survived — and under Justinian tried to reconquer the west, a project that the plague of 541 helped to wreck.

Christianity spread north through those kingdoms. Clovis of the Franks converted around 500; Pope Gregory sent a mission to the English in 597. By the end of this chapter, Christian kingdoms lie immediately south of Denmark, and Charlemagne's grandfather is in power. The pressure that will define the Viking Age (8–11) is already building.

05 / NARRATIVEEverybody left. Did they?

The other great event of the fifth century, seen from here, is that a substantial number of people got in boats and went to Britain.

Bede, writing in Northumbria in 731, gives the classic account: the Britons invited Germanic warriors to help against their enemies, and the newcomers came from three peoples — the Saxons, the Angles, and the Jutes. The Angles, he says, came from Angeln, the district between Flensburg and the Schlei in what is now northern Germany but was then very much the Danish world. The Jutes, on the obvious reading of the name, came from Jutland.

The English consequences are enormous and well known: the language you are reading is descended from what those people brought with them, and it has been argued that the earliest English kingdoms carried institutions from the same source.

The Danish consequences are argued about. There is a real archaeological gap in parts of Jutland in the fifth century — settlements go quiet, cemeteries stop being used — and one long-standing reading is straightforward depopulation: they went, and the land lay empty until people from the islands moved in.

The counter-argument is that the gap is patchier than it looks, that changing burial customs can make a population invisible without removing it, and that the 536 catastrophe supplies a much better explanation for settlement abandonment than emigration a century earlier.

The honest summary: substantial emigration from this region to Britain certainly happened; the scale and the demographic effect at the Danish end remain unresolved. It is worth adding that this question has never been politically neutral. A Jutland emptied by Angles and refilled by Danes is a different national story from a Jutland continuously Danish, and both versions have been useful to somebody.

06 / NARRATIVEThe long recovery

Danish terms in this section

dyrestil
Animal style — the interlaced beast ornament of Germanic art, in successive phases.
sejl
Sail. Its arrival in the north, around 700, is the single most consequential technical change in this series.
-lev, -inge, -sted
Place-name endings coined in this period; much of the Danish village map dates from here.

The two centuries between the catastrophe and the Viking Age are the thinnest stretch in Danish prehistory for spectacular finds, and among the most important for what was actually being built.

The gold runs out first. It had come from a collapsing empire, and once the supply of late Roman and Byzantine coin dried up there was no more of it. By the seventh century silver has replaced gold as the metal of status, and the sheer weight of precious metal in circulation falls sharply.

What does not decline is craftsmanship. Northern smiths develop the interlaced dyrestil — animals stretched, split, folded and woven into each other until the eye has to work to find a head. It is shared across the whole Germanic north, and it is the direct ancestor of Viking art and a cousin of the interlace in Irish and Northumbrian manuscripts. The Sutton Hoo ship burial in East Anglia, from around 625, contains a helmet and shoulder clasps whose closest parallels are not English but Scandinavian. The North Sea in this period is not a barrier between separate worlds; it is one world with water in the middle.

On the ground in Denmark, something quieter and more permanent is happening. Settlements stop wandering. Chapter 6's Vorbasse moved every century or two; from now on villages increasingly stay put, and many of them occupy the exact ground their medieval successors will occupy. The place-name layer of Denmark forms in these centuries too — the villages ending in -lev, -inge and -sted that cover the map today. When you drive through Danish countryside you are, mostly, driving through a settlement pattern laid out between the catastrophe and the Vikings.

The sail

And then there is the thing that changes everything, and about which the evidence is frustratingly thin.

Chapter 6 left the Nydam boat of around 310 CE: clinker-built, iron-riveted, and rowed. It is a superb hull and it has no mast. Four hundred years later, Scandinavian ships have sails.

Exactly when is not settled. Mediterranean and Roman ships had used sail for millennia and northerners had seen them; the technology was not secret. The best current view is that the sail comes into general use in Scandinavia in the decades around 700, though earlier limited use is possible and some argue for it. Picture stones on Gotland show masted ships from roughly this period, and by the time we have an excavated ship with a keelson and a mast step — Oseberg, in Norway, from 834 — the technology is mature.

What a sail does is convert a coastal raiding craft into an ocean-going one. A rowed ship is limited by how long men can pull; a sailed ship is limited by food and water. Add sail to the clinker hull that the north had been perfecting since Nydam, and the distance a Danish crew can reach in a season goes from tens of kilometres to thousands.

Lindisfarne is sacked in 793. The technology that made it possible arrives in the century before, and no chronicler recorded it.

07 / NARRATIVEHalls

Danish terms in this section

hal
Hall: a large single-room building for feasting, gift-giving and rule.
guldgubber
'Gold old-men' — tiny stamped gold foils, a few millimetres across, found in hundreds at cult sites.
centralplads
Central place: a settlement combining rule, cult, craft and trade.

Out of this period comes a building type that will dominate northern politics for the next six hundred years: the hall.

A hall is not a farmhouse. It is a single great room, often thirty to fifty metres long, without the byre end that every earlier Danish longhouse had. Nobody keeps cattle in it. What happens in it is feasting, drinking, the giving of gifts and the receiving of oaths — which is to say, government. A lord who can fill a hall and feed the people in it has a following; a following is power.

At Lejre on Zealand, excavation has revealed a sequence of enormous halls built and rebuilt from the sixth century onwards, the largest around fifty metres long. Lejre is where medieval tradition placed the seat of the legendary Skjoldung kings, and it is where the Old English poem Beowulf places Heorot, the hall of King Hrothgar. The poem is a poem, and its dates and people are not history. But the archaeologists found halls of the right size, in the right place, in approximately the right centuries — which is an unusual and slightly unnerving thing for a legend to be right about.

Gudme on Funen, which chapter 6 met as an emerging centre, is now at its height: a hall complex inland, the seasonal trading beach at Lundeborg five kilometres away, gold in quantity, and workshops handling precious metal. And on Bornholm, at Sorte Muld, excavation has produced something found almost nowhere else in such numbers — thousands of guldgubber, gold foils the size of a fingernail stamped with human figures, usually a man and a woman facing each other. They are too small and too thin to be jewellery. The likeliest reading is that they were offerings, made and given by the hundred at a cult site attached to a hall.

08 / NARRATIVEThe Dani get a name

Somewhere in the sixth century, people in southern Scandinavia acquire a collective name in somebody else's writing.

The Gothic writer Jordanes, working in Constantinople around 551, compiled a description of the northern island of Scandza and its peoples, and among them he lists the Dani. He adds that they had driven out the Heruli and claimed their territory, and — in a line that got quoted for the next fifteen centuries — that they were of notable height. Around the same period the Byzantine historian Procopius also mentions the Danes.

This is the first time the word appears. It is worth being careful about what it does and does not tell us. It does not tell us there was a Danish kingdom, or a Danish king, or a defined territory. It tells us that a people identifiable as Danes existed, that they were doing something energetic enough for the news to reach the Mediterranean, and that outsiders had a name for them.

Where they were is not stated. The name is later attached to the whole area — Danmark, the mark or borderland of the Danes — but that compound is not attested until chapter 10, on a stone at Jelling and in a king's mouth in England. What we have here is a people, not a country.

The consolidation implied is real, though. Somebody was pulling the petty kingdoms of the islands and Jutland together, or at least fighting over them at a scale that made a single name useful to outsiders. And within two centuries the results of that process become impossible to miss.

09 / NARRATIVEThree things that need a state

Danish terms in this section

Danevirke
'The Danes' work' — the rampart across the neck of the Jutland peninsula.
Kanhavekanalen
The Kanhave canal, cut across the narrowest point of the island of Samsø.
dendrokronologi
Dendrochronology — dating timber by its tree rings, often to the exact year.

In the first half of the eighth century, three things happen in Denmark that no chieftain with a hall could have done.

First, around 726, a canal is cut across the island of Samsø at its narrowest point: five hundred metres long, eleven metres wide, revetted with oak planking, deep enough for the ships of the day. It saves a long sail around the island and lets a fleet based there move quickly to either coast. It is a work of naval infrastructure.

Second, around 737, a substantial phase of the Danevirke goes up — a rampart running east to west across the neck of the Jutland peninsula, closing the land approach from the south. Earlier beginnings have been argued for, but the tree-ring dates put major construction firmly in this decade. It is a border defence, and a border defence implies both a border and something on the other side worth defending against.

Third, at Ribe on the west coast, a market place is laid out around 704–710 with plots divided by ditches in a regular pattern. It is not a village that grew. It was planned, and someone had the authority to plan it. Ribe is the oldest town in Denmark and one of the oldest in Scandinavia, and it belongs properly to the trading towns (9).

Dendrochronology is what makes this section possible, and it is worth pausing on. Oak grows a ring a year, wide in good years and narrow in bad, and the pattern of wide and narrow over a lifetime is effectively a barcode shared by every tree in a region. Match a timber's rings against a master sequence built from thousands of samples and you get the year the tree was felled — not a range, a year. The Kanhave planking and the Danevirke timbers are dated this way. For the first time in this series, we have exact dates.

Take the three together. A canal, a frontier work and a planned town, within about thirty years. Each requires the mobilisation of large amounts of labour from a wide area, sustained over a season or more, to build something that benefits nobody in particular and everybody in general. That is not what a chieftain does with his retinue. That is a state, or near enough to one that the distinction stops mattering.

We do not know his name. But by 737 somebody was giving orders across most of what we would recognise as Denmark, and had been obeyed.

Meanwhile in the world

The century that closes this chapter is one of the most consequential in world history. Islam emerged in Arabia in the 620s and, within a hundred years, ruled from Spain to the Indus. The Umayyad advance into France was checked at Poitiers in 732 — five years before the Danevirke timbers were felled.

In China the Tang dynasty presided over what is often reckoned the most cosmopolitan empire on earth. And a consequence of the Islamic expansion will reach Denmark directly within a century: the silver of the caliphate, which chapter 9 will find arriving in Danish towns by the tonne.

10 / NARRATIVEWhat Part B leaves behind

This chapter closes the long prehistory. From here on there are names, dates, kings and documents — still thin, still mostly written by foreigners, but present.

What the four chapters of Part B produced, between 1700 BCE and 750 CE, is a country. Not a nation, and not yet a state in any developed sense, but a recognisable place with recognisable problems. It has a southern land border it feels the need to fortify. It has a fleet, and a canal to move it. It has a town, and a trading system reaching to the Mediterranean and the Rhine. It has kings who claim descent from gods, and a name that outsiders use.

And it has the three permanent facts that chapter 1 identified: a landscape divided by an ice margin, no raw materials worth speaking of, and a position on the water that everyone else has to pass through. Everything in the next thirty-three chapters is worked out from those.

Stand at Jelling and you can see the join. The two great mounds and the runestones belong to Jelling (10), three centuries after this page ends. But the gold from Vindelev, displayed a few hundred metres away, was buried eight kilometres from that spot around the year 500, by people who thought their ruler was Odin's man.

Between the hoard and the stones lie the two centuries this chapter has just described: the catastrophe, the recovery, the settlements that stopped moving, the villages acquiring the names they still have, and — somewhere in there, unrecorded by anyone — a mast being stepped into a clinker hull for the first time.

Everything after this point in the series has documents. Thin ones, mostly written by foreigners and mostly hostile, but documents: annals, charters, chronicles, laws, and eventually letters and diaries and newspapers and film. This is the last chapter assembled almost entirely from things people left in the ground without meaning to say anything.

They said a great deal. A rhombus of oak posts laid out around a grave. A gold disc the size of a saucer with eight words on it. A canal cut across an island in 726, a rampart raised in 737, and a market laid out in regular plots on a west-coast river around 704. Those three dates are known to the year, from tree rings, and nobody wrote them down. The state that ordered them has no name, no king we can identify, and no chronicle.

It had the Danes, and it had a border, and it had ships. The Viking Age (8) is what it did with them.

Myth-check

"Norse mythology is a Viking Age religion."
Odin is named on a Danish gold bracteate from the early 400s — some four hundred years before the Viking Age, and eight hundred before the Icelandic texts most people read. The gods are much older than the longships.
"The Golden Horns are in the National Museum."
Copies are. The originals were stolen in 1802 and melted down within days. Everything known about them comes from drawings made beforehand — which is why the drawings, and Oehlenschläger's poem, matter as much as the objects did.
"The Angles and Jutes emptied Jutland."
Emigration to Britain certainly happened and was substantial. Whether it depopulated Jutland is disputed, and the 536 catastrophe is a better candidate for the settlement abandonment usually cited as evidence. The question has never been politically innocent.
"Denmark is named after the Danes in the sixth century."
The people are named in the sixth century, by Jordanes. The compound Danmark — the Danes' borderland — is not attested for another four hundred years.
"The Danevirke was built by Queen Thyra."
A medieval tradition, repeated for centuries and still on some signs. The tree-ring dates put major construction around 737, roughly two centuries before Thyra. She and Gorm belong to Jelling (10).

Summary

IF YOU REMEMBER FIVE THINGSThe page in five

  1. Rome's collapse sent gold north. The fifth and sixth centuries put more gold into the Danish ground than any period before or since, much of it as bracteates — stamped discs that began as copies of imperial medallions and became something else.

  2. The Vindelev hoard, found by a first-time detectorist in 2020, carries the world's oldest inscription naming Odin — pushing Norse mythology back to the early 400s and showing a ruler claiming a god's backing.

  3. In 536 a volcanic dust veil wrecked the northern summers, followed by plague in 541. Scandinavia suffered badly, settlements were abandoned, and the peak of gold deposition coincides exactly with the catastrophe.

  4. Angles, Saxons and Jutes crossed to Britain from this region, taking the ancestor of the English language with them. What that did to the population left behind is disputed.

  5. By the early 700s someone could order a canal cut across Samsø, a rampart built across the peninsula, and a town laid out at Ribe. A people called the Dani had been named two centuries earlier; now they had something that behaved like a state.

Causal

Why did that follow from this?

  • Why does the fall of an empire make a region beyond its border richer in gold, not poorer?
  • Why is a hall without a byre a political building rather than a domestic one?
  • Why would people bury gold during a catastrophe — and how would you tell an offering from a hidden savings deposit?
  • Why does a canal across Samsø imply a different kind of authority from a big hall at Lejre?

Counterfactual

Pull one thread and see what moves.

  • Suppose 536 had not happened. Does the consolidation that produces the Danevirke still occur, or does catastrophe do the work of centralisation?
  • Suppose Heidenreich had been caught a week earlier. How much less would the Golden Horns mean to Denmark today?

Contested

Where researchers actually disagree.

  • Did the migrations depopulate Jutland? Set out what evidence would settle it, and consider why the question attracted nationalist attention on both sides of the border.
  • Lejre's excavated halls match the legendary seat of the Skjoldungs and the Beowulf hall in place, scale and roughly in date. How much weight should archaeology give a poem, and what is the risk of digging where the story says to dig?
  • The Vindelev reading rests on a worn inscription in a language known mainly from short, formulaic texts. How confident should a museum be about a translation this consequential, and what would change if a rival reading prevailed?
  • Is "the gods took the horns back" — the Romantic reading — a distortion of the archaeology or a legitimate part of what the objects now mean? Can a nation's relationship to an object be wrong?
