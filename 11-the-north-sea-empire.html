The North Sea Empire

c. 1000 – 1066 · Svend Tveskæg · Knud den Store · Hardeknud

The largest state ever ruled from Denmark, and the shortest-lived. For nineteen years a Dane governed England, Denmark and Norway from Winchester — and within seven years of his death none of it was left.

Introduction

WHAT THIS PAGE ANSWERSFive questions

  1. How does a country of a few hundred thousand people conquer the richest kingdom in northern Europe?
  2. How much silver actually changed hands, and what did paying it do to England?
  3. Cnut ruled three kingdoms. Was he a Danish king of England or an English king of Denmark?
  4. Why did the empire collapse within seven years of his death?
  5. Two of the three claimants in 1066 were of Norse descent. Is 1066 the end of the Viking Age or its last act?

01 / NARRATIVEThe son who overthrew his father

Danish terms in this section

'Forked beard' — Sweyn's nickname, and the only physical detail we have of him.
danegæld
Danegeld: tribute paid by the English to buy off a Scandinavian fleet.

The household troop — now large, professional and permanent.

Chapter 10 ended with Sweyn Forkbeard driving his father into exile around 986. It was not a promising start, and the churchmen who wrote the history never forgave him for it — Adam of Bremen treats him as a pagan usurper punished by God.

What he actually was is the most effective Danish king before the modern era. He held Denmark for nearly thirty years. He beat the Norwegian king Olaf Tryggvason at the sea-battle of Svold around 1000 and took overlordship of Norway. He was the first Danish king to put his own name on a coin. And he took England.

His instrument was the fleet his father had built the infrastructure for. Chapter 10's ring fortresses were abandoned, but the organisation that produced them — the capacity to summon ships and men from every district — was not.

02 / NARRATIVEWhy England could be taken

England in 1000 was the wealthiest and most administratively sophisticated kingdom in northern Europe. It had a silver coinage of high purity, regularly recalled and reminted; a system of shires with royal officials; and a mechanism for assessing and collecting tax that no continental kingdom could match.

All of which made it worth attacking, and — perversely — easier to attack.

A kingdom that can raise enormous sums in silver can pay enormous sums in silver, and once it has paid once, everybody knows it. Æthelred II, on the throne from 978 as a child and unlucky in almost every decision he made, paid Danegeld repeatedly. The amounts recorded by the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle climb: ten thousand pounds, sixteen thousand, twenty-four thousand, thirty-six thousand — and in 1012, forty-eight thousand pounds of silver.

These are not small figures. Forty-eight thousand pounds is on the order of eleven million silver pennies.

Whether they are literal is one of the better-known arguments in the field. The numbers come from the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle — that is, from the people paying, writing up their own humiliation — and John Gillingham argued in the 1980s that sums of this size are simply too large for the English economy of the period to have produced, with M.K. Lawson defending them at length in reply. The exchange ran in the English Historical Review and was not settled. Chapter 8 taught the reader that a chronicler's "two hundred ships" may mean "a great many"; the same scepticism belongs here.

What is not in doubt is the direction, because that evidence is in the ground rather than on the page. Danish and Swedish hoards are full of English coins, and there is more Anglo-Saxon silver buried in Scandinavia than in England. Whether the totals were forty-eight thousand pounds or a fraction of it, an extraordinary quantity of English silver went north.

The coinage is worth a sentence of its own, because it is the clearest measure of what England was. English silver pennies of this period are struck to a controlled weight and high purity, at dozens of mints, from dies issued centrally — and the whole currency was periodically called in and reminted, which is a thing only a state with real reach can do. Danish kings noticed. Sweyn was the first to put his own name on a coin; Cnut's Danish coinage copies English design and in some cases was struck by moneyers with English names.

The strategic effect was to convert raiding from a gamble into a business, and then into a takeover. A fleet paid off in 1012 does not disperse; it is a standing professional force with a demonstrated income stream, and some of it took service with Æthelred, which meant England was paying Danish soldiers to protect it from Danes.

St Brice's Day, 13 November 1002

Æthelred, who would pay twenty-four thousand pounds that same year, tried a different approach. He ordered the killing of the Danes settled in England — on the reported grounds that they intended to kill him first.

What happened on 13 November 1002 is imperfectly recorded and was probably uneven in its execution, but it was real. At Oxford, a group who took refuge in St Frideswide's church were burned in it, an event Æthelred himself described in a charter two years later while explaining why the church needed rebuilding. Excavations at St John's College in 2008 uncovered a mass grave of around thirty-five young men, most with severe injuries, dated to the right period; the excavators suggested a connection.

Later English chroniclers report that among those killed was Gunhild, sister of the king of Denmark.

Whether or not that detail is true — it appears in sources written long afterwards — the consequence was immediate. Sweyn's campaigns from 1003 have a different quality from the raids that preceded them. They are sustained, annual, and aimed at conquest.

03 / NARRATIVEThe machine that carried them

Danish terms in this section

leding
The naval levy: the obligation on each coastal district to provide a ship, its crew and its provisions.
skipæn
A ship-district — the unit of land assessed to man one bench or one vessel.
thingmannalid
Þingalið: Cnut's paid standing troops in England, funded by an annual tax.
hærmand
A fighting man under obligation to serve, as distinct from a hired professional.

Before the conquest, the mechanism. An invasion of England is not a matter of enthusiasm; it is a matter of hulls, benches, food and the authority to demand all three.

The Danish answer was the leding. Its fully developed medieval form belongs to the Valdemar age (13), and how far back it reaches is genuinely disputed — but something like it must already have existed, because Sweyn and Cnut repeatedly put fleets of a hundred ships and more to sea. The principle is that the coastal districts of the realm are divided into units, each assessed to produce a ship with its crew, its weapons and its provisions for a season's campaign. A king who summons the leding is not hiring an army. He is calling in an obligation.

Alongside it sat the hird — the king's own household troops, professional, permanently on the payroll, and loyal to him personally rather than to a district. Chapter 10's ring fortresses were probably built to house exactly this sort of force.

What Cnut did in England was to make the second thing into an institution. Having sent the conquering fleet home in 1018, he kept a standing force of paid Scandinavian soldiers — the thingmannalid — supported by an annual tax and quartered in and around London. Eleventh-century Europe had almost nothing comparable: a permanent professional army paid in cash from a regular levy, in peacetime. It is one of the more remarkable administrative facts of the period, and it was possible only because he had inherited the best tax system in northern Europe.

Six weeks to Dublin

The practical parameters can now be measured rather than guessed, because a full-scale reconstruction of the Skuldelev 2 warship has been sailed. Havhingsten fra Glendalough made Roskilde to Dublin in about six weeks in 2007, and the return the following summer. Under favourable wind she has logged well over ten knots.

Set that against the campaigns. A fleet leaving Danish waters in spring could be off the English coast in a fortnight or less, fight a season, and be home before the autumn gales — or winter in place and start again in March. The distances that had constrained chapter 8's raiders were no longer the limiting factor. Silver was.

Skuldelev 2

Sometime around 1042, in Dublin, shipwrights built a longship about thirty metres long, of Irish oak, capable of carrying sixty to eighty men. It was a warship of the first class — the kind of vessel this entire part has been about.

It ended its life in Roskilde Fjord. Around 1070, five ships were deliberately sunk in the narrow channel at Skuldelev to block the approach to Roskilde against attack from the sea. Skuldelev 2 was one of them. They were raised in 1962, and the dendrochronology and timber analysis established where each was built.

Consider the arc. A Danish-ruled shipyard in Ireland builds a warship. It is sailed to Denmark. Thirty years later a Danish king fills it with stones and sinks it in a channel to stop other people doing to him what his grandfather's generation had spent three centuries doing to everyone else.

Skuldelev 2 · built Dublin c. 1042, scuttled Roskilde Fjord c. 1070

Why the throne kept breaking

One consequence of all this deserves flagging, because it explains a great deal of what follows in Part D.

When Sweyn died at Gainsborough in February 1014, the Danish army in England elected Cnut. It did not acclaim an heir; it chose, and it chose the younger son over the elder. Danish kingship in this period is not straightforwardly hereditary: a king needs to be accepted by the great men and by the fighting force, and both had views.

That is workable while there is a fleet to command and plunder to distribute. It becomes lethal when there is neither. Chapter 12 opens with Sweyn Estridsen's five sons succeeding one another and ends in a civil war that lasts a generation — and the root of it is here: a monarchy whose legitimacy came from military success, in a country that had stopped raiding.

04 / NARRATIVE1013

In the summer of 1013 Sweyn sailed for England with his son Cnut beside him, and this time he was not there for silver.

He landed at Sandwich, went north to the Humber, and took the submission of Northumbria, then the Danelaw, then the midlands. Oxford and Winchester submitted. London held out — London usually did — until it did not. By Christmas 1013, Æthelred had fled to Normandy and Sweyn was accepted as king of England.

He had ruled it for about five weeks when, on 3 February 1014, he died at Gainsborough. He was buried at York and later brought home to Roskilde.

What followed is a decade of ferocious instability. The Danish army in England elected Cnut, then in his early twenties; the English magnates recalled Æthelred; Cnut withdrew to Denmark, where his elder brother Harald was now king. Harald helped him fit out a fleet. In 1015 Cnut came back with something like a hundred and sixty ships.

Æthelred died in April 1016 and was succeeded by his son Edmund Ironside, who was a considerably better soldier than his father. Through the summer the two armies fought a series of engagements across southern England. On 18 October 1016, at Assandun in Essex, Cnut won decisively — helped, the chronicles say, by an English ealdorman changing sides at the critical moment.

Edmund and Cnut then divided England between them. Edmund died a few weeks later, and Cnut was king of all of it.

05 / NARRATIVECnut

Danish terms in this section

Earl: the deputy who governed a province in the king's absence.

Cnut was about twenty-six, had spent much of his life at war, and proceeded to govern England for nineteen years with a competence that surprised everyone including his own chroniclers.

The first act was the crucial one. In 1018 he collected an enormous tax — the sources say seventy-two thousand pounds from the country and ten and a half thousand more from London — and used it to pay off his army and send most of the fleet home. He kept forty ships. Having conquered England with a Viking host, he immediately dissolved it.

The second act was a marriage. In 1017 he married Emma of Normandy, the widow of the king he had displaced.

Emma

She is the most consequential woman in eleventh-century northern Europe and is usually reduced to a line in someone else's story.

A Norman duke's daughter, she had been married to Æthelred as part of a treaty in 1002, and had borne him sons — including the boy who would become Edward the Confessor. When Æthelred fled and died, she was in London. She then married the man who had conquered her husband's kingdom.

It is easy to read that as a woman disposed of. The evidence does not support it. The marriage gave Cnut continuity with the old dynasty and neutralised the Norman court that was sheltering Æthelred's heirs. It gave Emma a second reign, and she used it: she was a major landholder, a patron of churches, and effectively co-ruler in Cnut's long absences. She commissioned a book about her own life — the Encomium Emmae Reginae — which is the earliest surviving work of political self-justification by anyone in English history, and which quietly writes her first marriage out of the record.

Her son by Cnut, Harthacnut, ruled Denmark and then England. Her son by Æthelred, Edward, ruled England after him. Her great-nephew was William of Normandy. Every claimant in 1066 traces through her family or against it.

Emma of Normandy · queen of England twice, 1002–1052

Then Cnut inherited Denmark. His brother Harald died around 1018 or 1019 and Cnut took the throne after some resistance. In 1028 he drove Olaf Haraldsson out of Norway and took that kingdom too. He had a foothold in Sweden.

06 / NARRATIVERuling from Winchester

Here is the fact that unsettles the Danish national version of this story: Cnut was, in practice, an English king.

He lived mainly in England. He governed through English institutions — the shire system, the writ, the coinage — and he kept them, because they worked better than anything Denmark had. He issued law codes in English, drafted for him by Archbishop Wulfstan of York. He endowed English monasteries, including at Assandun where he had won, and made a great show of venerating English saints — notably Ælfheah, the archbishop of Canterbury whom a Danish army had murdered in 1012.

In 1027 he travelled to Rome for the imperial coronation of Conrad II, and sent home a letter to the people of England describing the journey: he had gone for the good of his soul and the good of his people, and had secured from the emperor and the pope better terms for English and Danish pilgrims and merchants going to Rome. It is the letter of a Christian European monarch, not a Viking warlord, and it was almost certainly meant to be read that way.

Denmark he governed largely through deputies, and one episode shows the strain. His brother-in-law Ulf Jarl, married to Cnut's sister Estrid, was governing Denmark and had had Cnut's son Harthacnut proclaimed king without Cnut's authority. Cnut had him killed — in the wooden church at Roskilde, at Christmas — and paid compensation to the church and to his own sister.

What Denmark got out of the empire is a real question, and the answer is mixed. English silver in quantity; English clergy, who staffed the new Danish church and brought Latin literacy with them; English coin design and moneyers; and a place in Latin Christendom. What it did not get was a capital, an administration, or a king who lived there.

Meanwhile in Europe

Cnut's contemporaries reckoned him one of the two great rulers of Latin Christendom, alongside the emperor. He attended an imperial coronation as an equal and married his daughter Gunhilda to the future emperor Henry III.

Elsewhere the eleventh century was setting up the next several hundred years: the Great Schism between Rome and Constantinople came in 1054, the Norman conquest of southern Italy was under way, and the reform movement that would produce the papacy's long struggle with emperors was beginning.

07 / NARRATIVESeven years, then nothing

Cnut died at Shaftesbury on 12 November 1035, aged about forty-five, and was buried at Winchester. The empire did not survive him by a decade.

The reason is structural, and it is worth stating plainly: the empire had no institutions. It was three kingdoms held together by one man's person, prestige and fleet. There was no shared law, no common administration, no capital, and — fatally — no rule of succession.

Three Scottish kings submitted to him in 1031, on the Chronicle's dating. Cnut had sons by two women. Harold Harefoot, by Ælfgifu of Northampton, took England. Harthacnut, by Emma, had Denmark and was stuck there fighting off Magnus the Good of Norway, which is why the English gave up waiting for him. Norway went its own way at once under Magnus.

Harold Harefoot died in 1040. Harthacnut crossed to England, had his half-brother's body dug up and thrown into a marsh, ruled for two unpopular years, and died at a wedding feast in 1042, aged about twenty-four.

And with that the Danish royal line in England simply ran out of men. The English recalled Emma's other son, Edward the Confessor, who had spent twenty-five years in exile in Normandy and brought Norman advisers, Norman tastes and Norman expectations back with him.

In Denmark, after a war with Magnus, the throne went to Sweyn Estridsen — son of the Ulf Jarl whom Cnut had murdered in Roskilde, and of Cnut's sister Estrid. He reigned for nearly thirty years, and every subsequent Danish monarch descends from him. The dynasty that rules Denmark today comes not from the conqueror but from the man he had killed.

08 / NARRATIVESeventeen years against Norway

Danish terms in this section

Nisåslaget
The battle of Niså, 1062 — the largest naval engagement of the northern eleventh century.
hærgning
Harrying: the systematic burning of a coast, used as a strategy rather than an outrage.
Hardråde
Harðráði, 'hard ruler' or 'hard counsel' — Harald of Norway's byname, and deserved.

Between Harthacnut's death in 1042 and the year 1066 lie twenty-four years that most English accounts skip, because nothing happens in England. In Denmark they are the whole story, and without them the next section makes no sense.

Harthacnut and Magnus the Good of Norway had agreed that whichever of them outlived the other would take both kingdoms. Harthacnut died first, so Magnus claimed Denmark — and made it stick, beating Sweyn Estridsen in the field more than once. Then Magnus died in 1047, and his uncle took Norway.

That uncle was Harald Sigurdsson, called Hardrada. He had fought at Stiklestad as a teenager, escaped to Kiev, commanded the Varangian Guard in Constantinople, campaigned in Sicily and the Holy Land, and come home rich enough to buy a kingdom. He is the most travelled man in this series and, by a distance, the most dangerous.

He spent the next seventeen years trying to take Denmark from Sweyn Estridsen, and the method was burning. Almost every summer from 1048 a Norwegian fleet came south and harried the Danish coasts — not to hold ground, but to make the country ungovernable and its king look powerless.

In 1050 he burned Hedeby. The sagas say he sent fire-ships into the harbour on the current, and the charred remains of vessels have been excavated from the Schlei. The largest town in Scandinavia, the town Godfred had stocked by force in 808 and Harald Bluetooth had walled in the tenth century, never recovered. Chapter 9 described its end; this is who ended it.

The war culminated at Niså, off the Halland coast, in 1062 — a fleet action of a scale the north would not see again, with both kings present and, on the saga account, several hundred ships engaged. Harald won the battle. Sweyn escaped, and kept his kingdom, which is the only measure that mattered.

Because that is the pattern of the whole seventeen years. Harald could beat Sweyn whenever he could catch him, and could never hold anything. Sweyn lost every major engagement and was still king at the end of it. In 1064 the two men made peace, each keeping what he had, with no indemnity on either side.

Two years later Harald Hardrada sailed for England.

Read the next section with that in mind. Hardrada is not a Norwegian adventurer arriving out of nowhere in September 1066; he is a man who has just spent seventeen years failing to conquer the kingdom next door and has turned to an easier one. And Sweyn Estridsen — who had a claim to England as good as anyone's, being Cnut's nephew — did not sail, because he had spent those same seventeen years being burned out every summer and had nothing left to sail with.

09 / NARRATIVE1066

Edward died childless in January 1066, and four people had a claim.

Harold Godwinson, the most powerful earl in England, was chosen by the English council. William of Normandy claimed a promise and a kinship through Emma. Harald Hardrada of Norway claimed through the agreement between Magnus and Harthacnut. And Sweyn Estridsen of Denmark had a claim as Cnut's nephew — which he asserted and never sailed on.

Hardrada came first, two years out of a war he had not won, with a large fleet and Harold's own exiled brother Tostig. He beat the northern earls outside York. Harold Godwinson force-marched his army from the south — something like four hundred kilometres in under a week — and destroyed the Norwegians at Stamford Bridge on 25 September. Hardrada was killed. It was, by any measure, one of the great feats of Anglo-Saxon generalship.

Three days later William landed in Sussex. Harold marched back and fought him at Hastings on 14 October, and lost, and died.

The temptation is to say that Hardrada cost Harold England, and there is something in it. But notice what actually happened: England was taken in 1066 by a Norman duke who was the great-great-great-grandson of a Viking granted land at the mouth of the Seine in chapter 8. The Scandinavian conquest of England succeeded twice — once directly under Sweyn and Cnut, and once at several removes through Normandy.

Danish claims did not stop in 1066. Sweyn Estridsen sent fleets to England in 1069 and 1070 in support of the northern rebellions against William, and there were further Danish attempts as late as 1085. None came to anything. But the idea that England was a Danish possession took a long time to die, and the last Danish king to plan an invasion of it did so twenty years after Hastings.

10 / NARRATIVEWhat the Viking Age left

Part C has covered three centuries. It is worth asking what actually changed.

Denmark became a kingdom in the European sense. In 750 it was a people with a name, a rampart and a canal. By 1066 it had a hereditary Christian monarchy, dioceses, coinage, towns, a written law tradition beginning, and a dynasty that still occupies the throne. That transformation is the substance of these four chapters, and it was largely paid for abroad.

The silver came home and became a state. The Danegeld, the eastern dirhams, the tolls — the capital that built the ring fortresses, the churches and the towns was extracted from other people's countries. Denmark's institutional foundation was funded by tribute and slaving.

The expansion stopped. After 1066 there are no more great western ventures. The reasons are partly that the targets hardened — England under the Normans was not England under Æthelred — and partly that Denmark's kings now had a different project, which was the Baltic. The Baltic crusades (13) go east instead.

Tóki, who went west

There are around two hundred and fifty runestones in Denmark, and most of them were raised in the century this chapter covers. They are the only substantial body of Danish writing from the Viking Age, and almost none of it is royal.

The formula is nearly always the same: X raised this stone in memory of Y, who was Z. What varies is the last part, and that is where the lives are. A stone at Hedeby records that King Sveinn set it up for his retainer Skarthi, who had gone west and then met his death at Hedeby — a man who survived the England campaigns and was killed at home. Another from the same town commemorates Erik, a comrade and a shipmaster, who died when fighting men lay around Hedeby. Elsewhere the stones name brothers, partners in trade, men who died in England, men who died in Sweden, and — repeatedly — men described simply as very good, or as the finest of landholders.

Around a tenth of Danish stones were raised by women, and a number commemorate them. One at Sønder Vissing was set up by a woman named Tófa, Mistivi's daughter, for her mother — and she identifies herself as the wife of Harald the Good, Gorm's son, which is how we know a Danish king married a Slavic prince's daughter.

These are the people Part C has otherwise not contained: retainers, shipmasters, wives, brothers. They are the men who rowed to England and the households that paid for it, and for a century around the year 1000 they had the habit of carving who they were into granite. Nobody higher up wrote a word about them.

Danish runestones · c. 950–1050

The English inheritance is permanent. Several hundred Norse place names, the ridings of Yorkshire, and a layer of the commonest words in the English language. Chapter 8 listed some; here is the point about them. They, them, their — English replaced its own third-person plural pronouns with Norse ones. That happens only through intimate, prolonged, everyday contact between populations. The Danelaw was not an occupation; it was a place where people lived together long enough to borrow each other's grammar.

Meanwhile in the world

The Song dynasty was inventing the compass for navigation, movable type and paper currency. The Seljuk Turks took Baghdad in 1055 and defeated Byzantium at Manzikert in 1071, setting up the First Crusade within a generation. In West Africa the empire of Ghana was at its height on the gold trade.

And in the North Atlantic the Norse colony in Greenland was two generations old and the Vinland voyages had been abandoned. The Viking expansion reached North America and could not hold it — for the same reason the North Sea Empire failed. Reach without institutions does not last.

And the last thing Part C leaves is a question that the next four chapters answer badly.

For three hundred years Danish kings had funded themselves abroad. Tribute, plunder, tolls on other people's cargo, the sale of other people's people — the ring fortresses, the Jelling monuments, the first churches and the first coins were all paid for with silver that came from somewhere else. It worked because the ships were better than anyone else's and because the targets were soft.

By 1066 neither is true. Norman England is not Æthelred's England. The eastern silver has stopped. Hedeby is a burnt site with grass over it, and the man who burnt it has just died in Yorkshire. Sweyn Estridsen is on the Danish throne, and he will reign for nearly thirty years without a single successful foreign venture.

So the money has to come from home. That means land, and tithes, and a church that can count, and written law, and men who can administer a district on the king's behalf without being able to overthrow him. Every one of those is a Part D problem, and Sweyn Estridsen's five sons will spend their reigns on it and mostly fail.

The Viking Age does not end because the Vikings were defeated. It ends because a kingdom that had lived on the proceeds of other countries had to learn to live on its own — and that turned out to be much harder than sailing to England.

Myth-check

"Cnut was a Danish king who also happened to rule England."
He lived mainly in England, governed through English institutions, issued English law codes, endowed English churches and was buried at Winchester — so the usual Danish framing is at best half of it. How much attention the Scandinavian half of the empire actually received is a live argument, and Timothy Bolton has made the case that it was governed far more closely than the older literature allowed. A king who has his brother-in-law killed in a Roskilde church over an unauthorised proclamation is not a king ignoring Denmark.
"Danegeld was extortion that achieved nothing."
It achieved a great deal, in the wrong direction. It transferred an extraordinary quantity of silver from the best-run treasury in northern Europe to Scandinavia, where it funded churches, towns and state-building — and it demonstrated that England could pay, which turned raiding into conquest.
"Cnut tried to command the tide."
The story is from Henry of Huntingdon, a century later, and the point of it is the opposite of how it is usually told: Cnut ordered the sea back in order to demonstrate to his flattering courtiers that a king's power is nothing beside God's. He was making a show of humility, not arrogance.
"1066 ended the Viking Age."
It is a reasonable marker, but note that both invaders in 1066 were of Norse descent, and that Danish kings launched fleets at England in 1069, 1070 and as late as 1085. The Norman conquest was in one sense a Scandinavian conquest at five generations' remove.
"The Danish royal line descends from Cnut."
It descends from Sweyn Estridsen — son of Estrid, and of the Ulf Jarl whom Cnut had murdered in a church at Roskilde. Cnut's own line was extinct within seven years of his death.

IF YOU REMEMBER FIVE THINGSThe page in five

  1. Danegeld payments climbing to 48,000 pounds of silver in 1012 turned raiding into a business and then into conquest. There is more Anglo-Saxon silver in Scandinavian ground than in English.

  2. Sweyn Forkbeard took all England in a few months in 1013 and died five weeks after being accepted as king. His son Cnut won it back at Assandun in 1016.

  3. Cnut ruled England, Denmark and Norway together from 1028 to 1035 — seven years — mostly from Winchester, through English institutions, with Emma of Normandy as queen.

  4. The empire had no shared law, no capital and no succession rule. It dissolved within seven years of Cnut's death, and the Danish throne passed to Sweyn Estridsen, from whom every later Danish monarch descends.

  5. In 1066 both invaders of England were of Scandinavian descent, and Danish fleets kept sailing until 1085. The Viking Age ends less as an event than as a change of subject.

Questions & discussion

FOUR KINDSWork the material

Recall

Did the facts land?

  • What was Danegeld, and roughly how large did the payments get?
  • What happened on 13 November 1002, and what did it change?
  • What did Cnut do with his army in 1018, and why is it significant?
  • Who was Emma of Normandy, and how many claimants of 1066 connect to her?
  • Which Danish king's descendants sit on the throne today, and what did Cnut do to his father?

Causal

Why did that follow from this?

  • Why did England's administrative sophistication make it more vulnerable rather than less?
  • Why would a conqueror dissolve the army that won him the kingdom?
  • Why did the North Sea Empire dissolve so completely when the Norman conquest of England lasted?
  • Why does English borrow its third-person plural pronouns from Norse, and what does that tell you that place names do not?

Counterfactual

Pull one thread and see what moves.

  • Suppose Cnut had lived another twenty years and left one adult heir. Does a North Sea state become permanent — and what happens to 1066?
  • Suppose Harald Hardrada had not invaded in September 1066. Does Harold beat William, and does that make England a Scandinavian-facing kingdom for centuries?

Contested

Where historians actually disagree.

  • Was Cnut a great ruler or a lucky one? He inherited his father's conquest, and his empire outlived him by seven years. What would count as evidence either way?
  • Denmark's state formation was funded by tribute extracted under threat and by trade in enslaved people. Does that alter how the achievement should be described — and does the same question apply to the slave trade (27)?
  • Emma of Normandy commissioned a book about herself that omits her first marriage. How should historians use a source written to order by its subject — and is it worse than a chronicle written by a monk with a patron?
  • Is 1066 a real boundary for Danish history, or a date imported from English historiography because it is convenient?
