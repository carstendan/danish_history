# 04 · Bronze Age: amber, sun and the long road south — study page

Page: `04-bronze-age-amber-sun-and-the-long-road-south.html` · learning pass, session 4 (D-21)

## Checkpoints

After §02:

1. What happened to Denmark when the eastern Mediterranean world collapsed around 1,200 BCE?

   > Denmark got richer, but the trade / contacts shifted towards central europe, and away from
   > the Mediterranean

   *Against the page (§02, Meanwhile in the world):* right, and both halves are there. Missing: the
   first thing the page says, that Denmark "does not collapse". At "the far end of the supply
   chain" it was cushioned by the relays: it never depended on the palaces directly.

2. What did Danish smiths do with the metal that came north, and how can their work be recognised?

   > they melted and recast it in the local nordic style.

   *Against the page (§02):* right on what they did. Missing: the second half of the question, how
   it is recognised. The style is spirals, "above all, endlessly and confidently", and "objects made
   here are identifiable anywhere". The metal came both as ingots and as finished objects.

After §04:

3. Roughly how many burial mounds were built in the Early Bronze Age, and how many from the Single
   Grave culture on?
   > 50.000, from single grave culture more than 100.000

   *Against the page (§03):* right on both. Missing: that 50,000 is a minimum, and one of two
   figures. The heritage agency's panels say "at least twenty thousand". The 50,000 comes from
   Holst et al. 2013 counting the national register. The more than 100,000 covers 2,800–1,100 BCE.

4. What did the sprig of yarrow in the Egtved coffin tell us?

   > That the burial was in summer, as it flowers in summer.

   *Against the page (§04):* right. Missing: what the season adds to. The dendrochronology of the
   coffin gives the year, 1,370 BCE, so the flower narrows the funeral to a season of a known year.
   lex.dk takes the felling year as the burial year.

After §09:

5. How does the sun chariot work, and why are there two faces to the disc?
   > Gilded face and plain face. Sun cross the day left to right, pulled by a horse. At night
   > travel back through the underworld, right to left. Symbol of how life is.

   *Against the page (§05):* right on the mechanics: pulled one way, the gilded sun travels right
   across the day sky, and pulled back, the dark face travels left through the underworld. One
   thing to correct: the page calls it "a model of how the universe works, and you operate it by
   hand", which is cosmology rather than life. Missing: what tells the faces apart besides the
   gold. Only the gilded face has the rayed rim. And the chariot runs on six wheels so that it can
   be pulled.

6. Why does almost everything we have from the Late Bronze Age come from bogs?

   > The only that survived was from the bogs, covered with water.

   *Against the page (§09):* wrong on the reason. It is selection, not preservation. Bronze
   survives in dry ground too. What does not survive is bronze in circulation, because "a broken
   sword is raw material" and gets recycled. Only metal "deliberately taken out of circulation and
   given to the water" escaped the melting pot. Missing, and it follows: the picture is "of what
   people thought worth sacrificing, which is not at all the same as … what they owned". (The
   water-seals-it reading belongs to the oak coffins, §03: iron pan and waterlogging.) Cremation
   from c. 1,100 BCE is why the bodies drop out.

## Work the material

### From the checkpoints: what the hoards measure *(settled 26 October)*

From checkpoint 6, 23 October. Claude: if the bogs hold what people chose to give, what does that
do to claims about wealth? Carsten: "in itself not much. But you can make inference from what people
chose to give." Right: a deposit is a floor, since you can only give up what you can spare. Claude's
point: the page's own wealth claim is a deposit measure. §01 says "by volume of metal deposited, one
of the richest in Europe", and the Myth-check says "More bronze was deposited here". Question put:
does a high deposit count show wealth, or a religion that demanded more of it?
Carsten, 26 October: "The society was rich in-so-far they were able to make the deposits. So they
were able to trade their way to it, but they did not keep it in society as increased wealth. So
yea, the religion took it." Settled: wealth passed through, and the deposits measure the flow, not
the stock. Claude's one sharpening, from the page: giving it away was itself social. §11's
aristocracy rested "on access to imported bronze", and its religion's central image "was carried
on objects made from it". Destroying bronze in public is how an elite shows it has access, so
"the religion took it" and "the elite spent it" may be the same act. Left as the page has it: the
Myth-check's "it was rich" rests on deposits, and the page says so ("by volume of metal
deposited"); an argument, not an error. §01's "per head" is separately on To research.

### The page's end questions: Recall

- *Which two metals make bronze, and where did each come from?* *(settled 26 October; Carsten:
  "Cornwall gave tin", so copper from the Alps and the Carpathians)* Carsten:
  "Tin and Copper. From england, brenner and carpathian." Right on the metals (§01: about nine
  parts copper to one of tin) and the places are in the right regions. Missing: which metal came
  from where. Per §02, copper came from the Alps and the Carpathians/Slovakia, "later from further
  afield", and tin from Cornwall. "Brenner" is not on the page; it is a pass in the Alps, so it
  falls under "the Alps". Asked back: which metal is the English one?
- *Who lay in the three oak coffins at Borum Eshøj?* *(open, 26 October)* Carsten: "a man 50-60, a
  woman of same age and a young man in 20s." Right (§04). Missing: what the page makes of them, "A
  family group, or presented as one", and the details that carry it: the old man's trimmed nails
  and shaven face, the woman's hairnet. Asked back: why would the page hedge "or presented as
  one"?
  Carsten: "DNA testing?" Right for kinship. Claude added a second test: were they buried at the
  same time? A family "presented as one" might be three burials of different dates in one mound.
  The page is silent on both. Hook offered: the agent's lead (SLKS panel, not re-read) that the
  young man "var gravlagt senere", and whether any DNA work has been done. Carsten declined for
  now ("move on to the lurer"); settled as far as the page goes, the hook left open.
- *What are lurer, and how are they usually found?* *(settled 26 October)* Carsten: "bronze horns,
  curved and long. found in bogs and in pairs." Right (§06). Missing: that the pairs are "mirror
  images of one another", cast in sections by lost wax, and that many still play. Asked back: what
  does a mirrored pair say about how they were used? Carsten: "most likely played together."
  Right. The page takes it one step on: "Two horns, tuned, played together, by two people standing
  in the open", which is public ceremony, and a society with "the surplus to pay for it".
- *What did wear analysis show about the swords?* *(settled 26 October)* Carsten: "both. Some were made
  for offerings, some were heavy used and battleworn." Right on "both" (§07). One shift: the page's
  other half is display, not offering. The most elaborate fuldgrebssværd "look like objects meant to
  be seen at a distance by people who were not going to be allowed to touch them". Missing: where
  the wear is, "not on every sword, and not on the finest". Asked back: what does it tell you that
  the finest swords are the least used?
  Carsten: "Those on top of society, the wealthy, were not those who did the fighting." Claude
  pushed back: one reading, not the only one, and it goes past the page. The wear shows which
  *swords* fought, not which *people*. The same man could own a working sword and a parade sword.
  §07 itself mentions Kristiansen's "warrior elites". Asked back: what evidence would tell the two
  readings apart? Carsten: "dunno". Claude: the graves. A rich grave holding a worn sword, or a
  rich man's skeleton with healed blade wounds, would point to fighting elites. Rich graves with
  only pristine swords, and the worn ones in poorer graves, would point to Carsten's reading. The
  page gives neither, so the thread is settled as open on the page's evidence (26 October).
- *What changes about burial around 1,100 BCE, and why does it matter?* *(settled 26 October)*
  Carsten: "They started to burn bodies, which makes it harder to investigate. bodies, clothing,
  hair is no longer there for analysts." Right (§09). Missing: what happens to the mounds. Urns go
  "into the flank of an existing mound rather than under a new one", sometimes with miniature
  grave goods, and "the great mound-building stops". And what replaces the evidence: "we go back to
  having metalwork", from the hoards of checkpoint 6.

### The page's end questions: Causal

- *On Kaul's reading, why are ships the commonest image?* *(settled 26 October)* Carsten: "It is his
  interpretation of the sun chariot, of how the sun travels." Right that it is about the sun's
  journey, but the answer is missing: what the ship does in it. In the evening the sun "is received
  by a ship and carried down", and the page in five says "a ship carries it back through the night"
  (§05). So ships are religious images, not only boats. One correction: Kaul reads it from "engraved
  razors and rock carvings" (Figure 3). The chariot shows only the horse. Missing too: the page's
  plain alternative, that "many of the carvings lie close to the sea". Asked back: if the ship
  carries the sun for half its round and the horse for the other half, why would ships outnumber
  horses? Carsten: "Dunno. Have not noticed that in the text." **Claude conceded:** the question
  overreached. The page says ships are the commonest image but gives no ship-to-horse count and
  no account of why ships would outnumber horses within Kaul's model. §05 even has the night leg
  "helped by a fish or a snake". Settled at what the page gives: on Kaul's reading ships are
  common because the ship is part of the sun's daily round, plus the plain reason of the sea
  (26 October).
- *What sealed the core of some mounds, and why did that preserve what was inside?* *(settled 26
  October)* Carsten: "iron leached down and settled into a hard crust." Right on the first half
  (§03: water through the turf leached iron down into *al*, iron pan). Asked back for the second
  half. Carsten: "it is not exposed to oxygen, staying waterlogged." Right: "oxygen was excluded,
  the contents stayed permanently waterlogged, and organic material did not rot". Worth carrying:
  the page calls it something "the builders could not have intended", and it is why §04 says it
  worked only "in those where the iron pan sealed properly".
- *Why was textile production large, and why is it barely visible?* *(settled 26 October)* Carsten:
  "There was a lot of sheep, and textile work was done by women." Right ingredients (§08), but the
  links are missing. Large: not the sheep but the labour. Wool became "the standard clothing
  material", and "the Egtved girl's outfit alone represents a great deal of spinning", a household
  industry at scale. One hedge to keep: the page says "overwhelmingly likely to have been women's
  work", not that it was. Asked back: why would women's work be less visible in this record than
  men's?
  Carsten: "My first reaction is that it is how it have been and still is - women are just less
  recognized." Claude pushed back: here the main cause is material, not recognition. Cloth rots;
  bronze survives. What we have is metal given to bogs (§09) and the few sealed coffins (§03–04).
  This chapter is in fact unusually full of women: Egtved, Skrydstrup, the belt disc as "the
  defining female object". Asked back: in which of the chapter's kinds of evidence can cloth
  survive at all? Carsten: "in the graves, that got sealed by leached iron." Right. Settled: one of
  the period's largest industries shows up only where an accident sealed a coffin, and only until
  cremation from c. 1,100 BCE ends even that. Hence "least visible" (§08).
- *Why would iron undermine an elite that had done well out of bronze?* *(settled 26 October)*
  Carsten: "because iron is a superior metal that makes bronze superflous ?" Not the page's
  reason. §11 makes it about access, not quality: "Copper and tin are rare, and they made Denmark
  dependent. Iron is not rare." It can be got from Danish bog ore "by anybody with fuel and
  patience", and the elite "had defined itself by controlling access to imported metal". Asked
  back: so what was the elite actually controlling, and what happens to that once the metal is
  under the meadow? Carsten: "they were controlling access, and when useful metal is everywhere
  there is no control." Right. Settled 26 October. Worth carrying, from §11: it was not only the
  elite. The religion's central image was "carried on objects made from" bronze, and the mounds
  stood along the trade routes, so "all of that lost its footing at once". And iron is one of two
  causes the page gives; the other is the climate turning "cooler and wetter".

All four Causal questions answered 26 October.

### The page's end questions: Counterfactual

- *Amber as plentiful on every North Sea coast?* *(settled 26 October)* Carsten: "Much less rich. The
  amber would not be as valuable for trade." Right: scarcity is the value. Claude tied it to the
  page's own formula (§01): Denmark has "position, one or two things nobody else has much of, and
  the willingness to trade". Amber is the "nobody else has much of". Asked back: take it away, and
  is position alone enough here, for a country at "the far end of the supply chain"?
  Carsten: "at that time, the position was not as valuable as it would be later with the sound
  due. So no, not enough." Right, and the page itself names the Sound as the later case (§01;
  carry-forward to Part E). Settled 26 October. It raised W1 (below).
- *Suppose Peter Platz had emptied the coffin in the field?* *(settled 26 October)* Carsten: "The
  Egtved girl / woman would not have had this central position. Later other graves were found, and
  more information would still have been excavated. But the original would have been different."
  Claude, from the page: one fact to correct. Most of the comparable graves came *before* 1921:
  Trindhøj 1861, Borum Eshøj 1871, Muldbjerg 1883. Only Skrydstrup (1935) came after (§04). And the
  answer is too general. The page's point is what lifting it whole saved: contents "recorded in
  place". Asked back: name something on this page that depends on her being excavated slowly
  indoors. Carsten: "The skirt." Right, and it is the best example: free-hanging cords "from a
  woven waistband" would come out of a field dig as loose wool, not as a garment. Asked back:
  what in §10 also depends on it? Carsten: "The whole discussion of where she grew up - which is
  still not known for certain." Right. The study used her "teeth, hair, nails and wool", all of
  them kept because the coffin went to Copenhagen whole. Worth carrying: Platz's choice in 1921
  saved evidence for a method that did not exist until nearly a century later. Settled 26 October.

### The page's end questions: Contested

- *The strontium dispute: what would settle it, and why has publishing not?* *(settled 26 October)*
  Carsten: "Proper baseline maps of strontium levels, but we do not have that." Right on what
  (§10: provenance "depends on baseline maps whose reliability is itself a live research
  question"). The second half is missing. Publishing has not settled it because the two sides
  disagree about what a proper baseline is. Thomsen and Andreasen sample unfarmed ground. Frei's
  side argues lime strontium stays in the topsoil, so farmed water is fine. Each paper measures
  inside its own assumption. Asked back: today's land has been limed for a century. What could a
  Bronze Age baseline be measured from instead? Carsten: "dunno". Claude: from things that lived
  on that land before any liming, such as the teeth and bones of prehistoric animals that did not
  travel, or of people known to be local. The page is silent on this. Hook offered: a February 2026
  *PLOS ONE* paper by K.M. Frei et al. builds a baseline for Bronze Age Denmark from human remains
  (agent lead, not re-read). Settled at the page's level, 26 October.
- *The folding stool: travelled or invented twice?* *(settled 26 October)* Carsten: "I would guess the
  idea travelled, as i don't believe much in coincidences. But some trace of material that had
  moved from Egypt to Denmark or vice versa." Claude: two points, one now. A folding stool is a
  simple mechanism, so two inventions would not need much coincidence. And on the page's own
  model, goods moved "in short relays from community to community" (Figure 1). An idea could
  arrive without any object travelling the whole way, so a direct Egypt–Denmark trace may never
  exist even if it travelled. Asked back: what would you expect to find between Egypt and Jutland
  if the idea travelled? Carsten: "connection points. Places of trade." Right direction. Claude
  sharpened it into the test: folding stools at those places, older the further south you go, a
  chain in time and space. If northern stools appear with no chain and no earlier date, two
  inventions gain ground. For the record, the National Museum's own view is that they "appeared
  in northern Europe" around 1400 BC and "reflect contacts" with the eastern Mediterranean (*The
  chair as status symbol*, re-read 20 October). Settled 26 October.
- *How should a museum handle a live dispute over its headline object?* *(settled 26 October)*
  Carsten: "To showcase an impartial variant of each argument. Easier said than done though."
  Right, and the page does that itself: "The honest position for this page is that we do not
  know" (§10), and the Myth-check says "'We don't know' is the accurate answer". Claude's point on
  why it is hard: here the museum is not only the exhibitor but a party. Its own researcher led
  the 2015 study, and it "responded within days, maintaining its position" (§10). Asked back: is a
  museum defending its own research a conflict of interest, or just normal science? Carsten:
  "That is tricky, and i cannot answer that generally. It is natural to defend the position.
  Question is, is it honest and transparent?" Settled 26 October on Carsten's framing: defending
  is normal, and the test is transparency. Claude made it concrete: does the museum's own text on
  the Egtved girl tell visitors the origin is disputed? The page does not say. Hook offered:
  check the museum's current online text.
- *Competing chiefdoms: what is the model built on, and how far does the evidence carry it?*
  *(settled 26 October)* Carsten: "You have a few that are rich, so there is inequality. And there
  graves point to hereditary. And with fighting, you then conclude competing chiefdoms. But there
  is no text, name or anything concrete." Right on the chain and on its limit (§07: "no texts, no
  names, no boundaries"). Missing: the geography, centres "each controlling a stretch of coast and
  a share of the flow of metal", with Voldtofte as the example (§07, §09). Claude raised the
  weakest link: "hereditary". §07 says the material shows "marked and hereditary inequality", but
  gives no evidence for the *hereditary* part. Asked back: what would a grave need to show for
  inequality to be inherited rather than earned? Carsten: "dunno". Claude: children buried
  richly, with wealth they were too young to have earned, or rich graves in one kin line across
  generations (DNA). The page offers neither. Settled 26 October. "Hereditary" goes to To research
  (a framing sentence is a claim).

All four Contested questions answered 26 October. **The end questions are done.**
