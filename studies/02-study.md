# 02 · Coast and forest: the hunter Stone Age — study page

Page: `02-coast-and-forest-the-hunter-stone-age.html` · learning pass, session 2 (D-21)

## Checkpoints

After §03:

1. What is a mikrolit, and why is an edge of several of them better than one large point?

   > A small 1-2 cm sharpened piece of flint, made to be placed in recesses on an arrow. Makes a
   > stable and deadly arrow for the individual prey that were hunted now.

   *Against the page (§02):* right on what it is, and right that it goes with dispersed forest game.
   Missing: the page's *why* is economic, not ballistic. One big point breaks and is lost whole; a
   composite edge of several mikrolitter set in resin has parts that are "individually cheap", so a
   broken one is replaced, not the whole point. That is the "different economic logic".

2. What drowned Doggerland — the Storegga tsunami, or something slower?

   > Doggerland was slowly drowned by rising water levels from melting ice. By the time of Storegga
   > it was already under water.

   *Against the page (§03, Figure 1):* right. Missing: the scope of "already". The page says
   *largely* under water. Around 7,000 BCE the Dogger Bank still stood as an island, and the wave
   of c. 6,200 BCE struck what was left. Walker et al. 2020 has the island gone only c. 5,000 BCE.
   The wave was real, and in recent modelling hit western Jutland hard. It was not the cause.

After §05:

3. What is a køkkenmødding, and why does bone survive in one?

   > A køkkenmødding is waste from a household that stays in one place. It overwhelmingly consists
   > of seashells, which is full of calcium which in turn alters the pH value of the soil and help
   > maintain the good condition of whatever is buried there.

   *Against the page (§05):* right on the mechanism. Two things to tighten. (a) Not one household:
   the Ertebølle midden, 140 m long, built up over roughly a thousand years of *repeated*
   occupation (§05; Slots- og Kulturstyrelsen). (b) The direction of the pH change and what it
   saves: calcium carbonate keeps the soil *alkaline*, and it is bone in particular that survives,
   where acidic Danish ground dissolves it everywhere else. Hence the page's line: the middens "are
   the reason we have evidence".

4. What did Ertebølle people make that a non-farming society is not supposed to have?

   > Ertebølle people made pottery, which goes against the old concept of when you change from
   > hunter-gatherer to farming, you became richer and started pottery. Ertebølle was doing quite
   > fine already.

   *Against the page (§05):* right. Missing: where the pottery came from. It was not borrowed from
   the farmers to the south; its "primary roots" lie with *forager* potters in the south-eastern
   Baltic (Povlsen 2013, §05 "Meanwhile in Europe"), and Jōmon and Chinese pottery are older still,
   also made by hunter-gatherers (§05 "Meanwhile in the world"). So it is not an early borrowing
   of a farming trait; the link between pottery and farming was never there. (The page also counts
   textiles by nålebinding, fixed weirs and ten-metre logboats on the same side of the ledger.)

After §09:

5. What was found in the grave of the eighteen-year-old at Bøgebakken?

   > An infant. Her child. Both the woman and the child died in the birth.

   *Against the page (§06):* right, at the page's own confidence ("almost certainly died in
   childbirth"; the infant "born or unborn at eight or nine months"). Missing: what the infant lay
   on, the swan's wing, which is the detail the page builds its point on: "A swan's wing for an
   infant who may never have drawn breath is not a subsistence strategy." (Also in the grave: a
   flint blade at the baby's waist, read as marking a boy, and tooth beads under her head, read as
   a pillow or, by Peter Vang Petersen, a baby-carrier.)

6. What did Lola's chewing gum turn out to contain, and what did she look like?

   > She was chewing birch pitch. She was dark skinned. dark hair blue eyes.

   *Against the page (§09):* right on the material and on her looks. Missing: the *contents*, which
   is what the question asks: a **complete human genome**, read from something she chewed rather
   than from a body. With it came her oral microbiome (including Epstein–Barr virus) and traces of
   her meal, mallard and hazelnut; and from the genome, that she was lactose intolerant (Jensen et
   al. 2019).

## Work the material

### The page's end questions: Recall (9 October 2026)

- *The three cultures in order, with dates.* Carsten: Maglemose, Kongemose, Ertebølle. Order
  right; dates to carry: Maglemose c. 9,000–6,400 BCE (lex.dk ends it c. 6,800), Kongemose
  c. 6,400–5,400, Ertebølle c. 5,400–3,950 (§02, §04, §05, Figure 3).
- *Tybrind Vig 1.* Carsten: 10 m, birch, sides about 5 cm, very smooth. Length right (the page's
  "about ten metres"; Andersen 1985: 9.5 m × 0.65 m). **Lime, not birch** (§05; Andersen 1985;
  birch is the *pitch*, §09, which may be where it crossed over). Sides about **2 cm**, 0.02–0.03 m
  in Andersen 1985, "consistently, along ten metres".
- *Ringkloster.* Carsten: dolphin; it could not get there itself, so someone carried it. Right:
  three bones of bottlenose dolphin (Andersen 1994–95), at a site 14–18 km from the east Jutland
  fjords (§05). Open on the page: one group's winter station or a camp shared by several coastal
  groups. Either way coast and interior are one economy, moved between by water.
- *Gøngehusvej double grave.* Carsten: a woman of 43 and her child; she had survived a severe
  blow to the head. Right on the healed blow. The page says "about forty" and a three-year-old
  child; it does not say the child was hers. Missing: the grave goods, which are the point of the
  grave (§06): a bone pin and a grebe's bill by her head, probably a cap of bird skin; on her chest
  two bone netting needles and roe-deer foot bones, from a hide wrapped round her.
  - *The netting needles* (settled 9 October). Carsten: one instance, but given what is known of human
    societies, women would primarily have made and mended the nets. Claude: the hedge is right; the
    warrant is the weak part. "What is known about human civilization" is later and ethnographic
    pattern read back into her; what the grave itself supports is narrower — net-making was tied to
    her, at least by those who buried her. Same move the Contested question on Bøgebakken asks
    about (how much of a reading comes from the evidence, how much from us). Question put: would a
    man's grave with netting needles have made us say men made the nets? Carsten: no — some
    researchers would have read gift or token; gender bias shapes how history is read. Settled:
    the bias runs both ways, and the first answer ("women would make the nets") was the same move
    from the other side. What the grave carries is the association, not the division of labour.
    Hook offered, not yet taken: Vedbæk's graves show a pattern in grave goods by sex.
- *Amber figures and coasts.* Carsten: bird and elk; Danish coasts and the Baltic coast. Right on
  the animals (the bird "possibly a waterbird"; from Engesvang, Ertebølle work, with small amber
  animals already carved in the Maglemose, §07). Narrower on the coasts: the page says the *west
  Jutland* beaches and the *eastern* Baltic, "in quantity only" there — which is what later makes
  it a trade good (→ Mycenae, chapter 4).

### The page's end questions: Causal (9 October 2026)

- *Fannerup boar* (settled 9 October; one question onward). Carsten: the bones show a diet they would not get roaming free.
  Right in kind. Specific (§05; Maring & Riede 2019): an *isotope* signal of substantial *marine*
  food in half the boar sampled (four of eight, Fannerup F, 5290–4335 cal BC); "wild boar do not
  fish". The page gives two readings: people fed them, "or at least" let them at the food waste.
  Question put: which is the weaker claim, and does it still count as "animal management"?
  Carsten: the waste is weaker — free animals visiting the heaps, not chased away, is not
  management; active feeding shows intent (and "a willingness to share"). Claude, two points:
  (1) feeding an animal you mean to eat is investment, not sharing; (2) tolerance at the refuse
  heap is itself where domestication theory puts the first step — the "commensal pathway", in
  which the animal comes to people's waste rather than being recruited (Dartmouth, on Wang et al.,
  PNAS 2025, South China:
  https://anthropology.dartmouth.edu/news/2025/06/pigs-were-domesticated-wild-boars-south-china).
  Question put: on that reading, is the page's "a form of animal management" for both readings
  defensible after all? Carsten: defensible — weak, but a step in a transformation; only a step.
  **Settled:** the page's wording stands; not an error. Onward (§11; Krause-Kyora et al. 2013): the
  step did not run its course here. The domestic pigs that do appear in Ertebølle contexts
  (Rosenhof, Poel) carry Near Eastern descent, from the farmers' herds, not from local boar.
  Question put: what does that say about the Fannerup step? Carsten: we do not know enough —
  feeding or scavenging is itself unsettled, so this over-concludes on scarce evidence. **Agreed;
  Claude's question overreached.** Ends here: four boar at one site show marine food; how they got
  it, and whether it led anywhere, is open. The imported pigs of §11 do not bear on it either way.
- *Fish weir and bow* (settled 9 October; led to §10). Question put: why does a fixed weir imply a different society from a
  hunting bow? Carsten: mobile vs immobile; a static society is fundamentally different. Right,
  but the page's mechanism is missing (§05): the weir is "a serious capital investment; you build
  it where you intend to be next year" — labour spent now for a return later, at one spot. The bow
  goes with you; the weir ties you to a place. Question put: what does the weir-builder now have
  that the bow-hunter does not, and what follows from it? Carsten: an investment expecting a
  return, a commitment to a location, and an interest in protecting it. **Settled**, and it is the
  page's own §10 argument: fixed resources (weir, oyster bed, fjord narrows) are "something to
  fight over"; "Sedentism and violence arrive together".
- *"Sedentism and violence arrive together"* (§10; open). Question put: the page's evidence is
  Bøgebakken's bone point, Gøngehusvej's healed blow and Skateholm. Before the Ertebølle we have
  almost no bodies (one cremation at Hammelev, §02), and bone survives where middens and
  cemeteries keep it (checkpoint 3). Can the record show violence *arriving* with sedentism, or
  only that we first see bodies when people settle? Carsten: the timing gives an indication, a
  guess with other factors, but it is not evidence; no clear causality. **Agreed.** The page's
  argument up to that sentence is hedged ("the record suggests they sometimes did"); the sentence
  itself states as fact a sequence the bones cannot show. *Candidate finding, not yet one:* the
  page cites no source for it. Read: Fibiger, Ahlström, Meyer & Smith, "Conflict, violence, and
  warfare among early farmers in Northwestern Europe", PNAS 120(4) 2023,
  https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9942812 — does not treat Vedbæk or Skateholm; frames
  the Mesolithic as "mobile or semi-mobile"; from Estabrook's 77 Mesolithic violent injuries over
  c. 2,000 individuals, "a crude percentage rate of 3.85% overall and 2.3% for cranial injuries",
  against Neolithic cranial trauma of 16.9% in Denmark; sees "a genuine increase … following
  domestication, notwithstanding issues of preservation". It neither supports nor refutes the
  page's Ertebølle claim; one search is not "no source found" (item 162). Next: Claude searches
  for a source on Ertebølle/Skateholm violence and sedentism before calling it an error.
- *Salt water into the Baltic, c. 6,500 BCE* (settled 9 October). Carsten: salt water carries a different
  population — here, oysters. Right on the mechanism (§03: the Littorina Sea brought oysters,
  which "need proper salinity"; "that single ecological fact is going to shape the rest of this
  page"). The thing to correct: the page does not make oysters the main food. The middens are
  overwhelmingly oyster shell, but the diet, by isotopes, was "oysters, mussels and cockles, but
  far more importantly fish" (§05). Question put: if fish mattered more, why does the record look
  like an oyster story? Carsten: we tell the period from what we find, and the shells stayed.
  **Settled.** A shell is large, durable and piles up into a monument; a fish leaves small, fragile
  bones. Same pattern as the §10 thread: the record shows what survives, and the reading has to
  correct for it — here the isotopes do the correcting.
- *Oysters and the first interdisciplinary commission* (settled 9 October). Question put: why did an argument
  about oysters produce it? Carsten: the argument was the vehicle; what mattered was that the
  government set the commission, that it added a third discipline, and that one of the two
  opponents publicly changed his mind. Right on the third discipline (Forchhammer, geology) and on
  Steenstrup changing his mind and publishing it (§08). To correct: not the government — the
  **Royal Danish Academy of Sciences** appointed it in 1848 (§08; DBL, Japetus Steenstrup). Pushed
  back on "vehicle": the question itself was natural deposit or human refuse — a zoologist's and a
  geologist's reading (oyster banks raised by uplift) against an archaeologist's — so no one
  discipline could settle it. Question put: is the argument then the cause, not the vehicle?
  Carsten: fair; the disciplines' readings were at odds, and they came to agreement. **Settled**,
  with one sharpening: the agreement came from digging together at Mejlgård (1850–51), not from
  negotiation — shells sorted by species and size as no tide sorts them, layers of charcoal and
  ash, bones of eaten animals (§08). The evidence moved Steenstrup.

Causal done.

### The page's end questions: Counterfactual (9 October 2026)

- *If the land had sunk everywhere* (settled 11 October). Carsten: we would know quite a bit less — the
  middens would be submerged and never found, with all that follows. Right on the middens; the
  page's own chain is middens on dry land, dug since the 1850s (Figure 2), → the 1848 commission
  (§08). Two corrections to "never found": submerged sites *are* found — Tybrind Vig, from 1957 —
  but only once divers could reach them, so the loss is a century of the discipline, not the sites;
  and drowned sites keep what dry ones lose — wood, bast, textiles, ten-metre boats (Figure 2:
  "some of the best-preserved wooden objects in European prehistory"). Question put: what would
  an all-underwater Mesolithic know *better* than ours, and what worse? Carsten: some materials
  last better underwater, some do not; overall our knowledge would be narrower — the split between
  dry and drowned sites is what gives breadth. **Settled.** From the page: the drowned south gives
  the organics (Tybrind Vig's boats, paddles, nålebinding, weir stakes; Figure 2, §05); the
  raised north gives the middens, reachable and dug since the 1850s, and with them the 1848
  commission (§08). Each record covers what the other cannot; the hinge line is why chapter 02
  has both (← chapter 1, "What to carry forward").
- *If the Ertebølle refusal had held two thousand years longer* (ended 11 October; partly open). Question put: what would
  Denmark have looked like when metal arrived from the south? Carsten: the page builds to the
  Ertebølle being "materially better off than many later Danish peasants" (Myth-check); Denmark
  would be richer tribes, mostly settled, some mobility. Claude: that describes the Ertebølle
  continued, not the meeting with metal, which is the question. Denmark has no copper or tin; the
  page says what it had instead — amber, found in quantity only on west Jutland and east Baltic
  shores, which "will be travelling to Mycenae" and pay for "Denmark's whole Bronze Age" (§07).
  Question put: could a forager society have run that trade, and what would it have needed?
  Carsten: as well as farmers or better — farming gives a stable, larger food supply, but if
  farmers were not materially better off, why better traders? Foragers could gather amber and
  move with traders more easily. Claude: the answer names the decisive thing and then sets it
  aside. "Materially better off" is per person; a larger, stable supply is a *total* — more
  people, and food stored to feed people who do not get food (carriers, specialists, whoever
  organises the exchange). Also in tension with "mostly settled": the page's Ertebølle barely
  moved (§01, §05). Question put: what does a surplus let a society do that matters for
  long-distance trade? Carsten: the question presupposes knowledge not on the page — **fair;
  Claude was leaning on chapter 04.** Taking it as "farming feeds more": foragers rely on everyone
  pulling their weight, so cannot spare people to trade and travel. Claude, from the page: too
  strong for *this* society — the Ertebølle already exchanged and travelled: southern adzes reached
  them (§05), bear teeth point to traffic with Skåne (§06), Ringkloster ties coast to interior,
  ten-metre boats carried a family group across open water (§05). Question put: is the limit then
  sparing people at all, or the scale of what can be spared? Carsten: those are short trips, not
  a month's journey to Greece. **Where it ended:** the limit is scale and distance, not sparing
  people at all. One caution, from the page's wording: §07 says the *amber* travels to Mycenae, not
  that anyone from Denmark did; a chain of short exchanges would move it as well. Whether a forager
  Denmark could have fed into such a chain is left open; how the chain actually ran is parked to
  chapter 04.

Counterfactual done.

### The page's end questions: Contested (11 October 2026)

- *Dyrholm and Rosenhof* (settled 11 October). Carsten: one grain can have several causes, not necessarily
  growing it yourself; to contest the long refusal you would want multiple instances. Right, and
  the page already reports both readings failing on exactly those grounds (§11; Rowley-Conwy 1985):
  the Dyrholm cattle teeth were reclassified as wild (aurochs cows proved about the size of
  domestic bulls), and the Rosenhof grain impression lay in a level that also held pots imported
  from farming cultures — Carsten's "other cause". Firmer ground is the pigs: ancient DNA from
  three Ertebølle pigs at two sites (Rosenhof, Poel) shows Near Eastern farmyard descent, one with
  a domestic spotted coat, c. 4,700–4,600 BCE; how they were acquired "remains unknown"
  (Krause-Kyora et al. 2013). Question put: several animals, two sites, DNA — does that break the
  "long refusal"? Carsten: it does not break it but challenges it; and it is only pigs. Agreed —
  a few pigs, acquired by trade or as strays (unknown), show contact, not farming: no crops,
  cattle or clearing. One more thing the page states and the answer can use: both sites are
  outside Denmark — Rosenhof in Schleswig-Holstein, Poel on the Mecklenburg coast (§11), at the
  southern edge of the Ertebølle world, nearest the farmers. Question put: does that matter for a
  *Danish* refusal? Carsten: of course — a few places on the outskirts dabbled towards farming;
  it was not part of the culture further north. **Settled:** the refusal stands for the Danish
  area, challenged at its southern margin. One caution on words: "Danish" here is geography, not
  a people — the Ertebølle ran across the modern border, so the margin is a gradient towards the
  farmers, not a frontier (ties to the Lola question on labels).
- *Bøgebakken and "tenderness"* (settled 11 October). Carsten: Bøgebakken is broad — are all the graves read
  that way? No: the page's tenderness rests on one grave, the young woman with the infant on the
  swan's wing (§06: "people took trouble"; "A swan's wing for an infant … is not a subsistence
  strategy"; and The page in five). The same cemetery holds the triple grave with a man killed by
  a bone point, which the page reads as violence (§10). Question narrowed: for the swan's-wing
  grave, how much of "care" comes from the evidence, how much from us? Carsten: mostly from us;
  we do not know what was meant — tenderness is our wish for a story. Claude: right about the
  feeling, but "mostly" may overcorrect. Split two claims: *effort* — someone chose a swan's wing,
  laid the infant on it, placed a blade at its waist — is in the ground; *tenderness*, a feeling,
  is ours. The page's own phrase, "people took trouble", is the first kind. Question put: is that
  phrase from the evidence or from us? Carsten: effort — a fact; the reason behind it is hidden.
  **Settled**, and it answers the question's second half: the unsentimental description is the
  effort described, and it is more accurate, not colder. The page keeps to it ("people took
  trouble"); only "tenderness" in the question's framing goes past the ground.
- *Farming as a movement of people* (open). Carsten: not sure of the answer from this page alone.
  Fair — the page defers it to chapter 03 (§11). Reframed to what the page gives: §11's own wording,
  the Ertebølle "declined it for forty or fifty generations, and then took it up almost at once",
  and "the Ertebølle way of life stops". Question put: if the farmers were newcomers, who is the
  subject of "took it up"? Carsten asked back: would many incoming farmers not have created other
  conflicts? Claude: this page is silent on how the two populations met; that is chapter 03's
  ground (parked). But the question already does the work: conflict, mixing or displacement are
  each a *different event* from "the Ertebølle took up farming". Question put again: of those, which
  does the page's sentence describe, and which does it leave out? (§11: "The question is why the
  Ertebølle people declined it for forty or fifty generations, and then took it up almost at
  once.") Carsten: not displacement — an action of the resident population; no conflict in it
  (that was his inference); so mixing. Claude: closer, but the sentence has no newcomers in it at
  all, so not mixing either. It describes *adoption* by the same people — ideas spreading — and
  leaves out all three. Yet the next paragraph lists "a movement of people, with farmers arriving
  in numbers" as one possible answer, and ancient DNA "points" somewhere "not comfortable".
  Question put: does the framing sentence quietly assume the answer to the question the page then
  calls open? Carsten: the page as good as says "I have the answer but won't tell you yet".
  Checked against chapter 03 and its source: it is worse than a teaser — chapter 03 §02 rules out
  exactly what §11's sentence says ("the comfortable twentieth-century picture in which the
  Ertebølle people looked over the fence, liked what they saw, and became farmers. Most of them did
  not become anything"), on Allentoft et al. 2024. **Became Finding F1 (below), corrected.** Where
  the thread ended: the page's framing gave the residents the action; the source gives a turnover.
- *Lola and period labels* (settled 11 October). Carsten: labels generalise periods; reality does not keep to
  boxes. In general: labels help conversation and understanding, and risk squeezing things into
  boxes. Right, and the page says the same (§11: "a line drawn on a chronology chart is a
  convenience, and … the people alive on either side of it did not know it was there"). Made
  concrete: "the Mesolithic–Neolithic transition" reads as one people changing its way of life,
  which is the picture F1 corrected. Question put: is F1 an instance of the second risk — the
  label doing the arguing? Carsten: yes, he takes it to be; people straddling periods are
  annoying. **Settled:** the annoyance is the use — Lola is the evidence that the box is drawn
  round a date, not round a people.

## Findings

### F1 · §11 and The page in five: the Ertebølle "took up" / "adopted" farming — *corrected*

Found 11 October 2026 in the Contested thread on a movement of people (above).

**What is wrong.** Two sentences say the resident population adopted farming. The online source
says the transition was a population turnover with little local contribution; chapter 03 §02
says so too, citing it.

- Allentoft et al. 2024, "100 ancient genomes show repeated population turnovers in Neolithic
  Denmark", *Nature* 625, 329–337, https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-023-06862-3 (read
  11 October 2026). Abstract: the transition "was very abrupt and resulted in a population turnover
  with limited genetic contribution from local hunter-gatherers", when "Neolithic farmers with
