# 03 · First farmers and the megalith builders — study page

Page: `03-first-farmers-and-the-megalith-builders.html` · learning pass, session 3 (D-21)

## Checkpoints

After §03:

1. What is landnam, and how does it show up in a pollen core?

   > It is the old icelandic word for land-taking. And the first sign of a change came from the
   > change in pollen cores for that period.

   *Against the page (§03):* right on the word (Iversen borrowed the Old Norse term for the
   taking of land in Iceland, 1941) and right that pollen shows farming before artefacts do.
   Missing: the sequence, which is how a core is read. Elm pollen drops (elmefaldet, c. 4,000
   BCE), then charcoal, weeds of cultivation such as plantain, and cereal pollen; then the forest
   partly recovers, and in many cores the sequence repeats. That repetition is what made Iversen
   read it as clear, crop, abandon, move on.

2. What did Iversen's clearance experiment in Draved Skov show about polished flint axes?

   > polished flint axes worked better than expected in taking down trees. It is not a toy.

   *Against the page (§03):* right. Missing: the experiment's second result, which is why it
   mattered to landnam. The burnt and sown plot left a pollen signature in the local soil that
   could be compared directly with the one in the ancient cores. The axe result says the
   clearance was possible; the pollen result ties the cores to clearance.

After §06:

3. What is the difference between a dysse and a jættestue?

   > dysse is a grave for a few, build and closed. Jættestue can be opened and closed repeatedly,
   > and could house more than a 100 dead.

   *Against the page (§05, Figure 2):* right on the difference that matters: a few dead and
   closed, against collective and re-opened. One number to correct: the page says some held
   "towards a hundred individuals", not more than a hundred. Missing: the passage, which is what
   makes the re-opening possible ("A passage grave can be opened; that is the point of the
   passage"). It is built in from the long side of a bigger chamber. And the order: dysser from
   c. 3,500 BCE, jættestuer from c. 3,300.

4. Roughly how many megalithic tombs were built, and how many still stand?

   > The estimate was 25.000 to 40.000 . 2.400-2.500 are visible around the country.

   *Against the page (§05):* right on both (Trap Danmark's range; Jensen's *Danmarks Oldtid*
   says on the order of 40,000). Missing: the middle figure and where the estimate comes from.
   Some 7,000 are on record. The jump from 7,000 to tens of thousands rests on local surveys,
   where archaeologists walked every field in a district and kept finding far more than the
   registers list.

After §09:

5. Where do Single Grave barrows cluster, and why is that location familiar?

   > They cluster is western jutland, west of the Hovedopholdslinjen. The main line, now a
   > cultural frontier.

   *Against the page (§09, Figure 3):* right (central and western Jutland; the ice margin line is
   chapter 1's hovedopholdslinje). Missing: what the line did that makes it a frontier, which is
   the soils. East of it is moraine country with heavy clay, where the megaliths cluster. West of
   it are the sandy outwash plains that meltwater laid down. The usual reading is that the light
   western soils suited herding.

6. What happened to the Funnel Beaker world before the newcomers arrived?

   > It is now guessed at that the plague came to the country and ravaged its population

   *Against the page (§08):* partly wrong. It gives a cause, and more firmly than the page does,
   where the question asks what happened. What happened, over some three centuries, is that the
   enclosures closed (c. 3,200–3,100 BCE), megalith building stopped, the settlement pattern broke
   up, the pottery lost its coherence, and forest came back on cleared land. Why is "genuinely
   open". Plague is one candidate of four, alongside soil exhaustion, a cooling climate and social
   machinery that ran down. Its evidence is from Scandinavia, the clearest from western Sweden
   (Falbygden), not from Denmark. The point the page builds on is the order: decline first, then
   the newcomers, so no conquest of a thriving society.

## Work the material

### The page's end questions: Recall

All five answered 20 October; all settled.

- *The 2024 study on Maglemose, Kongemose, Ertebølle.* Carsten: a single genetic cluster; three
  cultures, one population. Right (§02), stable "from about 8,500 BCE right through to the end".
  Worth carrying: it is this chapter's opposite. There, material culture changed three times
  with no change of people; here, both farming and the Single Grave culture arrive *with* new
  people.
- *The ard.* Carsten: a form of plough; its marks survive under burial mounds. Right. Missing:
  how it differs from a plough. It "does not turn a furrow but scores the soil in criss-crossing
  passes", dragged by oxen. The marks survive because a mound sealed "the last ploughing of a
  field before someone built a monument on top of it" (§03).
- *Tustrup kulthus.* Carsten: sherds of thirty fine vessels; for housing the dead. Right on both
  (also clay ladles: pedestalled bowls and funnel beakers). Missing: the rest of the usual
  reading, the dead housed "and the ceremonies prepared, before final burial in the chambers
  some fifty metres away", and that it is contested. Others read a temple, and a re-excavation
  suggests it may have had no roof (§06). Same pattern as Sarup: a stage before the tomb.
- *Single Grave burial.* Carsten: men on the right, head west; women on the left, head east; men
  with a stridsøkse. Right. Missing: the shared posture, one to a grave under a small round
  barrow, "on the side with the knees drawn up and the face turned south" (§09), the opposite of
  the collective jættestue.
- *The flint dagger.* Carsten: a metal dagger, of copper or bronze. Right. Missing: how far the
  copy went, "including the details that only make sense in metal", down to a stitched-looking
  seam on the grip, and the page's two readings: provincial imitation, or virtuosity metal could
  not match. "Both readings are probably true" (§10).

### The page's end questions: Causal

- *Why mine flint from the chalk when it lay on the fields?* (settled 18 October). Carsten: better
  quality, no cracks, unlike flint on the ground. Right (§07: surface flint is "full of invisible
  cracks" from frost; mined flint "had never been exposed to frost"). Missing: what the quality
  was for. The page names outsized ceremonial axes and, later, daggers, which need "stone that
  will not betray him at the last blow". The axes were polished "to a gloss the working edge does
  not require", "Some of the finest were never used", and many "were given away — to bogs and
  lakes". Question put: if the best mined flint went into objects never used, what was the
  mining really for?
  Carsten: offerings. Claude: right for many of them, with one more use. "The distribution of
  the resulting axes shows them travelling well beyond the districts that produced them" (§07),
  so exchange too. What the two share is that the axe's value lay in giving it away, to the gods
  or to other people. Question put: what does a household gain by giving away the finest thing
  it owns?
  Carsten: an offering is the idea of having a god on your side; an idea, since he doubts there
  is evidence of any actual response. Claude: fair, and it leaves the giver's other gain. Even
  if no god answers, giving away what took many hours to make shows you can afford it. An axe
  given to another person, unlike one given to a bog, may come back as a return gift or an
  obligation. **The page does not discuss status or obligation**; this is a reading, not its
  claim. Question put: even if no god answered, could the giver still gain something, and from
  whom?
  Carsten: giving, today too, leaves the receiver feeling a debt whether they want it or not,
  which is why one sometimes refuses a free present. So giving can be both goodness and tactical
  positioning, obliging a return. Claude: and that joins his two answers into one logic. An
  offering is a gift that puts the god in debt ("a god on your side"); an axe given to a
  neighbour puts the neighbour in debt. The bog and the exchange route are the same act with
  different receivers. **Settled** (18 October). Hook offered, not yet taken: this is the
  argument of Marcel Mauss's *The Gift* (1925), the classic anthropology of obligation.
- *Why is Sarup read as a gathering place for a whole district?* (settled 18 October). Carsten:
  the ditches held human bone, animal bone, broken pottery and flint; settlements lay not far
  around. Right on both (§06: "broken pottery, burnt flint, animal bone, fragments of human
  bone"; "some twenty contemporary settlements"). Missing: the other half of the landscape,
  "around 125 megalithic graves" within a few kilometres. Those are what the human bone points
  to: the reading is feasting, exchange, and "handling the dead before their final burial in
  the tombs". Question put: why would *fragments* of human bone in a ditch suggest a stage before
  the tomb, rather than burial itself?
  Carsten: a burial would hold whole bones, not fragments. Right: the fragments are what was
  left behind when the dead moved on, and the whole remains went to the tombs. That matches how
  the passage graves were used, with bones "pushed aside and stacked to make room" and a
  "chamber's worth of accumulated ancestors" (§05). Sarup and the tombs are two stations of one
  treatment of the dead. **Settled** (18 October).
- *Why does barley gradually win out over wheat?* (settled 18 October). Carsten: barley fits the
  soil and climate better. Right; the page's words are that it "tolerates poor soil and a short
  season" (§04), and it keeps winning "for the next five thousand years". The short season is
  the same limit the Contested thread on the pause turned on: a warmer summer moves the edge of
  what will grow.
- *Why does agriculture make individuals less healthy and societies more powerful?* (settled 18
  October). Carsten: a less varied diet with fewer nutrients; but more calories per individual,
  so a much larger population. Right on the shape. The page also names teeth (caries and heavy
  wear from "gritty stone-ground flour") and disease, from "Living close to livestock in
  permanent settlements" (§11). The one correction is the unit. The page says farming supports
  "a population several times larger *on the same ground*". That is more food per field, not
  per person. Question put: what is the difference, and how does it resolve the paradox of
  sicker individuals in a stronger society?
  Carsten: fields produce up to, and set, a sustainable maximum; and some people will want to
  abuse the system. Claude: the first half is the resolution. The extra food per field goes into
  *more people*, not better-fed ones, until the population presses against that maximum. So
  individuals live near the limit while the society counts in numbers: "Against this: far more
  people. That is the whole trade" (§11). His second point, inequality, the page touches only at
  the end of the period (§10: richly equipped male graves "suggest a warrior elite"). **Settled**
  (18 October).

### The page's end questions: Counterfactual

- *No Funnel Beaker collapse before 2,850 BCE: does the steppe expansion still reach Denmark, and
  still replace the population?* (settled 13 October). Carsten: yes and yes, but slower and with
  more integration. Claude: the page has a partial test of this inside Denmark. Where Funnel
  Beaker held on, on the islands and in eastern Denmark, the two lived side by side for "a couple
  of centuries" against about fifty years in west Jutland (Figure 1 caption). Old traditions
  persisted, the megaliths stayed in use, and the material culture mixed (§09: "west Jutland as
  replacement and the islands as accommodation"). So his "slower, more integration" is roughly
  what happened where the old world was strongest (overstated as a test; see below). Question put: §02 calls the second turnover
  "as rapid and as complete as the first", and its profile the one modern Danes carry. So did
  accommodation on the islands change the end result, or only the pace?
  Carsten: mostly the pace; replacement would have happened, the only question was how fast.
  Claude, a correction to its own framing first: the islands are not a no-collapse case. §08's
  collapse is of the whole Funnel Beaker world, so the islands test strength of the old culture,
  not its absence of decline; "a partial test" overstated it. Then the point: the page's mechanism
  for the second turnover *is* the collapse. "It is a society that collapsed first, and a
  replacement that moved into the space" (§08); the Myth-check: collapse makes "a simple conquest
  story less likely". Take the collapse away and moving into empty space is no longer available.
  Question put: of §02's four ways an ancestry shift happens (killing, displacement, low-rate
  absorption, out-reproduction), which still works against a thriving farming population, and
  does "only a question of pace" still hold?
  Carsten (13 October): all but displacement would work. Claude, the most important problem
  first: out-reproduction. The page's case for it (§02: "Farming populations grow quickly;
  foraging populations do not") is an edge farmers have over *foragers*. In the second turnover
  both sides farm or herd, and the page gives the newcomers no faster demographic cycle. Without
  the collapse thinning the Funnel Beaker population, the page gives no reason the newcomers
  would outbreed it. Question put: what would give them that edge, or does out-reproduction fall
  with displacement?
  Carsten: it *could* work, but it would need a cultural or other reason he does not know of;
  on what he knows, unlikely. **Settled:** possible, unsupported, so unlikely. That separates
  what would work from what the evidence makes likely, which is the right line. Next point:
  absorption at low rates. It describes how few locals entered the incoming population, not
  what happened to the rest. Against a large, thriving population it removes no one, so it needs
  another mechanism doing the removing. Question put: with out-reproduction and absorption both
  leaning on something else, what is left, and what does that do to "only a question of pace"?
  Carsten: killings, or death in other ways. Claude: "death in other ways" is disease, and it
  splits two ways. Disease that struck *before* the newcomers is the collapse the counterfactual
  took away (§08: plague as a candidate, in the Funnel Beaker population). Disease the newcomers
  *brought* would be a different story, one the page does not tell. Question put: which does he
  mean? If the first, what is left is killing, and removing the collapse changes the kind of
  event, not its pace.
  Carsten: any death apart from killing; and the newcomers could have brought a disease they
  were themselves immune to. Claude: conceded. That mechanism works, the page does not rule it
  out, and it is not far-fetched: it was the earlier research model, that steppe migration
  spread plague into Europe (as summarised by The Science Breaker on Rascovan et al., *Cell*
  176, 2019, read 13 October:
  https://thesciencebreaker.org/breaks/microbiology/a-bacterium-with-the-power-of-changing-the-course-of-human-history).
  But it changes the counterfactual. Disease the newcomers bring is a collapse they cause, so the
  old world does not stay thriving. **Where it stands:** without some collapse, replacement needs
  killing; with one, it can be quiet. The collapse matters to *what kind* of event the second
  turnover was, not only its pace. Hook offered, not yet taken: Rascovan et al. 2019 argue the
  opposite of the steppe model, that plague was in Europe before the migrations (a Swedish
  Funnel Beaker-era find, c. 4,900 years ago).

- *Suppose Denmark had possessed copper ore. How much of chapters 3 and 4 changes?* (settled 18
  October). Carsten: not sure how much it would change here; it would have established the use
  of metal instead of flint. Claude: the most important problem first, which is that ore is not
  metal. Copper reached Denmark as finished axes and ornaments, and "It changes nothing" (§07).
  Metalworking itself only "takes root" at the very end of dagger time (§10), some two thousand
  years after the first copper. Ore in the ground needs smelting, which the page shows arriving
  last. Question put: would Funnel Beaker farmers have known what to do with copper ore, and if
  not, what does that leave of the counterfactual?
  Carsten: not much. Claude: for chapter 3, agreed; most of the change would land later. The
  page's own carry-forward to chapter 4 says why: "A country with no copper or tin buys bronze
  with fossil resin, and gets rich doing it." The lack of ore is what makes amber a currency.
  Question put: once smelting is known (end of §10), what does ore in Danish ground do to that
  arrow, and who might come for it? (Tin would still be missing, so bronze would still need
  imports.)
  Carsten: it would make trade more possible and more profitable. Claude: yes, with a second
  export besides amber. The other side of that is that a deposit others want is also a reason
  for them to come and take it. **Where it stands (proposed for settling):** in chapter 3 little
  changes, because ore is useless without smelting, which comes last. The difference lands in
  chapter 4: a richer trading position, and a more exposed one. **Settled** (Carsten, 18 October).

### The page's end questions: Contested

  Carsten (20 October): a nationalist would angle for who the original people are, define that
  as who "we" are, and others as wrong and not belonging. Claude: and the page hands them one
  sentence for it, the §12 line Claude proposed leaving on 13 October: "if you want to know when

  inherited". In its favour, it says they *arrived*, as the second set of newcomers, which
  undercuts "original". But it does in the third person what F1's "us" did in the first:
  equates modern Danes with one ancestry. **Claude revises its 13 October call.** Possible
  rewording, if Carsten agrees it is the same fault: "…if you want to know when most of the
  ancestry Danes carry today arrived in Denmark, the answer is not the Ice Age, and not the
  Vikings. It is here." Question put: same fault as F1, or a fair population-level statement?
  Carsten: removing the "us" is better and creates some distance, but nothing stops someone
  reading it differently, and he is not sure what would. Claude: nothing can make a misreading
  impossible. The obligation is narrower, not to supply the words for one. The page's best
  defence is its whole story: there is no "original" people in it. The reindeer hunters arrived
  (chapter 1), farmers replaced their descendants c. 3,950 BCE, and steppe-descended people

  reword the §12 line the same way, yes or no?
  Carsten: yes. **F2**, agreed (below). Where it stands: the obligations are (1) say what the data
  measures, (2) do not fold readers into an ancestry, and (3) keep every population a layer, so
  that no "original" people is on offer. Nothing stops a determined misreading; the page should
  not supply the words. **Settled** (20 October).

- *"Population replacement": what would distinguish violence, displacement, disease and
  differential fertility, and does the distinction matter morally or only historically?* (settled
  13 October; follows from the Counterfactual). Carsten: it matters morally only insofar as we
  apply modern morals to history, which we do a lot and is its own subject. If Danes descend
  from a population that won by violence, that does not alter Danish achievements in the
  millennia after; you do not tear down buildings paid for by slavery. Claude: the principle is
  sound, that descent carries neither guilt nor credit. But it cuts both ways. If a violent
  origin taints nothing, a glorious one confers nothing either. That second half is what the
  page warns about (§09: migration explanations "thoroughly abused politically in the first half
  of the twentieth century"; the first Contested question). Question put: if descent confers
  nothing, why did it matter so much to the people who abused it, and what does that ask of how
  the 2024 results are written up?
  Carsten: agreed it goes both ways; it is abused out of convenience ("Danes are Vikings" shows
  it is an idea), by people focused on the nation state, on belonging and the right to be here,
  and others not. Claude: then the page's own result is awkward for that use. Its answer to when
  the ancestors of modern Danes arrived is "not the Ice Age, and not the Vikings. It is here",
  c. 2,850 BCE, as newcomers who replaced the people already living here (§12). That undercuts
  "we were here first". It can still be read the other way, though ("newcomers take the land;
  that is how it works"), so the data does not choose the moral. Question put, turning it on the
  page's own writing (the first Contested question): §02 says of the Ertebølle "Their
  descendants are not, in the main, us", and §12 asks after "the ancestors of modern Danes". Is
  "us" careful enough, or does the word itself invite the belonging reading?
  Carsten: it does, and so does the hedge "in the main". Claude: agreed, and by item 165's lesson
  (a framing sentence is a claim) it is more than tone. "Us" says the reader descends from the
  Single Grave people, which is false for any Dane whose family came later, and "in the main"
  measures that as a share of persons. The genomes measure ancestry proportions, which is the
  page's own point six lines earlier. The ancestry claim is sound; the pronoun is the fault.
  **F1**, agreed (below). Carsten: collect all edits and apply them together once the chapter
  is through. §12 left, as proposed.

- *Was the elm fall caused by farmers or by disease, and how would you tell?* (settled 18 October;
  ran on into the fourth Contested question, the common cause of the pause, settled with it).
  Carsten: look for patterns of change in time and place, but he cannot yet see how to read
  them. Claude: right method; the step is to ask what each cause *predicts*, then look. Disease
  should hit elm only, everywhere at about the same time, whether or not farmers are there.
  Clearance should hit where and when farmers are, take other trees too, and come with the
  other signs. The page gives both clues: the drop is "recorded right across northern Europe"
  around 4,000 BCE, and in the cores it is "followed by charcoal, by weeds of cultivation like
  plantain, and by cereal pollen" (§03). Question put: which prediction does each clue fit?
  Carsten: the wide, simultaneous drop sounds like disease; the sequence in the cores sounds
  like farming, clearing by burning and then sowing. Right on both, and that is the page's own
  position: the elm fall "owe[s] something to disease … rather than being purely the work of
  axes" (§03). So it is not either/or. Next point: the page's word is "followed". In the cores
  the elm falls first, and the signs of farming come after. Question put: what does that order
  allow, and what might early farmers do with a forest full of dying elms?
  Carsten: the natural reaction would be to cut them down and plant something else. Claude:
  close, with one turn. They were not replacing trees; a dead elm is a gap in the canopy, light
  on the ground and timber already falling. The order allows disease to come first and make
  clearing easier, so farmers are helped rather than reacting. **The page does not say this**;
  it is an inference from its "followed". Question put, linking to the fourth Contested question:
  if it held, with the elm fall around 4,000 BCE across northern Europe and Denmark and Britain
  both turning to farming about then after a long pause (§02 "Meanwhile in Europe"), what would
  that suggest about the pause's "common cause"?
  Carsten: that the elm disease was region-wide and spurred on the transition. Claude: as a
  hypothesis, fair. It rests on two steps the page does not take (the canopy inference, and the
  dates lining up, which the fourth Contested question itself doubts). The most important point,
  though, is whose transition. §02: farming came with people, and the locals mostly "did not
  become anything". So the disease could not have spurred Ertebølle hunters to farm; it would
  have had to draw farmers north. Question put: does the hypothesis survive that rephrasing, and
  what would it need to explain?
  Carsten: still a region-wide disease, but it made it easier for people to migrate in: easier
  clearing, easier farmland. Claude: that survives, and it is the right shape (a condition that
  lowered the cost of moving in, not a cause of conversion). What it must still explain is the
  pause. The farmers to the south had already farmed forested country for a thousand years
  (central Europe, Rhine by 5,300 BCE, §02 "Meanwhile in Europe"). Question put: if forest had
  not stopped them there, why would it hold them at Denmark's edge for a thousand years, and is
  "easier clearing" then enough?
  Carsten: no; could there be another environmental factor? Claude: **page 03 is silent** on
  what caused the pause; it says only that it "had a common cause" (§02). Chapter 02 §11 lists
  candidates, one of them environmental: something went wrong with the coastal economy, "oyster
  beds shifting with salinity, perhaps". But chapter 02 frames them all as reasons for the
  *locals'* choice, and chapter 03 has since shown the locals did not convert. The question is
  now why the *farmers* stopped. One candidate is not environmental at all: the Ertebølle
  themselves, a dense, settled coastal population holding fixed resources (chapter 02 §10).
  Question put: could people be the barrier rather than the land? Hook offered, not yet taken:
  look up online what has been proposed for the pause (two quick searches, 18 October, found
  nothing citable yet: Sørensen 2017/18, *Acta Archaeologica Lodziensia* 63, describes a
  scouting phase before pioneering farmers c. 4000 cal BC but gives no cause in its abstract).
  Carsten: a population adept at fighting would make migrating there unattractive, but something
  would still have to happen to end it. Claude: right, and the page says where to look. Whatever
  ended the pause "ended it everywhere at once": Denmark, Britain and Ireland, c. 4,000 BCE (§02
  "Meanwhile in Europe"). Those are separate hunter populations; a barrier failing in all of them
  together is unlikely, so the change more likely sat on the farmers' side or in something
  region-wide. (Not raised in chat: the evidence for Ertebølle aggression is thin. Chapter 02's
  F6 hedged it to "may well arrive together".) Question put: on the farmers' side or
  region-wide, what kind of change would fit?
  Carsten: the farmer population grew markedly, making migration both needed and possible, with
  overwhelming numbers. Claude: a real candidate, and it fits "farmers' side". The page does not
  say it. One problem for it: growth is gradual, and the end of the pause was abrupt and
  simultaneous. A gradual cause needs a threshold, or a trigger alongside it. Candidates now on
  the table from this thread: elm disease easing clearance, farmer population pressure, and
  something region-wide. Question put: what would make a slow build-up tip over in many places at
  once? Hook offered again: look up what has been proposed, and test the three against it.
  Carsten: yes (hook taken, 18 October). Claude read, 18 October:
  - Warden et al. 2017, "Climate induced human demographic and cultural change in northern
    Europe during the mid-Holocene", *Scientific Reports* (abstract, via
    https://research-portal.uu.nl/en/publications/climate-induced-human-demographic-and-cultural-change-in-northern/):
    "a sudden increase in summer temperature at 6,000 cal. yr BP in northern Europe" (Baltic
    sea-surface temperatures), which "coincided with both the introduction of farming, and a
    dramatic population increase"; farming in the north had come late "for unknown reasons".
    Offered as a hypothesis ("The evidence supports the hypothesis…"); a coincidence in time,
    not shown to be cause.
  - Shennan et al. 2013, *Nature Communications* (via
    https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3806351): farming was followed by a regional
    "boom-and-bust" in population, which the authors found unrelated to climate. It does not
    test what drove the spread, so it neither supports nor refutes Carsten's
    population-pressure candidate.
  - Sørensen 2017/18 (above): scouts first, then pioneering farmers from c. 4000 cal BC; no
    cause in the abstract.
  Put to Carsten: Warden fits "region-wide" and "sudden" (c. 4,000 BCE, all of northern
  Europe). Question put: how would a warmer summer tip a slow build-up of farmers into a move
  north, and what does it do to the other two candidates?
  Carsten: warmer weather makes land more farmable (and could not recall the other two).
  Claude: reminded him (elm disease easing clearance; farmer population pressure). Sharpened:
  the page itself shows the season was a real limit. §04 says barley wins "because it tolerates
  poor soil and a short season", so Near Eastern crops were working at their edge here. A warmer
  summer moves that edge north. That is the tipping point the population-pressure idea lacked:
