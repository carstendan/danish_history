# 01 · Reindeer hunters and the retreating ice — study page

Page: `01-reindeer-hunters-and-the-retreating-ice.html` · learning pass, session 1 (D-21)

## Checkpoints

After §02:

1. What is the hovedopholdslinje, and which side of it has the better soil?

   > It is the point where the icecap stopped. And the eastern side had far better soil, whereas
   > the western side was fairly poor and took great effort in the 19th century to make into usable
   > land.

   *Against the page (§01):* right on the line and on the sides. Thin in two places. (a) The line is
   where the ice *stood*, not just where it stopped: the advance from central Sweden c. 22,000 years
   ago held there long enough for advance and melting to cancel out, and that standstill is what
   built the outwash plains to the west. (b) The answer gives the outcome but not the mechanism, the
   one the page leans on later: east = clay moraine, which holds water and nutrients; west = sorted
   outwash sand (hedesletter), which does not. West Jutland was never under this ice at all; it was
   frozen tundra on older moraine (bakkeøer) with sand washed over it.

2. Sea level stood some 80–100 m lower. What did that join Denmark to?

   > It means that what now is the North Sea, was walkable land. And you could walk from what is
   > now Denmark to Britain.

   *Against the page (§02):* right. Thin: name it, **Doggerland**, and it was not a land bridge but a
   habitable plain with rivers, lakes, marshes and coast of its own. The page's larger point is
   missing: there was "no Denmark", only the northern edge of one plain from Britain to Poland,
   reachable only from the south. That is the pattern the chapter says recurs. Also on the page: the
   Baltic was a freshwater ice-dammed lake, not a sea, and Bornholm was joined to land in the south.

After §04:

3. What is a zinken, and which culture does it identify?

   > Zinken is a curved element of something, identifying the Hamburg culture. Researches had for a
   > long time hoped to find evidence of such present in Denmark. And it wasn't until a schoolteacher
   > randomly stumbled upon it, and gave it to a museum - where it sat un-checked for years - that
   > evidence surfaced.

   *Against the page (§03, §04):* the culture is right, and so is the story's shape (Jørn Fynbo, Jels
   Oversø 1968, Haderslev Museum, recognised only in 1980, dug from 1981). The *what* is the thin
   half. A zinken is a **flint tool**: a curved, beaked **borer**, the hunters' all-purpose tool for
   working hide and antler. It is the culture's **type-fossil**, its single most diagnostic artefact,
   which is why one stray find could announce a whole culture. "A long time" is concrete on the
   page: more than thirty years, since Alfred Rust's 1930s digs at Meiendorf and Stellmoor showed
   what a reindeer-hunter site looked like. Not quite "randomly": Fynbo was puzzled enough by the
   flint to go and read about it. Keep the two museum stories apart: the box that sat on a shelf
   for twenty-odd years is **Slotseng's** (collected 1962, recognised 1985), not Jels's.

4. How did the antler at Slotseng reveal the season of the hunt?

   > Reindeer antlers changes according to a strict seasonal schedule, varied between male and
   > female reindeer. So a specific antler will show both the sex and the season. Thus it have been
   > possible to say, that the antler found was between September and December, and therefore in
   > the fall. This is set against the belief that hunters were only present in the summer period,
   > and makes plausible that they were present all year around.

   *Against the page (§03):* right, and the strongest answer so far. You have the mechanism, the
   result and why it matters. Small sharpenings: the page's window is late September to early
   December. It is antler *fragments* from one autumn kill of eleven adult reindeer, not one antler.
   The assumption it overturned is that the hunters came north in summer and **wintered somewhere
   warmer**. Missing link: the reason there is bone to read at all. The kill waste was thrown into a
   dead-ice pond, and oxygen-poor mud five metres down preserved it; Danish soil normally keeps no
   bone from this period.

After §07:

5. Name the four cultures in order, and the climate phase each belongs to.

   > Bølling => Hamburg, Allerød => Federmesser, Young Dairys => Bromme, Holocene => ???. This does
   > not really seem described at this point.

   *Against the page (§05 Figure 3, §07, §08):* the first two are right and the second two are not.
   The order is **Hamburg** (Bølling), **Federmesser** (through the Older Dryas into the Allerød,
   from c. 11,800 BCE), **Bromme** (the *later* Allerød, still warm, birch woodland) and
   **Ahrensburg** (Younger Dryas). Each answer after the second is one step late. The Holocene
   (from c. 9,700 BCE) has no culture on this page; it is where chapter 02 begins.

   On "not described at this point": half right. Before this checkpoint the prose names
   Ahrensburg only as a place (the tunnel valley where Rust dug Stellmoor, §03). The culture is
   introduced in §08, *after* the checkpoint. But Figure 3 (§05) labels all four cultures against
   the curve, AHRENSBURG under YOUNGER DRYAS, so the page has said it, in the figure. Read as
   D-17 reads ("asks only what the page has said before its anchor"), this is not an error; it does
   lean on the figure. Not recorded as a finding unless you want it raised.

6. What does the Laacher See hypothesis claim about the Bromme culture?

   > That it was not a separate culture, but part of the federmesser culture.

   *Against the page (§07):* wrong. That is the **Brommeproblemet**, Riede's *later* argument
   (2017), which the page presents as a separate line, "another direction entirely". The Laacher
   See hypothesis (Riede 2008) is causal. The eruption c. 11,050 BCE emptied the worst-hit regions
   and broke the exchange networks Federmesser groups depended on. That left the northern periphery
   isolated, and small isolated populations lose complex skills. So the simpler Bromme toolkit,
   with the bow apparently lost, is *cultural impoverishment* made visible. The mechanism is
   social, not climatic: ash on Denmark was only traces, and on the current dating the eruption
   came some 150–200 years before the Younger Dryas.

## The myth check

> **Ice age.** Ice-age is not a singular, forward moving event, but more cyclical with growth and
> retractions. In the same way, the few inhabitants there were throughout may not be related.
>
> **Denmark under ice.** Western Jutland were never covered by ice. With the consequence that
> western jutland have a very different soil compared to what was covered by ice.
>
> **The first danes.** There were no country. The was no denmark. The area was physically different
> to what we know call denmark. And the people were not uniform. There is simply no concept of
> nationhood nor of being danish.
>
> **Lacher See and Younger Dryas.** Recent research shows that there were 150-200 years from
> eruption to the next cold phase (younger dryas). There is no direct or casual link between them.
>
> **Stone Age people and caves.** There are practically no caves in denmark. In France / Spain were
> there are caves, it is different but in denmark it is not an option.

*Against the page (Myth-check):*

- **Ice age.** This misses the myth's target. The myth is about *order*: "the ice ended, *then*
  people arrived". The page's answer is that people came **before** it ended. Hamburg hunters were
  here in the Bølling, and Ahrensburg hunters through the Younger Dryas. They were here in cold
  phases, left, and came back. "Cyclical" also overstates it: within this window the page says the
  ice age ended *twice*, with a millennium-long relapse between. The continuity point (four pulses,
  no reason to assume a link) is right.
- **Denmark under ice.** Right as the page has it. But "never covered" is true only of the
  *last* glaciation (Weichsel). In the one before it (Saale), ice covered the whole country, and
  west Jutland's bakkeøer are that older moraine (§01 says so: "the worn-down moraine of the
  previous glaciation"). The page's own myth line says flatly "Western Jutland never did", which
  is where this reading comes from. → Findings, F1.
- **The first Danes.** "No nationhood" is right. Thinner on the page's main point, which is
  *ancestry*: they were the first people **here**, but whether anyone later descends from them is
  unknown and not to be assumed, given repeated population turnover (§09). "Not uniform" is not
  quite the page's claim. The claim is that successive groups may be *unrelated*, and that
  Europe-wide populations were replaced.
- **Laacher See.** The 150–200 years is right. Careful on scope: the page says the redating rules
  out a **direct** causal link to the cold. It does not say "no causal link of any kind". Missing:
  the 2023 challenge and the 2025 cave date that answered it. Also missing is the line that matters
  most after checkpoint 6: Riede's Bromme argument never needed the eruption to cause the cold; it
  needs it to break social networks.
- **Caves.** Right. Thin on what replaces the cave: open-air camps, almost certainly hide tents,
  and people on the move. The cave-dweller image is imported from French and Spanish prehistory.

- **Doggerland → 2.** Half right. The settlements do not just disappear. The page's point is that

- **Flint → 3, 4.** Right, and it matches the page.
- **Population turnover → 3.** Right but thin. The page names the occasion, **farming's
  arrival**, and the conclusion: the assumption of a continuous Danish people does not survive it.
- **Missing: west Jutland's sand → Part H.** The heath, the nineteenth-century reclamation, and the
  different farming economies of east and west. (You had it in checkpoint 1, but not here.)
- **Missing: the thread.** "From great power to small state" gets its prologue here: Denmark as
  the northern edge of somebody else's continent, dependent on what moves through it. This is the
  same idea that was missing from checkpoint 2, so it is worth fixing in place.

## Work the material

### The page's end questions (5–6 October 2026)

**Recall**

1. *Who picked up the first recognised Hamburg-culture flint, where, and how long before the site was dug?*
   > It was Jørn Fynbo, who on a walk found an odd-looking piece of flint. He was curious and at the
   > end handed it over to Haderslev museum, where it was for ... two decades?

   Who and where are right (Jørn Fynbo, south shore of Jels Oversø, 1968; Haderslev Museum). The time
   is wrong: recognised in **1980**, trial dig **1981**, so 12–13 years. "Two decades" is Slotseng's
   box (collected 1962, recognised 1985). This is the same merge as checkpoint 3. (§03)

2. *Where were the Allerød and Bølling warm phases first identified, and by whom?*
   > Two geologist was doing research near Allerød. Can't remember more of that in detail.

   Half. Allerød: June 1897, a brickworks clay pit at Allerød in north Zealand. The two were a
   geologist (**Vilhelm Milthers**) and a **botanist** (**Nikolaj Hartz**). They found a thin brown
   organic band with tree birch and elk: trees, so a warm interval inside the glaciation. Bølling is
   missing: **Johannes Iversen, 1942, Lake Bølling in Jutland**, which extended the scheme backwards.
   Both names are now standard across the Northern Hemisphere. (§05)

3. *Who pioneered the isotope method behind the Greenland ice-core climate curve?*
   > Denmark was the pioneer in using that method, which is a great method but needs to be balanced
   > against the changes in the environment throughout the period.

   No name: **Willi Dansgaard**, University of Copenhagen. Danish-led drilling made the reference
   records ("Denmark named the warm phases and built the instrument that measures them"). Your
   caveat is the page's, if made exact: ice-core climate zones and pollen biozones do not line up.
   At Trollesgave the pollen says late Allerød, while the calibrated dates fall in the ice-core cold
   interval. Greenland got cold before Zealand did, so it matters which clock a date was read from.
   (§06)

4. *What is a Lyngbyspids, and what sets Bromme points apart from what comes after?*
   > Lyngbyspids is the largest of the pointed heads found. It is like a separate technology line
   > that it is being refined to the maximum, until it dies out and is replaced by bow and arrow.

   The first half is right: the heaviest *tanged* point (skafttungespids), over 14 cm at its largest.
   The second half inverts the page. Bromme is not refinement to a maximum: its toolkit is
   **simpler** than what came before (fewer types, less refined blades). The bow is not new after
   it; it came **before**, with Federmesser, seems **lost** in Bromme (the big tanged points look
   like spearthrower darts), and **returns** with Ahrensburg's small light points. What sets Bromme
   points apart is size: even the short type is 4–6 cm, against the small points that follow. That
   bow-lost-then-back sequence is the puzzle the Laacher See hypothesis tries to explain. (§07, §08)

5. *What is the oldest known burial in Denmark, and when?*
   > Do not remember

   A cremation of a young woman at **Hammelev**, southern Jutland, c. **8,250 BCE**, after this page
   ends. The point of the question is §09's: **not one human bone** survives from the page's four
   thousand years. (§09)

**Causal**

6. *Why does a 22,000-year-old boundary still predict agricultural geography in 1900?*
   > The hovedopholdslinje had such a huge impact on the quality and composition of the soil, that
   > even after soil reclamation in the 19th century, it was vastly different production whether
   > the land was east or west of the hovedopholdslinje. Furthermore many choices and events
   > throughout history were impacted by that line, that it got settled deep in the makeup of the
   > country.

   Right on persistence, and better than the checkpoint. Still missing, for the third time, is the
   one-line mechanism: **clay holds water and nutrients; sand does not**. Reclamation changes the
   cover, not the substrate. "Many choices and events" is vague; the page names them: the
   **watershed** (rivers east of the line run east, west of it west), **Hærvejen** on the dry ridge,
   and in §10 the line still showing in soil maps, road networks and **farm sizes**. (§01, §10)

7. *Why are the earliest settlements in southern Jutland and on Lolland, not northern Jutland?*
   > In migrating from the south you naturally stop at the first useful place. Guesswork.

   Not guesswork, but half of it. The page has: (a) **from the south**: Denmark is reachable only
   from the south, and two routes show in the finds, one up eastern Jutland and one across Lolland
   towards Zealand and Skåne (Fig. 2). Lolland is on the way *to* Scandinavia, not a stopping point.
   (b) **The ice margin**: Jels and Slotseng sit almost on the line because tunnel valleys, an
   ice-margin feature, are where reindeer were funnelled and slowed: ambush ground (Fig. 1, §03).
   **The page is silent on northern Jutland itself.** Looked up: the Yoldia Sea covered large parts
   of North Jutland c. 17,000–14,600 years ago, with only the highest ground left as islands; in the
   Bølling (14,600–14,000) a later sea is known only in north-west Vendsyssel (lex.dk, "Yoldiahavet",
   https://lex.dk/Yoldiahavet; "Zirphæahavet", https://lex.dk/Zirph%C3%A6ahavet). So northern Jutland
   had only just come up from the sea when the first hunters arrived. That is context, not an
   explanation: neither article links it to settlement, and this distribution is of *known* sites.

8. *Why should a small isolated population lose complex technologies, and what does that say about
   knowledge without writing?*
   > As there is no other way of transferring knowledge than direct communication, the smaller the
   > population, the greater risk of losing information due to circumstances. And the more complex a
   > technology or piece of knowledge is, that harder it is to transfer said information.

   Right, and it is the page's argument (§07: a point "anthropologists have made since the 1910s";
   §10: knowledge "held in people's heads and passed on"). Two additions from the page. The mechanism
   is also about **contact**, not only size: Riede's case is that the eruption broke exchange
   networks, so the northern groups lost the wider pool they learned from. And the general rule is
   sound, but its use **for Bromme** is contested: Mortensen et al. read the simpler toolkit as an
   adaptation to richer wooded country, not a loss.

9. *Why is flint so central, and what does that distort?*
   > Flint is a hard, durable material, so it is what stays, whereas other things rot and disappears
   > of the millennia's. This in turn also make the knowledge biased towards flint, with the risk of
   > granting it bigger importance than it should, due to its survivability.

   Right. The page adds why there is so *much* flint (Danish chalk near the surface in the east,
   flint scattered through the moraine everywhere else) and what is missing beside it: wooden shafts,
   resin and sinew, hide tents. Antler survives only occasionally (the Odense implement, the Lyngby
   axe). The sharper distortion, which ties back to item 4: **the four cultures are themselves
   defined by flint types**. The units the page's story is told in are flint typologies, which is
   why the Brommeproblemet (is Bromme a real unit?) is possible at all. (§04, §07)

**Counterfactual**

10. *Suppose the ice had stopped 100 km further west, leaving all of Jutland under moraine.*
    > The quality of the soil for half the country would be much better, giving the whole area a
    > better economic base in broad terms. So while you could argue that it would future Denmark a
    > stronger base to become a great country, at the same time Jutland would have become a more
    > enticing target. While the islands were easy for a sea-nation to defend, Jutland not so much.
    > So changing such a fundamental thing are likely to alter how Denmark would look today.

    The soil half is right. "Half the country" overstates it: the area is central and west Jutland
    west of the line. The *target* argument does not follow from soil. Jutland's weakness is its land
    border with the continent, and that is the same whatever the soil; the page's thread already makes
    Denmark "reachable only from the south". The answer also stays general ("likely to alter"), where
    the book lets you be specific about **how far forward**. Things that go: the heath, so no
    nineteenth-century reclamation (§01; → Part H) and no Hedeselskabet, founded 1866 after the 1864
    defeat to drain and plant the heath (chapter 34). The watershed and Hærvejen's line move (§01).
    The east–west farming difference "into living memory" goes (§01, §10). Chapter 03 shows the
    line reappearing as a **cultural frontier**: megalithic tombs on the eastern moraine, Single
    Grave barrows on the western sand. So the change reaches from c. 3,000 BCE to the twentieth
    century, by the book's own cross-references. The outwash plains themselves would have been laid
    down on what is now North Sea floor, and drowned with Doggerland (chapter 02).

11. *Suppose the Younger Dryas had lasted another two thousand years. Later farming, or farming from
    another direction?*
    > It would probably come later, but not two thousand years later. Technology and ideas spread in
    > many different ways. And over time the time delay would equalize and might even give other
    > opportunities, as sometimes an idea arriving later gives a cleaner implementation.

    The instinct (not a full two thousand years) is right, but for a reason the answer does not give:
    **do the arithmetic**. The Younger Dryas ends c. 9,700 BCE; two thousand years more is c. 7,700
    BCE. Farming reaches Denmark c. **3,950 BCE**, almost four thousand years after even the extended
    cold. What set the date was not Danish climate. It was the farming frontier coming up from central
    Europe, more than a millennium *behind* central Europe, against a rich coastal hunter economy
    (Ertebølle) that had no need of it; it then came abruptly, with farmers of Anatolian-derived
    ancestry (chapter 02; chapter 03 §01). So the more likely answer is "neither": the same time, from
    the same direction (from the south, the page's thread). The real cost would fall on chapter 02,
    whose forest and coast would start two thousand years later. The caveat: the Younger Dryas was
    hemispheric, so the counterfactual also touches the Near East, where farming began; this page
    only sets the two side by side (§07, "Meanwhile in the world"). "Cleaner implementation" has no
    mechanism here; in this book farming arrives as people, not as an idea adopted.

12. *Suppose one Slotseng-style waterlogged site had preserved a burial. How much would change?*
    > My guess - not so much. Yes it would have given more insight to the culture at the time, but
    > would not change what actually happened at the time.

    Wrong, by the page's own account. It is true that it would not change what happened, but the
    page is not only about what happened: it is about **what can be known and how** (§06 is all
    method; §09 is "What we do not have"). One burial would rewrite at least these. §09's opening fact
    ("not a single human bone"). "There is no Danish ancient DNA from this period": with it, the page
    could test whether these people were GoyetQ2 or Oberkassel-type ancestry, and whether they left
    descendants, which §09 says "the Danish evidence can[not] currently answer at all". That in turn
    rewrites the myth "These were the first Danes". "No graves, so no grave goods, so almost nothing
    about belief, status, kinship" (§09). And §10's "We do not know what these people believed … how
    they were related". The page says what one find did for Britain: Gough's Cave has human remains,
    "which Denmark … conspicuously has not" (§03). The point of the question is that this page's
    hedging is set by its evidence; change the evidence and the hedges move.

**Contested** (6 October 2026; Carsten scanned the page first)

13. *Is the Laacher See hypothesis compelling, or a dramatic single cause? What would settle it?*
    > I find it a bit weak. Traces of ash, maybe some changes. I find that the simpler explanation of
    > local adaptation much more compelling, as that is the tendency throughout humanity existence:
    > we adapt. And I feel the Laacher See hypothesis demands several independent factors to be true
    > at once.

    A defensible position. The arguments for it hold in part.
    - **"Traces of ash" argues against a claim Riede does not make.** The page says the ash reaching
      Denmark was traces, so "the mechanism has to be social and demographic rather than direct" (§07).
      The hypothesis is about broken networks far to the south (the Thuringian Basin depopulating),
      not ash on Danish ground. This is the same slip as checkpoint 6 and the myth check: reading it
      as an ash-or-climate argument.
    - **"Several factors at once" is the strongest point, sharpened.** They are not independent
      factors but a *chain*: eruption → depopulation to the south → broken exchange networks →
      isolated northern groups → skills lost. Every link must hold, so the whole is weaker than any
      one link. Fair. But the rival has a chain too, and one link it has not shown.
    - **"We adapt" is a prior, not evidence, and it cuts both ways.** Losing skills when a group is
      cut off is *also* a human regularity: it is what you argued in Causal 8 yesterday. Both readings
      are adaptation: to a richer country (Mortensen), or to isolation (Riede).
    - **The adaptation reading's gap.** Mortensen, Henriksen & Bennike 2014 (abstract read): Bromme
      finds are concentrated in the wooded region, and the varied environment "allowed local
      adaptations using the diverse resources available"
      (https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00334-014-0433-7). That explains *where* Bromme
      people lived. In the abstract it does not explain the puzzle the page sets: why the **bow is
      dropped**, then **comes back** with Ahrensburg. A better-provisioned group giving up its best
      weapon needs an explanation of its own.
    - **Not either/or.** Both could be true: isolated groups settling the richest country available.
    - **What would settle it (missing from the answer; the second half of the question).**
      (a) **Order in time.** The hypothesis needs Bromme to begin *after* the eruption. The 2021/2025
      dating (13,006–13,008 BP) and the Trollesgave dates (c. 12,870–12,590 cal BP, §06) are
      consistent with that, but consistent is not proof. (b) **Where the simplification shows.**
      Network loss predicts simplification across the whole cut-off periphery; adaptation predicts it
      where the woodland was. (c) *My suggestion, not on the page:* **raw materials.** Broken
      exchange networks should show as fewer non-local materials in Bromme than in Federmesser.
      (d) **The Brommeproblemet.** If Bromme is only a regional Federmesser facies, there is less of
      a break to explain at all (§07).

14. *The Brommeproblemet: if 'Bromme' may not be a real unit, what does that say about the other
    named cultures, and about archaeological cultures as a concept?*
    > That any culture here and in general are not a physical entity but a definition. When is
    > something a separate culture? Can you make hard and firm rules, that do not lead to other weird
    > situations?

    Right. This is the page's point (§07, "a live disagreement about a real thing"). It can be pushed
    three steps further with the page and the book.
    - **What the definitions are made of.** All four are named for a find-place or a tool: Hamburg
      after finds at Hamburg (type-fossil the zinken), Federmesser after its "quill-knife" blades,
      Bromme after the site at Bromme, Ahrensburg after the tunnel valley where Rust dug Stellmoor
      (§03, §07, §08). They are **flint-typology units** (item 9), not peoples. The question "is
      Bromme real?" is really "is this toolkit different enough to deserve its own box?"
    - **The book's own proof that a culture is not a people.** Chapter 03 §01: Maglemose, Kongemose and
      Ertebølle are "three archaeological cultures, three changes of material world, one population".
      In the other direction, §09 here: a Europe-wide genetic turnover c. 14,000 years ago that no
      Danish flint type marks. Culture boundaries and population boundaries are separate things, and
      only genetics can test the second.
    - **The trap it sets on this page.** Figure 3 says the four cultures sit on four segments of the
      climate curve, "which is why" a thin population yields four named cultures. If the units are
      partly defined *by* their dates, part of that neat fit is built in, not discovered. That is a
      reason to read Figure 3 as illustration, not evidence.
    - **On "hard rules".** Rules can be made; the trouble is that any cut through a continuous,
      regional spread of toolkits produces edge cases, like Bromme as a regional facies. The better
      test is not "is it real?" but "is it **useful**: does the label predict a date range, a region
      and a toolkit that hold up?" (A data-model analogy: a category is a schema choice, judged by
      what it lets you predict, not by whether it exists in the world.)

15. *With no human remains, how confident may a museum label be? Where is the line between
    inference and storytelling?*
    > I think it is important to differentiate between facts and assumptions / inference. A good
    > story does make it more digestable for the average person. Just this chapter as it stands is a
    > bit dry to get through. BUT and it is a big but, to be clear about what we know, what we assume,
    > and what we guess. Most people would still take the story as fact, and that is just how it is.

    The three tiers are the right tool. Run them on the page and it shows it already uses them, mostly
    in the words it picks:
    - **Know (observed):** flint scatters; eleven reindeer in the Slotseng pond; two flint tips in bone;
      antler fragments that put the kill between late September and early December; not one human
      bone (§03, §09).
    - **Infer (a named step from evidence):** "small mobile bands" is, in the page's words, "a
      reasonable inference from camp size and toolkit, not an observation" (§09). Year-round presence
      is "plausible" (§03). Tents are "probably hide over a wooden frame; we have no certain example"
      (§04). The fifteen-metre range comes from throwing experiments (§03; Milks, David & Pope 2019).
    - **Guess:** population "dozens to low hundreds", "perhaps a hundred generations" (§10).

    **A test for the line.** It is inference if you can name the evidence *and* the step from it;

16. *If the ice-core clock and the pollen clock disagree about when the Younger Dryas began, what
    does 'the Younger Dryas' name: a climate event, or a local ecological one?*
    > I would say a local ecological one. Also puts the question about what these phases mean - that
    > often it is presented as hard blocks, but more often is a sliding scale. It goes back to the core
    > problem of human understanding wants to put things into boxes and categories, and reality is a
    > sliding grey masses. With the occasional unique defining event.

    Half right on the name's history; the newer evidence points the other way on the substance.
    - **The name was born ecological.** "Dryas" is a plant, *Dryas octopetala*, the arctic flower in
      the layers (§05 glossary): Older and Younger Dryas began as vegetation phases read from
      Scandinavian lake mud. The ice cores came later and borrowed the name for the cold interval in
      Greenland. One name now does duty for both, which is why the page says to ask "which clock it
      was read from" (§06).
    - **But the European signal is climate, not ecological lag.** Engels et al. 2022 (Communications
      Earth & Environment, read): at Hämelsee in north Germany, vegetation change starts at **12,820
      cal BP**, "concurrent with the onset of changes in local climate", and the response was
      instantaneous and synchronous across north-west Europe. The same paper puts Greenland's onset
      at **12,909 cal BP**, so north-west Europe's vegetation shift comes about 90 years later, and it
      says earlier claims of centennial offsets or a north–south spread arose from dating uncertainty
      (https://dspace.library.uu.nl/handle/1874/421460). So the plants were not slow. The cold reached
      Europe later than Greenland, or the clocks still disagree by that much. That is consistent with
      the page ("Greenland got cold before Zealand did", §06), and does not show it wrong; the paper
      does not mention Denmark or Trollesgave.
    - **So the better answer:** a climate event, recorded in different archives, arriving at slightly
      different times in different places. "Local" applies to the timing, not to the kind of event.
    - **On "sliding scale": not here.** The page calls the onset a drop "in a matter of decades" and
      the end "within something like a human lifetime" (§08), and Engels finds the plants answering
      instantly. This is one of your "occasional unique defining events". Set against item 14 it
      makes a useful contrast: **cultures** are fuzzy boxes drawn on gradual toolkits; **this stadial
      has sharp edges**, and the fuzziness is in our clocks, not in the event.
