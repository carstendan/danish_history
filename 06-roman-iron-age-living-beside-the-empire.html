Living beside the empire

c. 1 – 400 CE · romersk jernalder · the Roman Iron Age

No Roman army, administration or settlement ever existed in Denmark, and the nearest frontier post lay several hundred kilometres south. The country was nonetheless, for four centuries, comprehensively inside the Roman economy — and it is here that Danish elites first learn to get rich on somebody else's war.

Introduction

WHAT THIS PAGE ANSWERSFive questions

  1. How does an empire change a country it never enters?
  2. What did Denmark have that Rome wanted — and how comfortable is the answer?
  3. What does it mean to find the complete equipment of a defeated army of a thousand men at the bottom of a lake?
  4. When do people here start writing, and what is the first thing they write?
  5. Why does wealth suddenly concentrate in a few places, and what happens to those places next?

01 / NARRATIVEThe empire next door but one

Danish terms in this section

romersk jernalder
The Roman Iron Age, c. 1–400 CE; divided into ældre (to c. 160/175) and yngre.
limes
The Roman frontier; here the line of the Rhine and Danube.
Germania libera
'Free Germania' — the Roman term for everything beyond that line.

Chapter 5 closed with Roman armies reaching the Elbe and then falling back after the disaster in the Teutoburg Forest in 9 CE. From then on the frontier runs along the Rhine and the Danube, and it stays there.

Denmark is a long way north of it. From the Danish border country to the legionary base at Mogontiacum on the Rhine is around six hundred kilometres in a straight line, and rather more on the ground; to the Danube frontier at Carnuntum, further still. That is four or five weeks' travel across country nobody controlled. No legion ever came here, no Roman administration was ever established, no tax was ever collected. On any map of the empire, this page happens off the edge.

And yet the four centuries after 1 CE transform Denmark more thoroughly than anything since farming arrived. Wealth concentrates. A recognisable aristocracy appears and buries itself with imported silver. Armies of a thousand men cross the sea and are destroyed. Writing arrives. Villages grow into something you could call estates. Every one of those changes traces back, directly or at one remove, to the existence of Rome.

This is a pattern worth naming, because the series will meet it repeatedly: Denmark's history is frequently made by a large power that has never heard of it. The Roman economy here, the Hanseatic League in chapter 18, the Reformation in 20, the Napoleonic wars in 30, the two world wars at the end. Small countries are shaped by weather systems generated elsewhere.

02 / NARRATIVEWhat went south

Danish terms in this section

trælle
Slaves or thralls — the Danish word covers unfree people of every kind.
huder og skind
Hides and furs, the bulk northern export.

Trade needs two directions, and the northern one is the harder to see, because it consisted of things that rot.

Hides and furs, above all: the empire's appetite for leather was enormous, and a Roman army ran on it — tents, boots, straps, shield covers, armour backing. Cattle on the hoof. Amber, which had been going south since chapter 4 and now revives sharply, with a Roman writer recording an expedition sent north specifically to fetch it for the arena. Perhaps goose down, wool, dried fish.

And people. This is the part that Danish popular accounts have historically hurried past, and it should not be hurried past. The Roman world consumed slaves in extraordinary numbers, and it bought them at the frontier. Germania was one of the principal sources. Every one of the wars that this page is otherwise about — the wars whose losing equipment ends up in Danish lakes — produced captives, and captives were the single most valuable movable commodity a northern chieftain could lay hands on.

Danish researchers have argued in recent years that the east Jutland warriors who deposited a thousand men's gear at Illerup were very likely selling the men who had carried it. The archaeological evidence for this is necessarily indirect: you cannot excavate an absence. But the Roman demand is documented, the northern supply of prisoners is implied by the deposits themselves, and the wealth flowing north has to have been paid for with something.

Denmark's first appearance in a European economy, in other words, is as a supplier of raw materials and human beings to a slave-owning empire. The overseas empire (27) will find the country on the other end of the same trade, seventeen centuries later, running plantations in the Caribbean. It is worth holding both facts at once.

03 / NARRATIVEWhat came north

What came back is, by contrast, extremely visible, because much of it was made of bronze, silver and glass, and much of it went into graves.

The characteristic import is a drinking service: a bronze bucket for mixing wine, a ladle and a strainer, a jug, a shallow basin for washing hands, and cups. Glass beakers and bowls arrive, some of them extremely fine. Roman silver appears in small quantities. Coins arrive in some numbers — several thousand Roman coins are known from Danish soil, mostly silver denarii — though they were almost certainly not used as money here. They were bullion, ornament, and proof of connection.

The distribution is uneven in a way that is itself informative. Roman imports are not spread thinly across the country; they cluster in the graves of a few people, in a few districts, and they increase over time. Chapter 5's cemeteries were flat, plain and egalitarian in appearance. Within two centuries a small number of burials contain more portable wealth than an entire Pre-Roman Iron Age graveyard.

The wine service matters more than the wine. Communal drinking was already central to northern social life; what Rome supplied was the equipment to do it in a way that displayed access to something nobody local could make. The Roman objects were not adopted because they were Roman. They were adopted because they were useful in an argument about status that was already going on.

How the objects arrived is a mixed question, and the mixture is the interesting part. Some came by trade. Some came as diplomatic gifts — Rome's standard frontier policy was to cultivate friendly chieftains beyond the line with presents, creating obligations and potential allies. Some came as plunder from raids on the frontier. And some came in the baggage of men who had served in the Roman army as auxiliaries and gone home, which is the likeliest route for much of the military equipment we are about to meet at the bottom of a lake.

04 / NARRATIVEHoby: a Roman dinner service on Lolland

The man with the silver cups

He was young — somewhere in his twenties or thirties — and unusually tall for the period, around 187 centimetres. Isotope analysis says he grew up on Lolland or near it. He died in the first century CE and was laid in a wooden coffin two metres down, with a joint of pork, a bronze belt, brooches and a gold ring.

And with a complete Roman dinner service: a bronze tray, a bucket for mixing wine, a ladle, a jug, a shallow basin for washing the hands before eating, and two large cups of silver. It is a set. Somebody who understood Roman dining conventions assembled it, and somebody on Lolland was buried using it.

The cups weigh about a kilogram each and are worked in high relief with scenes from the Iliad: on one, Priam kneeling before Achilles to beg for the body of his son Hector, with Achilles' soldiers asleep around them; on the other, Philoctetes, abandoned on his island with a suppurating snakebite, and Odysseus trying to get Heracles' bow away from him. They are signed — Cheirisophos, a Greek name, the master silversmith, working in Italy for Roman buyers. And scratched underneath is a Roman name: Silius.

He came up in 1920, when a trench was being cut for the sewer of a newly built house just outside the village. The diggers struck the tray, saw the cups standing on it, and did what anyone would do — kept digging to see what else was down there, and sent for the National Museum afterwards. A certain amount of information was lost that afternoon. What survived is still the richest Roman import grave known anywhere north of the frontier.

The Hoby grave and its silver cups · Lolland · found 1920

That scratched name is the reason the Hoby grave is famous beyond archaeology. Gaius Silius was commander of the Roman forces on the Upper German frontier, based at Mainz, between roughly 14 and 21 CE. If the Silius of the cups is that Silius — and the identification is plausible rather than proven — then we are looking at a set of plate that belonged to a Roman general on the Rhine and ended up in a grave on Lolland within a generation.

The usual explanation is diplomacy. Rome bought quiet on its frontier with gifts to Germanic leaders, and gifts of this quality bought serious obligations. Whether the Hoby man received them directly, or whether they passed north through two or three hands as one chieftain gave them to another, cannot be recovered.

Either way, consider what he had. Two silver cups depicting scenes from a poem he had certainly never read, in a language he could not speak, from a war fought a thousand years earlier at the far end of a world he had probably never visited. He cannot have known what the pictures meant. He knew exactly what they were worth.

Meanwhile in Europe

The empire this page orbits was at its height. Trajan pushed the frontiers furthest around 117 CE; Hadrian consolidated and built walls. Then the second century turns. The Marcomannic Wars of 166–180 saw Germanic peoples break across the Danube in force and reach northern Italy — the first time in centuries — and Marcus Aurelius spent most of his reign fighting them. The Antonine plague killed an unknown but very large number of people across the empire.

The third century brought civil war, currency debasement, and the near-collapse of the imperial system, before Diocletian and Constantine rebuilt it in a different shape. Constantine legalised Christianity in 313 and the capital moved east to Constantinople in 330.

These convulsions register in Danish bogs. When the empire's silver got worse, so did the silver arriving here. When frontier warfare intensified, so did the supply of Roman weapons to the north.

05 / NARRATIVEHimlingøje and the Zealand connection

In the second and third centuries the pattern of wealth changes. Instead of scattered rich graves, one district on eastern Zealand starts to accumulate almost everything.

Around Himlingøje, on the Stevns peninsula south of Køge, lies a cluster of burials extraordinary by any northern European standard: Roman silver cups, gold arm-rings and neck-rings, glass, elaborately worked brooches. The graves run over several generations, and the objects in them are not random imports but a consistent, curated set, several of the finest pieces apparently made by the same workshops.

The standard interpretation is that a single family or dynasty controlled the flow of Roman goods into the whole Baltic region for the better part of two centuries, and did so from Stevns. Similar objects turn up around the Baltic and into what is now Poland and Norway — distributed, on this reading, by the Zealanders as gifts that created obligations, exactly as Rome had done to them.

If that is right, then Denmark's centre of gravity has moved. The Bronze Age was densest in north-west Zealand and Jutland; the Iron Age power that matters now sits on the strait between Zealand and Skåne, controlling the entrance to the Baltic.

Two details carry most of the weight. The first is that the objects are not a random accumulation of imports. Several of the finest silver cups appear to come from the same workshops, and the brooches include types made specifically for this group — which suggests not a family that bought well but one that was being supplied, deliberately, from inside the empire. Rome cultivated useful people beyond its border, and somebody on Stevns was useful.

The second is where the related objects turn up. Ornaments of the same distinctive types are found around the southern Baltic, into what is now Poland, and up into Norway and Sweden. The standard reading is that the Zealanders were not consuming these things but redistributing them — passing Roman goods onward as gifts, in exactly the way Rome had passed them to Zealand, and creating the same obligations at one remove. If that is right, an eastern Danish family spent two centuries operating as the northern end of a Roman patronage system, and got rich on the markup.

It should be said that "dynasty" is doing interpretive work here. What exists in the ground is a dense cluster of exceptionally rich graves over several generations in one small district. Reading that as a single ruling family, with a policy, is an inference — a reasonable one, and the kind of inference that becomes a fact through repetition. The contested questions at the end of this page come back to it.

What is not in doubt is the geography. Look at a map of Danish history and this position keeps producing power: Himlingøje here, Lejre and Roskilde in the Viking Age, Copenhagen from 1167, the Sound Dues from 1429 to 1857. Whoever holds the narrow water between Zealand and Skåne can tax everything that passes between the Baltic and the North Sea. It is the country's most reliable asset, and this is the first time we can see anyone getting rich from it.

06 / NARRATIVEIllerup Ådal: an army in a lake

Danish terms in this section

krigsbytteoffer
War-booty offering — the destroyed equipment of a defeated army, deposited in water.
skjoldbule
Shield boss: the metal dome over the handgrip, and the clearest rank marker in the find.
pragtskjold
'Splendour shield' — the silver-fitted shields of the highest-ranking men.

Drainage, and then a motorway

In May 1950 a wet meadow in the Illerup valley north of Skanderborg was being drained so that the land could be farmed properly. As the water was pumped away the peat sank, and iron began coming up almost at the surface.

Moesgaard Museum sent P.V. Glob — the same man who would later write the book that made the bog bodies famous in chapter 5 — together with Harald Andersen. Between 1950 and 1956 they excavated 750 square metres and recovered around 1,200 objects, and understood fairly quickly that this was something exceptional.

Then it stopped, for nineteen years. What restarted it, in 1975, was the planned route of the E45 motorway. The second campaign ran for eleven years under Jørgen Ilkjær, who has worked on the material ever since, and it excavated 40,000 square metres — about forty per cent of what had been a lake of ten hectares.

Over fifteen thousand objects have come out of that forty per cent. If the rest of the lake is like the part that has been dug, thirty thousand or more are still down there.

P.V. Glob and Harald Andersen, 1950–56 · Jørgen Ilkjær, 1975–85

The bog at Illerup is alkaline, which is unusual and enormously consequential. Acidic bogs of the kind in chapter 5 preserve skin and hair and dissolve bone; this one does the reverse. Metal, wood, bone, antler and plant fibre survive; leather and wool are gone. It means we have the weapons, the shafts, the shield boards, the combs and the rope — nearly everything except the clothing.

Four separate depositions took place in the lake. The first and largest was around 205 CE; a second followed roughly a generation later; then further offerings around 375 and around 450, by which time we are into the Germanic Iron Age (7).

The 205 deposit is the equipment of a defeated army of more than a thousand men. Nearly two hundred swords. Over three hundred spear and lance heads. Shields, belts, scabbards, and the small personal things soldiers carry — around 124 combs, and about 129 fire-strikers. Horses and horse gear. All of it deliberately destroyed: blades bent and hacked, shafts snapped, then thrown into the water.

It is the small things that make it real. Combs, in their scores, of antler — men who cared about their hair. Fire-strikers, one per soldier, because every man had to be able to make fire. Knives, whetstones, tweezers, a wooden plane. At Vimose, the comparable deposit produced gaming pieces, dice and boards: an army that played board games in camp. Rope, textile fragments, and the fittings of horse harness, with the horses themselves killed and put in alongside.

Sorted together, the deposit is not an arsenal but a body of men — what they fought with, what they groomed with, what they gambled with, and what they rode.

The scale of the battle behind it must have involved at least two thousand men. Nothing written records it. Nobody knows what it was about.

07 / NARRATIVEReading an army

What makes Illerup more than a spectacular pile of iron is that the equipment can be sorted, and when it is sorted it turns out to be organised.

The shields divide into three grades. Five of them had fittings of solid silver, with gilded decoration and applied masks — the pragtskjolde, the shields of the commanders. Below them, a larger group with bronze fittings: the chieftains' retinues. Below those, the great majority, with plain iron. The proportions are roughly what you would expect of a command structure with a handful of leaders, a body of professional followers, and a mass of ordinary fighters.

The same three-grade pattern appears in the weapon deposits at Vimose on Funen and Thorsberg just south of the modern border. Armies across the whole of northern Europe, over several centuries, were built to the same hierarchical template. That is a genuinely important piece of social history recovered entirely from metal fittings.

Then there are the swords. A substantial number of the Illerup blades carry Roman workshop stamps. These are not copies; they are imperial products, made inside the empire and carried north. Whether they arrived as trade goods, as plunder, or in the hands of men returning from Roman service is exactly the sort of question the material cannot settle — but the third route is the most economical explanation for so many of them in one army.

Where did that army come from? On the evidence of equipment types and manufacture, the attackers appear to have come from western Scandinavia — the region that is now Norway. They crossed the sea, in numbers, with a fleet, and were destroyed by a local force in east Jutland which then gave the whole of their property to a lake.

A few kilometres down the same valley, at Alken Enge, excavation has recovered the remains of several hundred people — a battlefield deposit from an earlier phase, bones gathered up some time after death and put into the wetland. The valley was used for this for centuries.

08 / NARRATIVENydam: the boat that changed everything

Danish terms in this section

klinkbygget
Clinker-built: overlapping planks, here fastened with iron rivets rather than sewn.
åretold
Rowlock — the fitting that lets an oar pivot against the hull, and the whole point of what follows.

Among the weapon deposits there is one that contained ships.

Nydam Mose, on the Sundeved peninsula in southern Jutland, was excavated between 1859 and 1863 by Conrad Engelhardt. He found weapons in quantity, and he found three boats. One, of pine, did not survive. The one that did is of oak, about twenty-three metres long, and dates to around 310–320 CE.

The Nydam oak boat is the hinge between the Hjortspring boat (5) and the longship (10). The Hjortspring boat of 350 BCE was sewn together with bast and driven by paddles. The Nydam boat is klinkbygget — overlapping planks fastened with iron rivets — and it is rowed. It has rowlocks: fittings that let an oar pivot against the hull so that a rower can put his back and legs into the stroke instead of his arms.

That single change transforms what a northern boat can do. A paddled vessel is limited by the reach of a man's arms; a rowed vessel multiplies the power available and can carry far more. Set a mast in a hull like this — which nobody here does for another three hundred years — and you have a longship. Every Viking crossing in chapters 8 to 11 is descended from the technology in this bog.

An excavation interrupted by a war

Engelhardt was working at Nydam through the early 1860s, in a Denmark that still included the duchy of Schleswig. In 1864 the Prussian and Austrian armies came north, the Danish position at Dybbøl fell some fifteen kilometres from the bog, and the duchies were lost.

The excavation stopped. The finds — the boat included — were taken by the victors, and the Nydam boat has been in Schleswig ever since. You can go and see it today at Schloss Gottorf, in Germany, a little over an hour's drive from the Danish border.

It is the only object in this series so far whose present location was decided by a war that will not be described for another twenty-five chapters. 1864 (33) is about that war. When you get there, it is worth remembering that the oldest rowed ship in northern Europe is one of the things Denmark lost in it.

Conrad Engelhardt · Nydam Mose, 1859–63 · interrupted 1864

09 / NARRATIVEThe first writing

Danish terms in this section

runer
Runes — the letters of the futhark, cut rather than written.
den ældre futhark
The elder futhark: the original 24-character alphabet, named after its first six letters.
urnordisk
Proto-Norse — the language the earliest runic inscriptions record.

Somewhere around the middle of the second century CE, people in the north began to write.

The script is the elder futhark, twenty-four characters, and it is clearly derived from a Mediterranean alphabet — Latin or a north Italic script, the specific parent still argued over. The letterforms have been redesigned for carving: made of straight strokes, with horizontals avoided, because a horizontal line cut across wood disappears into the grain. This is an alphabet engineered for a knife and a stick.

The earliest datable example comes from the weapon deposit at Vimose on Funen. It is carved on a comb, and it reads harja — probably a man's name, possibly a word meaning warrior or army. Around 150–200 CE.

Take a moment with that. The first thing anyone in this part of the world ever wrote down, so far as we can recover it, was a name, on a comb, belonging to a soldier who lost a battle.

Illerup produced nine runic inscriptions, on metal and on wood. Two lance heads carry the same name — WAGNIJO — once scratched, once in raised relief, meaning it was cast into the weapon at manufacture. And the same name appears at Vimose, on the other side of the country. A leader whose men carried marked weapons? A workshop's maker's mark? Two different men with a common name? It is one of the small, sharp puzzles of Danish prehistory.

Other Illerup objects carry Nithijo made on shield-handle fittings, and further names on a drinking horn, a plane and a fire-striker handle. What people wrote, when they first could, was who made a thing and who owned it.

10 / NARRATIVEThe farm that moved

Underneath the silver and the drowned armies, the ordinary settlement pattern is developing steadily, and one site tells the story better than any other.

Vorbasse, in central Jutland, was excavated over more than a decade by Steen Hvass — the same archaeologist who dug Hodde in chapter 5. What emerged was not a village but a sequence of villages: the same community, moving its buildings around within its own territory, roughly every century or two, from the birth of Christ to the Viking Age. Something like a thousand years of one settlement's biography, laid out in postholes across a large area of Jutland.

Through that sequence the farms get bigger. Early on, the individual holdings are modest and broadly comparable. By the later Roman Iron Age, some farmsteads have grown into fenced complexes with a main longhouse, outbuildings, workshops and room for a great many cattle — while others nearby have not. The gap that was a hint at Hodde has become a gulf.

That is the settlement expression of everything else on this page. A society producing surplus for export, run by families who control access to imported prestige goods and can call on armed followers, will show up in the ground as a few large farms and many small ones. It does.

Vorbasse also answers a question the rest of the chapter cannot. The weapon deposits show armies; the import graves show an elite; neither shows how anybody lived. Here the ordinary unit is legible: a fenced farmstead with a longhouse of perhaps thirty metres, a byre for twenty or more cattle, outbuildings, a well, and workshops — including, in the later phases, iron smelting and silverwork on the premises. Craft was not yet concentrated in towns; it happened on the larger farms.

And the movement itself is informative. A settlement that relocates every century or two within its own land is not being driven off by anything. It is managing a resource — probably soil fertility, possibly building timber, possibly just the accumulated mess of a century of livestock — in a landscape it holds securely enough to leave and return to.

11 / NARRATIVEGudme: the place the gold went

In the later part of this chapter, a district in south-east Funen begins to behave differently from anywhere else in Denmark.

Inland lies Gudme — the name means, transparently, 'the gods' home'. Five kilometres away on the coast is Lundeborg, a beach site with evidence of ships being drawn up, cargo handled and craft work carried out seasonally. Between them, they constitute something Denmark has not had before: a central place, combining religious focus, elite residence, specialist production and long-distance trade in a single landscape.

The area has produced more gold than any comparable district in the country. Workshops there handled gold, silver, bronze and iron. Roman coins arrive in quantity. It looks like a capital, or at least like the seat of something that could organise a region.

The names are the strange part. Gudme is guð-heimr, the gods' home. The surrounding parishes include Gudbjerg — the gods' hill — and Galdbjerg, and a stream called Vældebæk that may carry a word for a sacred spring. A whole small landscape is named for the divine, and it is the same landscape that holds the hall and the gold. Whatever was happening at Gudme, the people who lived around it thought it was where the gods were.

Gudme belongs mostly to the next chapter — its great period is the fifth and sixth centuries, and the astonishing gold hoards of chapter 7 are its product. But it starts here, and it starts because of the trade this page has described. A place that can concentrate rule, cult, craft and long-distance exchange in one landscape is doing what a town will later do, several centuries before anybody in Denmark builds one.

12 / NARRATIVEThe end of the Roman order

Around 375 CE the Huns move west into the Pontic steppe, and the Germanic peoples of eastern Europe start moving with them. Over the following century the western Roman Empire comes apart: frontiers overrun, provinces lost, Rome itself sacked in 410, the last western emperor deposed in 476.

Denmark is far away from all of it and is affected by all of it. The supply of Roman goods changes character — silver denarii give way to gold solidi, and then to gold arriving as tribute and plunder rather than trade. The weapon deposits continue, at Illerup and Nydam and Ejsbøl, and the armies they represent get, if anything, larger.

The system that had organised the north for four centuries — an empire to sell to, serve, raid and imitate — dissolves. What replaces it is more chaotic, considerably richer in gold, and the subject of chapter 7.

Meanwhile in the world

The Han dynasty collapsed in 220 CE, ending four centuries of Chinese unity almost exactly in step with Rome's third-century crisis. Teotihuacan in the Valley of Mexico grew to well over a hundred thousand people, one of the largest cities on earth. The Gupta empire rose in northern India. Christianity went from a persecuted sect to the official religion of the Roman state within a single century.

Denmark, meanwhile, acquired writing, a warrior aristocracy and a taste for imported silver — and had a population that would have fitted comfortably inside Teotihuacan.

What that meant here is best seen at the place this chapter keeps returning to. Illerup Ådal received four separate armies' equipment over roughly two hundred and fifty years — around 205, a generation later, around 375, and around 450. The first two belong to the world described above: a Roman frontier a few weeks away, Roman swords in northern hands, a system that had held for centuries. The last two belong to a world in which the empire that made those swords was disintegrating.

The deposits do not get smaller. They get bigger. Whatever was happening in the fifth century, it was not a decline in the scale of organised violence in southern Scandinavia, and it was not a shortage of men willing to cross the water in numbers.

And then, shortly after 450, the practice stops. The lakes that had swallowed defeated armies since the Hjortspring boat went into a bog on Als eight hundred years earlier are simply no longer used for it. Nobody recorded the decision. Something about how victory was understood, or who had the authority to make an offering on that scale, changed — and the archaeological signal that has carried this chapter and the last one goes quiet.

What replaces it is gold, buried in fields rather than water, in quantities the Roman Iron Age never saw. That is the gold century (7), and the reason for it is that the empire on the other end of the road was coming apart.

Myth-check

"Roman history has nothing to do with Denmark."
The usual assumption, and the reason this period is skipped in most popular accounts. No Roman army or administration ever existed here — but four centuries of proximity to the imperial market produced a hereditary aristocracy, armies of a thousand men, the concentration of wealth on Stevns, and the alphabet.
"The weapons in the Danish bogs are local work."
A great many of the Illerup swords carry Roman workshop stamps. They are imperial products, made inside the empire, carried north — most economically by men who had served in the Roman army and gone home. The army that attacked east Jutland around 205 was substantially armed by Rome.
"Denmark had nothing Rome wanted except furs and amber."
It had people. The Roman world bought slaves at the frontier in large numbers, Germania was a principal source, and the wars represented by the weapon deposits produced exactly the commodity in demand. It is the least comfortable fact on this page and it belongs on it.
"Runes are a Viking script, and magical."
The elder futhark is in use by around 150 CE — six hundred years before the Viking Age. Its design is practical, not mystical: straight strokes for cutting, horizontals avoided because they vanish into wood grain. The oldest datable inscription in Denmark is a name on a comb.
"The bog deposits are religion, so they aren't history."
They are a religious act performed on a historical event. Illerup records a real battle involving thousands of men, fought for reasons nobody wrote down — and because the victors gave everything to the lake instead of recycling it, we can count the losers' army by rank.

Summary

IF YOU REMEMBER FIVE THINGSThe page in five

  1. Denmark was never Roman and was thoroughly reshaped by Rome: a market several hundred kilometres away restructured Danish society for four centuries.

  2. The exports were hides, cattle, amber — and slaves, taken in the wars whose losing equipment fills the Danish bogs.

  3. The Hoby grave on Lolland holds a complete Roman silver dining service, its cups signed by a Greek silversmith and scratched with the name of a Roman commander on the Rhine — most likely a diplomatic gift.

  4. Illerup Ådal preserves the destroyed equipment of an army of over a thousand men, deposited around 205 CE, sortable by rank into commanders, retinue and rank and file — the same structure found in weapon deposits across northern Europe.

  5. Writing arrives around 150 CE in the form of the elder futhark. The oldest datable inscription in Denmark is a name cut on a comb, lost with a defeated army.

Questions & discussion

FOUR KINDSWork the material

Recall

Did the facts land?

  • Roughly how far was Denmark from the Roman frontier, and what does that distance imply?
  • What was in the Hoby grave, and whose name is scratched on the cups?
  • How can the Illerup material be sorted by rank, and what are the three grades?
  • Why does bone survive at Illerup when it dissolves in the bogs of chapter 5?
  • What is the oldest datable runic inscription from Denmark, and what does it say?

Causal

Why did that follow from this?

  • Why would an empire give silver plate to chieftains beyond its border rather than conquering them?
  • Why does access to imported goods produce a hereditary aristocracy, when access to local iron in chapter 5 did not?
  • Why is a runic alphabet made almost entirely of diagonal and vertical strokes?
  • Why does wealth concentrate specifically on Stevns, and why does that position keep producing power for the next fifteen hundred years?

Counterfactual

Pull one thread and see what moves.

  • Suppose Varus had won in the Teutoburg Forest and the frontier had settled on the Elbe. Does Denmark become a border zone — and what would that have done to it?
  • Suppose the Illerup victors had kept the captured weapons and sold or reused them. What would we know about northern European armies, and how would the history of this period read?

Contested

Where researchers actually disagree — and where it gets uncomfortable.

  • How central was the slave trade to Danish wealth in this period? The evidence is indirect by its nature. What would count as evidence, and does the difficulty of proving it explain why the subject was neglected for so long?
  • The Silius identification is plausible and unproven. Museums present it as a good story; how should a label distinguish "possible" from "established", and does it matter?
  • Is the Himlingøje "dynasty" a real political entity, or a pattern archaeologists have imposed on a cluster of rich graves? What would distinguish the two?
  • Illerup records a war nobody wrote down. Is a war without a written record a historical event in the same sense as one with? What is lost — and is anything gained — when the only surviving account is the objects?

Go and look

STILL THEREPlaces you can visit

  • Moesgaard Museum, Aarhus The Illerup material, displayed as what it is: an army. Among the best archaeological displays in northern Europe.
  • Illerup Ådal The valley itself, north of Skanderborg, with paths down to the offering site. Part of it has been deliberately re-flooded.
  • Nationalmuseet The Hoby service, including both cups. Small objects; stand close.
  • Schloss Gottorf, Schleswig The Nydam boat, an hour south of the border. Also the Thorsberg deposit. Both were Danish finds in 1863.
  • Alken Enge Further down the same valley, by Mossø — the battlefield deposit of several hundred people.
  • Øhavsmuseet & Gudme The Gudme–Lundeborg landscape on south-east Funen, with the hall site marked in the field.
  • Vorbasse Nothing stands above ground — but the fields between Vorbasse and Hovborg are the most completely excavated thousand years in Denmark.
