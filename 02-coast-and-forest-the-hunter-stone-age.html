Coast and forest: the hunter Stone Age

c. 9,000 – 3,950 BCE · Mesolithic · ældre stenalder · jægerstenalderen

Five thousand years — an eighth of the whole span of this series — in which nobody farmed anything, and which produced some of the richest hunter-gatherer archaeology on earth. This is also where Denmark stops being a peninsula and starts being a country made of coastline.

Introduction

WHAT THIS PAGE ANSWERSFive questions

  1. What happens to people when open tundra turns into closed forest in the space of a few centuries?
  2. When and how did Denmark become islands — and what did the rising sea take with it?
  3. Why is the evidence for this period so extraordinarily rich, and why is half of it under water?
  4. Who were these people? For the first time in this series, we can answer that with faces, names of a sort, and a genome.
  5. Why did a way of life that worked for five thousand years end in about two hundred?

01 / NARRATIVEThe forest arrives

Danish terms in this section

jægerstenalderen
'The hunter Stone Age' — the Danish name for the whole Mesolithic.
urskov
Primeval forest; the closed, unmanaged woodland of the Atlantic period.
Ældre Lindetid
'Older lime period' — a Danish pollen-zone name, after the dominance of lime (linden).
urokse
Aurochs, the wild ox — extinct in Denmark during this period on the islands.

Around 9,700 BCE the cold ended, and it ended fast. Summer temperatures went from roughly 10°C to 14–15°C, and the last of the inland ice melted out of southern Scandinavia. The reindeer went north and the tundra went with them. What replaced it was, within a few human generations, unrecognisable.

The sequence is legible in pollen cores. First birch and pine — a light, open woodland you could see through and move through. Then hazel arrives and spreads explosively. Then, as the climate reaches its warm Atlantic optimum after about 7,000 BCE, elm, oak and above all lime take over, and Denmark becomes urskov: closed deciduous forest, tall, shaded, running from coast to coast. Danish pollen specialists call the period Ældre Lindetid, the older lime period, because lime pollen dominates. It was warmer than today by a degree or two.

For people, this was not obviously an improvement. Open tundra with migrating herds is easy hunting: the animals are large, they gather in numbers, and they follow predictable routes. Closed forest is the opposite. The big open-country animals thinned out — elk became rare, and the urokse disappeared from the islands entirely, partly hunted out and partly unable to be replaced once the islands were cut off. What the forest offered instead was red deer, roe deer and wild boar: solitary or in small groups, dispersed, hard to find under a canopy.

So the whole strategy changed. Instead of following one animal across a landscape, people learned to work a territory — to know where the hazel stands were in autumn, where the eels ran in September, where the seals hauled out, where the swans wintered. The Mesolithic in Denmark is a story of increasing local knowledge and decreasing movement, and it ends with people who barely moved at all.

02 / NARRATIVEMaglemose: hunters of lake and woodland

Danish terms in this section

Maglemosekulturen
The Maglemose culture, c. 9,000–6,400 BCE, named after a bog at Mullerup on Zealand.
mikrolit
Microlith: a tiny worked flint set into a shaft, several to an arrow.
Åmosen
A large west Zealand bog complex, the richest Mesolithic landscape in Denmark.
stammebåd
Logboat or dugout canoe, hollowed from a single trunk.

In 1900 Georg Sarauw excavated a settlement in a bog called Maglemose, near Mullerup on western Zealand. It gave its name to the culture and, in time, to a whole north European period: Maglemosian sites are now known from England to Poland.

These were inland people, camped by lakes and rivers in the light early forest. Their signature tool is the mikrolit — a small geometric flint, sometimes only a centimetre or two, set several at a time into a wooden arrow shaft with resin. Instead of one large point that breaks and must be replaced, you get a composite edge whose parts are individually cheap. It is a different economic logic, and it appears wherever people start hunting dispersed forest game.

They also worked wood seriously. Bows of elm around 160 cm long survive from Danish bogs. So do paddles: the oldest, from around 7,100 BCE, are the earliest evidence of watercraft in Denmark, and where there are paddles there were stammebåde. In a country of dense forest, lakes and rivers were the roads.

And they had dogs. Domestic dogs appear in Danish Maglemose contexts, the first animal to enter a partnership with people here — some four thousand years before any other.

The archaeology is concentrated in Åmosen on west Zealand, a huge bog basin where waterlogged conditions preserve wood, bark, bast and bone. It is also where the first identifiable Dane appears. At Hammelev in southern Jutland, a young woman was cremated and buried around 8,250 BCE — the oldest known grave in the country. After four thousand years in which chapter 01 could not produce a single human bone, we now have a burial. It is not much. But it is a person, and somebody put her there deliberately.

03 / NARRATIVEThe sea takes the country

Danish terms in this section

transgression
A rise of the sea relative to the land, drowning former coastline.
Littorinahavet
'The Littorina Sea' — the salty, warm Stone Age sea, named after a periwinkle.
landhævning
Land uplift; the crust rebounding after the weight of the ice was removed.

All that melting ice went somewhere. Through the early part of this chapter, global sea level rose by tens of metres, and the plain that had joined Denmark to Britain went under.

It is worth being clear about that last point, because Doggerland has attracted a lot of catastrophe writing. The Storegga slide was real and enormous: run-up heights of up to twenty metres have been estimated in Shetland, deposits are traced from Greenland to Denmark, and recent modelling suggests western Jutland took an unexpectedly hard hit. But the current view is that Doggerland was largely under water before the wave arrived. The land was lost the way land is usually lost — a generation at a time, a field, a camp, a river mouth, until people stopped going back.

The Baltic, meanwhile, went through its own contortions. The ice-dammed lake of chapter 1 drained and refilled, opened to the sea and closed again, running through the stages Danish geology names after the shells found in each. By around 6,500 BCE the sea broke through the Danish straits for good, and the warm, salty Littorinahavet flooded in. Oysters, which need proper salinity, arrived with it — and that single ecological fact is going to shape the rest of this page.

The country turns inside out

The consequence for Denmark was not simply that it got smaller. It got infinitely more coastal. Every fjord and inlet on the map today was cut then, and many more that have since silted up. A peninsula with a lot of interior became an archipelago in which almost nowhere is far from salt water. If you want one geographical fact to explain the next ten thousand years of Danish history — the boats, the trade, the tolls, the fleets, the fact that a country this small was ever a naval power — it is this one.

There is a complication, and it is the reason Danish Stone Age archaeology looks the way it does. The land was moving too. Freed of the ice's weight, the crust was rebounding, and it rebounded most where the ice had been thickest — in the north and east. So in northern Denmark the land rose faster than the sea, and Stone Age shorelines now lie inland and above water. In the south the land sank, and Stone Age shorelines are now under the sea.

04 / NARRATIVEKongemose: the move to the shore

Danish terms in this section

Kongemosekulturen
c. 6,400–5,400 BCE, named after a site in Åmosen on west Zealand, excavated in the 1950s.
rombisk pilespids
Rhombic arrowhead — the culture's diagnostic point.
køkkenmødding
Kitchen midden: a mound of discarded shells and food waste at an old shoreline.
strømsted
A narrow, fast-running channel in a fjord — prime fishing ground.

The Kongemose culture is the hinge between forest and coast. Its settlements sit at strømsteder, the narrow places in fjords where current concentrates fish, and the bone assemblages read like a fishmonger's slate: flatfish, eel, garfish, spurdog, cod, pike. Inland, they still hunted red deer, roe deer, beaver and boar in the surrounding forest.

The tools change: long blades, rhombic arrowheads, and bone daggers edged with inset flint and carved with geometric patterns. An antler axe-haft recovered from Værebro Å carries carefully incised chequerboard and triangle motifs. Whatever else these were, they were not purely functional.

And around 5,600 BCE something appears for the first time that will define the next two thousand years: the oldest Danish køkkenmøddinger, along the Limfjord. People had begun to sit still long enough that their rubbish accumulated in one place.

05 / NARRATIVEErtebølle: the rich coast

Danish terms in this section

Ertebøllekulturen
c. 5,400–3,950 BCE, named after a shell midden at Ertebølle on the Limfjord.
skaldynge
Shell heap — the plainer Danish word for a køkkenmødding.
tværpil
Transverse arrowhead: a chisel-edged point, the culture's signature.
nålebinding
A single-needle looping technique, older than knitting; used here with lime bast.
lystre
Fish spear or leister, with barbed prongs.

Everything comes together in the Ertebølle culture, and the result is one of the most comfortable, materially rich hunter-gatherer societies known anywhere.

Start with the middens, because they are the visible monument. The one at Ertebølle on the Limfjord was originally about 140 metres long, 20 metres wide and nearly two metres thick, accumulated over roughly a thousand years of repeated occupation. One at Bjørnsholm ran to 325 metres. They are overwhelmingly oyster shell — it has been calculated that the Mejlgård midden on Djursland held something like ten thousand oysters per cubic metre, which over a thousand years of use adds up to on the order of twenty million oysters eaten at a single spot.

The shells matter for a second reason. Shell is calcium carbonate, and it keeps the soil alkaline, which is why bone survives in a midden when it dissolves everywhere else in acidic Danish ground. The middens are not just evidence; they are the reason we have evidence.

What they ate, and how they got it

Isotope measurements on human and dog bone show a diet drawing very heavily on the sea — comparable, on the standard measures, to historically recorded Arctic peoples. That was oysters, mussels and cockles, but far more importantly fish, taken with hook and line of lime bast, with lystre, and above all with fixed weirs of hazel and willow rods set across tidal channels. A weir is a serious capital investment; you build it where you intend to be next year. Beyond the fish came seal, porpoise, and occasionally whale; swans and ducks in winter; red deer, roe deer and boar from the forest; hazelnuts by the sackful.

The technology is the surprise. Ertebølle people made pottery — pointed-base cooking vessels and shallow oval lamps that probably burned blubber. In European terms this is remarkably early for a society that does not farm. They wove nothing, but they made textiles by nålebinding from plant fibre, fragments of which survive from Tybrind Vig. And they built boats that are, frankly, hard to believe.

Ten metres of lime

Three logboats were recovered from the drowned settlement at Tybrind Vig on west Funen. The best preserved, Tybrind Vig 1, was about ten metres long and fifty centimetres wide, hollowed from a single lime trunk. To get it, someone had to find and fell a lime tree at least twelve metres tall with a trunk eighty centimetres across, and then remove between ninety and ninety-five per cent of the wood — with flint.

The sides were worked down to a thickness of about two centimetres. Not roughly: consistently, along ten metres of a hollowed log, using stone tools. There is a groove cut in the gunwale at the stern where a transom board could be slotted in to close the open end, and in one boat a small hearth of sand and clay, probably for burning a light at night to attract eels.

They were paddled with broad, heart-shaped blades of ash. Several are decorated. One from Tybrind Vig carries a careful symmetrical design, and paddles from Horsens Fjord and Norsminde Fjord share motifs with each other but not with the Tybrind ones — which has led to the suggestion, unprovable but hard to resist, that you could tell where a boat was from by looking at its paddles.

Tybrind Vig 1, a lime logboat · west Funen · underwater excavation from 1978

It was not all coast. Inland sites show the other half of a seasonal system. At Ringkloster, at the north end of what is now Skanderborg Sø and some fourteen to eighteen kilometres from the east Jutland fjords, people hunted wild boar in the winter woods — and the site has produced the bone of a bottlenose dolphin, an animal that does not swim in lakes. Somebody carried it up from the coast. Whether Ringkloster was one group's winter station or a shared hunting camp used by several coastal communities is not settled, but either way the coast and the interior were parts of one economy, and people moved between them by water.

There is also a hint of something stranger. Wild boar bones from the coastal midden at Fannerup carry an isotope signal showing that the animals had been eating a substantial amount of marine food. Wild boar do not fish. The likeliest reading is that people were feeding them — from the midden, probably — which would make it a form of animal management some centuries before anybody here kept livestock.

Put it together and you get people who were largely sedentary, who invested in fixed structures, who owned heavy specialised equipment, who made pottery, who buried their dead in cemeteries beside their houses, and who could move a family group ten kilometres across open water. The old textbook opposition — mobile poor foragers, settled rich farmers — does not survive contact with the Ertebølle evidence.

Meanwhile in Europe

Farming had been moving north-west across Europe for two thousand years, out of Anatolia through the Balkans and up the Danube. By around 5,300 BCE the Linearbandkeramik farmers had reached the Rhine and the edge of the north European plain.

And then the frontier stopped. For something like a thousand to fifteen hundred years, there were farmers a few hundred kilometres south of the Ertebølle coast, and the Ertebølle people did not become farmers. They knew perfectly well what was happening: polished stone adzes and other southern objects turn up on Danish sites, and the arrival of pottery here almost certainly reflects contact with ceramic-using neighbours. They took the pot and left the cow. This is one of the more interesting refusals in European prehistory, and it is discussed at the end of this page.

Meanwhile in the world

Çatalhöyük in Anatolia was occupied through much of this period — several thousand people living in a warren of mudbrick houses entered through the roof, with no streets. Jericho was already ancient. In Mesopotamia, irrigated agriculture was beginning on the plains that would produce the first cities.

Ertebølle pottery is early for Europe but nowhere near a world first: Jōmon potters in Japan had been making vessels since around 14,000 BCE, and pottery in China is older still — also, and this is the point, made by hunter-gatherers rather than farmers. The old assumption that pottery arrives with agriculture is simply wrong, and Denmark is one of the places that shows it.

06 / NARRATIVEVedbæk: people we can look at

Danish terms in this section

okkergrav
Ochre grave — a burial strewn with red ochre, the colour of blood.
tandperler
Tooth beads: pierced animal teeth sewn onto clothing.
jordfæstegrav
Inhumation grave, as opposed to a cremation.

Stopping the bulldozer

In 1975 the ground was being cleared at Bøgebakken, near Vedbæk station north of Copenhagen, for a new school and its car park. The area was well known to local amateur archaeologists — there had been Stone Age finds around the old fjord, now the bog called Maglemosen, for decades — and they were watching the work closely.

When human bone began coming up in the machine's spoil, they got the bulldozer stopped and the building work held long enough for the National Museum to be called. What came out of the ground was a cemetery: seventeen graves holding twenty-two people, from newborns to adults in their forties and fifties, in use around 5,500–5,000 BCE. It is thought to be perhaps two-thirds of the original number; the rest had already gone under earlier construction.

It is now regarded as the best-preserved hunter-gatherer cemetery in northern Europe.

The Bøgebakken cemetery · Vedbæk, north of Copenhagen · 1975

Most of the dead had been laid on their backs, arms at their sides, legs sometimes so tightly together that they were probably bound. Nearly every grave had red ochre in it, heaviest around the head. They were buried in their clothes — you can tell from where the ornaments lie — and the clothes were sewn with tandperler, pierced teeth of red deer, roe deer, elk, boar, bear and aurochs. Bear teeth suggest contact across the water with Skåne, where bears still were.

Three graves are worth describing individually, because they are as close as this chapter gets to people.

In one lay a woman of about eighteen. Beside her right side was an infant, born or unborn at eight or nine months, laid on the wing of a swan. A small flint blade had been placed at the baby's waist — a marker, on the reading usually given, that the child was a boy. She almost certainly died in childbirth, and the child with her. Under her head was a concentration of tooth beads and pierced snail shells, long read as a folded garment used as a pillow; the museum's Peter Vang Petersen has more recently suggested it may be the remains of a baby-carrier, with a parallel in an engraved slate plaque from Gönnersdorf in Germany showing a woman carrying a child on her back.

In another lay a man in his fifties, resting on red deer antlers placed under his shoulders and hips. In a third lay three people together, one of them a man with a bone point through him.

Nearby, at Gøngehusvej, a double grave held a woman of about forty and a three-year-old child. She had survived a heavy blow to the back of the head at some earlier point in her life. By her head lay a bone pin and the bill of a grebe — probably all that remains of a cap made of bird skin. On her chest were two bone netting needles and the foot bones of roe deer, from a hide that had been wrapped around her.

The technical term for all this is grave goods. The plainer description is that people took trouble. A swan's wing for an infant who never drew breath is not a subsistence strategy.

07 / NARRATIVEAmber, ornament and the dog

Danish terms in this section

rav
Amber — fossil resin, washed up on Danish beaches; Denmark's other great natural asset.
okker
Ochre: iron-rich earth, burnt to a strong red, strewn in graves.
ravfigur
Amber figurine — small carved animals, among the oldest art from Danish soil.

Flint and antler are the workhorses of this period, but they are not the whole of it. From the bog at Engesvang, by Bølling Sø in central Jutland — the same lake that named the warm phase in chapter 1 — came small carved figures of rav. One is a bird, possibly a waterbird; another is an elk, reduced to a few confident planes. They are Ertebølle work, and they are among the oldest representational art securely known from Danish ground.

Amber matters beyond these figures. It washes up on the west Jutland beaches, it is light, it is warm to the touch, it burns, and it is found in quantity nowhere else in northern Europe. In this chapter it is being carved into beads and animals for local use. Three thousand years later it will be travelling to Mycenae, and Denmark's whole Bronze Age will be paid for with it.

The decoration on everyday objects points the same way. Kongemose bone daggers carry incised geometric bands; the antler axe-haft from Værebro Å has chequerboards and triangles; Ertebølle paddle blades are painted or carved with symmetrical designs. None of this is necessary. Somebody spent hours on it.

So does the ochre. Nearly every grave at Bøgebakken had okker in it, heaviest around the head and hips, and it may have coloured the clothing rather than the body directly. It is red, which is to say it is the colour of blood, and people across Eurasia were using it in graves for tens of thousands of years before this. It is the closest thing to a religious statement that survives from Mesolithic Denmark.

The dog

Dogs had been living with people here since the Maglemose period, and by the Ertebølle they were being buried. There is a dog grave at Gøngehusvej in Vedbæk; across the Sound at the contemporary cemeteries of Skateholm in Skåne, dogs were interred with grave goods, in some cases more carefully than some of the humans. Isotope work shows that dogs ate what people ate, marine diet and all.

It is worth pausing on that. Ten thousand years before anyone in Denmark kept a cow, a sheep or a pig, there was already one animal that lived in the camp, ate the same fish, worked with the hunters and was put in the ground with something to take along.

08 / NARRATIVEHow we know: two arguments and a diving suit

Danish terms in this section

køkkenmøddingkommissionen
The Kitchen Midden Commission — a government-appointed research team, the first of its kind.
affaldsdynge
Refuse heap; the neutral Danish description of what a midden is.

In 1837 the National Museum received a parcel of flint tools said to have come out of a heap of old oyster shells by the Limfjord. That parcel started an argument that ran for over a decade.

The zoologist and the archaeologist

The zoologist Japetus Steenstrup had been looking at Danish shell heaps since 1837 and thought he understood them. They were natural oyster banks, laid down by the sea and then lifted clear of the water by land uplift — which was a perfectly reasonable thing to think in a country where raised beaches are everywhere. The flint tools in them were incidental, dropped by people who had come to gather oysters off a bank.

The archaeologist J.J.A. Worsaae, twenty-one years old when he began publishing and already the man who had proved Thomsen's three-age sequence stratigraphically, thought they were something else entirely: communal eating places, the accumulated rubbish of tribes living nearby in the remotest antiquity.

They argued from 1848. In 1850 the government appointed a commission to settle it — Worsaae the archaeologist, Steenstrup the zoologist and Georg Forchhammer the geologist, three professors sent out to dig together. At Mejlgård on Djursland in 1851 the answer became plain. The shells were sorted by species and size in ways no tide produces; the layers alternated with charcoal and ash; the bones were of eaten animals. Steenstrup changed his mind and published that he had.

He also supplied the word. He called the heaps køkkenmøddinger — kitchen middens — and the term entered scientific English, French, German and Portuguese and is still in use worldwide. Not everyone was pleased at the time: one contemporary writer thought the name malodorous and tasteless, and a newspaper joked that Denmark could now boast to the world of its national rubbish heaps.

Forchhammer, Steenstrup and Worsaae · the First Kitchen Midden Commission, 1848–51

It is hard to overstate what this was. Three disciplines, appointed by a government, digging the same site together to answer a question none of them could answer alone. It is generally taken as the beginning of interdisciplinary archaeology anywhere in the world, and it happened in Denmark in 1851 because a zoologist and an archaeologist could not agree about oysters. A Second Kitchen Midden Commission followed in 1893–95, this time with botanists added, and it was that commission which excavated the great midden at Ertebølle in 1895 and gave the culture its name.

The second transformation came a century later and required rubber. Danish underwater archaeology began around 1950; a submerged Ertebølle settlement in Tybrind Vig was published in 1959 and then more or less forgotten. It was sport divers from Fredericia and Middelfart who returned attention to it in the late 1960s, by which time diving equipment had improved and storms had eroded fresh material out of the seabed. Systematic excavation began in 1978 and became the most extensive underwater investigation of a Mesolithic site anywhere.

The most recent chapter is infrastructure. The Fehmarn Belt tunnel to Germany runs through Syltholm Fjord on Lolland — reclaimed land now, a shallow lagoon then — and the archaeology done ahead of the construction has produced an extraordinary quantity of material from exactly this period. Including, as it turns out, a person.

09 / NARRATIVEWho they were

Danish terms in this section

birkebeg
Birch pitch: black tar made by heating birch bark, used as glue — and chewed.
Syltholm
A former fjord on southern Lolland, excavated ahead of the Fehmarn tunnel.

Genetically, the people of this chapter belong to the population geneticists call Western Hunter-Gatherers — the descendants of the group that spread across Europe at the end of chapter 1. And the reconstruction that comes out of their genomes is not what a Danish schoolbook of 1960 would have predicted. They had dark skin and dark hair, and blue or blue-grey eyes. The combination looks strange to modern eyes because it no longer exists in Europe; pale skin arrives later, mostly with farmers and with the steppe migrations of chapter 3 and beyond.

Lola

Somewhere on the shore of a shallow lagoon on what is now Lolland, a young woman finished a meal of duck, eel and hazelnuts, and then chewed a lump of birch pitch. Birch bark, heated, yields a black tar that goes soft when warm and sets hard when cool; people had used it as glue for arrowheads for tens of thousands of years, and they also chewed it — to soften it for use, possibly for the taste, possibly for the teeth. When she had finished, she spat it into the water.

It was dug up in 2015 by archaeologists from Museum Lolland-Falster working ahead of the Fehmarn tunnel. There are no tooth marks in it, but it is folded and twisted in exactly the way a chewed lump is. Somebody had the idea of testing it for DNA.

It yielded a complete human genome — the oldest yet recovered in Denmark, and the first time anywhere that an entire genome had been extracted from something other than bone or teeth. One researcher not involved in the work observed that the pitch held as much DNA as the best bones they normally get. She had dark skin, dark hair and blue eyes. She was lactose intolerant, like almost everyone then. Her oral microbiome came out with the genome, including Epstein–Barr virus. The meal came too: mallard and hazelnut.

The researchers called her Lola, after the island. The radiocarbon date is 3,850–3,650 BCE — which, as the next section explains, is a genuinely awkward number.

10 / NARRATIVEThe limits of the idyll

It would be easy to make this period sound like a paradise, and some popular accounts do. The skeletal evidence is more mixed.

Physically these were robust people. Bone density is high — the product of a life of hard physical work and a diet full of marine protein. Teeth are heavily worn, from grit and from using the mouth as a tool, but essentially free of caries: no cereals, no sugar. Against that, arthritis is common and starts early, and life expectancy was not long by any modern standard.

And there was violence. The triple grave at Bøgebakken held a man killed by a bone point. The woman at Gøngehusvej had taken a serious blow to the head and lived. Across the water at Skateholm in Skåne, the contemporary cemeteries tell similar stories. Sedentary, territorial societies with valuable fixed resources — a good weir site, an oyster bank, a fjord narrows — are societies with something to fight over, and the record suggests they sometimes did.

The Skateholm cemeteries in Skåne, contemporary with Vedbæk, have produced several individuals with embedded points or healed cranial injuries. At Bøgebakken itself the triple grave held a man killed by a bone point. Across the wider Baltic Mesolithic the rate of skeletal trauma is high enough that it cannot be dismissed as accident.

Think about why that should be. A mobile forager who runs into trouble can walk away from it. A community with a fixed weir at a fjord narrows, an oyster bed it has worked for generations, a cemetery beside its houses and a ten-metre boat drawn up on the beach cannot. Everything that made the Ertebølle rich also made it worth attacking, and gave it something to defend. Sedentism and violence arrive together, and the same combination will show up again at Hodde in chapter 5 and at Borremose behind its rampart.

Nor was the abundance evenly spread across the year. Oysters and fish are seasonal; the isotope signal that looks so impressive averages out a calendar that certainly had thin months in it. Infant burials are numerous at Bøgebakken and at Skateholm, which is normal for any pre-modern population and is worth stating rather than passing over.

This matters for how we read what came next. A rich, settled, successful society is not necessarily a stable one, and it is not obviously a society that needed rescuing by agriculture — but it was also not the idyll that a photograph of a swan's wing in a grave invites you to imagine.

11 / NARRATIVEWhy it ended

Around 3,950 BCE, within something like two hundred years, people in Denmark began keeping cattle and sheep, growing barley and wheat, clearing forest, and building the first monumental tombs. The Ertebølle way of life stops.

What makes this hard is that it was not for want of knowing. The farming frontier had been sitting a few hundred kilometres south for a millennium or more. Southern objects reached Danish sites. Pottery was adopted. There is even evidence, at the Muldbjerg settlement in Åmosen, that has been read as domestic cattle and cereal traces in a late Ertebølle context — though claims like that are contested, and contamination from later layers is a standing hazard in bog sites.

So the question is not why farming took so long to reach Denmark. It reached the neighbourhood promptly. The question is why the Ertebølle people declined it for forty or fifty generations, and then took it up almost at once.

The honest answer is that nobody is sure. The available explanations divide roughly into: they were doing too well to bother until something went wrong with the coastal economy — oyster beds shifting with salinity, perhaps, or population pressure; or the transition was less a decision than a movement of people, with farmers arriving in numbers; or it was a change in what people wanted, with cattle and grain functioning as prestige and as the material of feasting and obligation before they were ever the basis of subsistence. Ancient DNA has moved this argument a long way in the last decade — and the direction it points is discussed when farming arrives (3), because the answer is not comfortable.

Which brings us back to Lola. Her date, 3,850–3,650 BCE, falls after the conventional start of the Danish Neolithic. She is genetically a hunter-gatherer, with no detectable ancestry from incoming farmers, living on Lolland at a time when farming had already arrived in Denmark. She is a reminder that a line drawn on a chronology chart is a convenience, and that the people alive on either side of it did not know it was there.

Myth-check

"Stone Age Danes were blond and pale."
Dark skin, dark hair, blue or blue-grey eyes. The pale-skinned northern European is a later product, arriving with farmers and steppe populations. This is one of the most reliably surprising facts in Danish prehistory and it is now firmly established from multiple genomes.
"Hunter-gatherers were poor, hungry and always on the move."
The Ertebølle people were substantially sedentary, made pottery, built fixed fishing weirs, buried their dead in cemeteries, and ate a diet rich enough to build notably dense bone. They were materially better off than many later Danish peasants.
"They took up farming as soon as they heard about it."
Farmers were a few hundred kilometres away for a thousand years or more. Danish foragers adopted pottery and southern tool types and declined agriculture. The refusal lasted roughly forty generations.
"The Storegga tsunami drowned Doggerland."
It was a real catastrophe and it hit western Jutland harder than once thought, but most of Doggerland was already under water when the wave came. The land was lost slowly.
"Køkkenmøddinger are natural oyster banks."
A serious scientific position held by a serious scientist until 1851 — and worth remembering as an example of how a wrong answer can be reasonable. Steenstrup was not being foolish; raised beaches really are everywhere in northern Denmark.

Summary

IF YOU REMEMBER FIVE THINGSThe page in five

  1. Tundra became closed lime forest within a few centuries. Hunting large migrating herds gave way to working a known territory, and people moved less and less until they barely moved at all.

  2. The sea rose, drowned Doggerland, broke into the Baltic and turned a peninsula into an archipelago. Because the land was also rising in the north and sinking in the south, Stone Age sites are inland in north Denmark and underwater in the south.

  3. The Ertebølle culture was one of the richest hunter-gatherer societies known: hundred-metre shell middens, pottery, textiles, fixed fish weirs, ten-metre logboats, and a diet as marine as an Arctic people's.

  4. At Vedbæk in 1975 a cemetery of twenty-two people came out of the ground, including an eighteen-year-old woman buried with her newborn laid on a swan's wing. This is where Danish history acquires individuals.

  5. Farming was available and refused for a thousand years, then adopted in about two hundred. Nobody is certain why, and the answer involves who these people were as much as what they knew.

Questions & discussion

FOUR KINDSWork the material

Recall

Did the facts land?

  • Name the three cultures of the hunter Stone Age in order, with rough dates.
  • What is a køkkenmødding, and why does bone survive in one when it dissolves elsewhere?
  • Why are Ertebølle settlements underwater in southern Denmark but on dry land in the north?
  • What was found in the double grave at Bøgebakken?
  • What was Lola chewing, and what did it turn out to contain?

Causal

Why did that follow from this?

  • Why did the arrival of dense forest push people towards the coast rather than deeper into the woods?
  • Why does a fixed fish weir imply a different kind of society from a hunting bow?
  • Why did the breakthrough of salt water into the Baltic around 6,500 BCE matter so much to how people ate?
  • Why did an argument about oysters produce the world's first interdisciplinary archaeological commission?

Counterfactual

Pull one thread and see what moves.

  • Suppose the land had sunk everywhere in Denmark rather than rising in the north. How much of what we know about this period would we not know — and would the discipline of archaeology have developed differently here?
  • Suppose the Ertebølle refusal had held for another two thousand years. What would Denmark have looked like when metal arrived from the south?

Contested

Where archaeologists actually disagree.

  • Why did the Ertebølle people decline farming for so long and then adopt it so fast? Which of the standing explanations do you find most convincing, and what evidence would decide it?
  • Bøgebakken is often described in terms of tenderness and care. How much of that reading comes from the evidence and how much from us? What would a deliberately unsentimental description of the same grave look like — and would it be more accurate or just colder?
  • If the transition to farming turns out to be substantially a movement of people rather than of ideas, does that change how we should describe what happened to the Ertebølle population?
  • Lola is dated after farming arrives, yet is genetically a forager. How useful are period labels like 'Mesolithic' and 'Neolithic' if real people straddle them?
