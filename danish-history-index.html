Denmark,
from the ice to the welfare state

c. 13,000 BCE — 1953 · 43 chapters, 2 flagged for possible split · 6 threads · 11 maps · 31 pages written

The history of the land we now call Denmark, set in its European and global frame at every step: the Roman economy, the Baltic crusades, the Hanse, the Atlantic slave trade, the Napoleonic wars, and the two world wars.

Written in English, with Danish terms kept and glossed on first use. A layman's book, not a university syllabus. Chapters run 25–50 minutes — a band, not a target: length follows the number of distinct stories a chapter has to carry, not the number of years it covers.

The threads

Six questions that outlive any single era

Chronology alone hides the continuities. Each thread gets its own overview page, and each era page carries a short section on whichever threads it touches.

  1. The border in the south

    Danevirke → Ribebrevet 1460 → 1848 → 1864 → 1920 → today

    Denmark's only land border has been the country's central political problem for 1,300 years. Following it explains more Danish history than any single reign.

  2. Denmark and Sweden

    union partner → arch-enemy → neighbour

    Eleven wars between 1521 and 1814. The relationship swings from Kalmar to Roskilde to Nordic cooperation, and it defines what 'Denmark' territorially means.

  3. The sea as income

    amber · Sound Dues · neutral shipping · Mærsk

    A small country astride the entrance to the Baltic. Tolls, freight and neutrality trade have paid Denmark's bills since the Bronze Age, and shaped its foreign policy.

  4. The realm beyond Denmark

    Norway · Iceland · Faroes · Greenland · Skåne · Estonia · India · Ghana · the Caribbean

    For most of its history Denmark was a composite state, not a nation-state. Tracking the possessions — and how each was let go — is a history of the country's self-image.

  5. From great power to small state

    1397 → 1658 → 1814 → 1864 → 1940 → 1949

    A four-century contraction, and the intellectual work done to make it feel like a virtue. This thread carries the myth-checks.

  6. Faith and the state

    Norse cult → Catholic church → Lutheran state church → secular society

    Religion as instrument of state-building: Harald's conversion, the 1536 Reformation, Grundtvig's popular Christianity, and the folkekirke today.

Page anatomy

How every era page is built

Prime, tell, consolidate, retrieve. The introduction poses the questions the summary answers, so the loop closes.

  1. Header blockDates, 4–6 key names, and one line on why the period matters. Makes the page usable as reference later.
  2. IntroductionThe 3–4 questions this page answers. Primes what to look for.
  3. NarrativeThe main text. Danish terms kept and glossed on first use.
  4. VignetteA named person at a specific hour in a specific place. One to three per page. In prehistory these are usually the excavators; from the Viking Age on, people inside the events.
  5. Meanwhile in Europe / the worldA distinct box so the wider context doesn't get swallowed by the Danish story.
  6. MapsSVG, inline, no external files. Real Natural Earth coastlines, one shared projection.
  7. CheckpointTwo or three recall questions dropped mid-page, next to what they test — not saved for the end, when attention is lowest.
  8. Myth-checkWhere the schoolbook version is wrong, and what replaced it.
  9. CallbacksTwo or three pointers back to earlier chapters, so the whole spine reads as one story.
  10. SummaryIf you remember five things from this page, these. Closes the loop opened by the introduction.
  11. Questions & discussionRecall · causal · counterfactual · contested. The last kind is where the real work is.
  12. Sources & places to visitA light source line, plus what still stands that you can go and look at.

Conventions

Decisions already made

  • LanguageEnglish throughout. Danish terms kept where they carry meaning — stavnsbånd, enevælde, retsopgøret — and glossed on first use in each page.
  • BaselineAssumes Danish gymnasium history somewhere in the past. Key figures get a one-line reintroduction, not a full biography.
  • DeliverySelf-contained HTML files. No CDN, no external fonts, no network at view time. Download once and they keep working.
  • Length25–50 minutes per chapter. Governed by how many separate stories a page must carry, not by how many years it spans. Two chapters are flagged dense in the spine above; those are the ones likely to run to the top of the band or to split in two.
  • SourcingLight — a source line per page, plus inline links where a claim is contested or surprising.
  • HistoriographyThe Viking Age, the colonial period, 1864 and the cooperation policy have all been substantially rewritten since the schoolbooks. Pages follow current scholarship and say where it changed.
