Towns, silver and the trade world

c. 700 – 1000 · vikingetiden · the other half of it

The same century that produced the raids produced the first Danish towns, the first Danish coins, and a silver supply that came from Baghdad. Denmark had never had cities. Within three generations it had three, and they were built on purpose.

Introduction

WHAT THIS PAGE ANSWERSFive questions

  1. Denmark had no towns for ten thousand years. Why do three appear at once?
  2. Who built them, and what did a king get out of one?
  3. How did silver minted in Baghdad end up in a Jutland field?
  4. How do you trade when nobody agrees on what money is?
  5. What did it look like — and what did it smell like — to live in one?

01 / NARRATIVEThe other half of the Viking Age

Danish terms in this section

handelsplads
Trading place — a market, often seasonal, and the ancestor of the town.
emporium
The scholarly term for these early trading settlements across northern Europe.
købmand
Merchant — literally 'buy-man'.

Chapter 8 was about ships going out. This one is about what came back, and where it was unloaded.

The two halves are not separate stories with separate casts. They are the same men in the same vessels, and the choice between trading and raiding was made on arrival according to what looked easier. The reeve at Portland died because he could not tell the difference in advance, and neither can we, three-quarters of the time.

It is worth pressing on that, because it is the claim the whole of Part C rests on and it is easy to assert and hard to show. The evidence is of three kinds. The objects are the same: the folding scales and cubo-octahedral weights that appear in merchants' graves also appear in the winter camps of raiding armies, and the hacksilver in a Danish hoard includes church plate cut up in Ireland alongside dirhams bought legitimately on the Volga. The ships are the same: a cargo vessel and a warship differ in proportion, not in kind, and a crew that owned one could crew the other. And the men are the same — Godfred, who appears in chapter 8 burning a rival trading place, appears in this one founding the largest town in Scandinavia, and he did both in 808.

But the commercial half has one feature the raiding half does not: it built things. Raids leave burnt layers in other countries' archaeology. Trade leaves towns — and towns, once they exist, turn out to be the most durable institution anybody in this series has invented. Ribe has been continuously occupied since around 704. It is still there.

02 / NARRATIVERibe: the first town

Danish terms in this section

grubehus
Pit-house: a small sunken-floored building, typically a workshop rather than a dwelling.
parcel
Plot — the marked-out unit of ground a merchant or craftsman occupied.
sceatta
A small, thick silver coin of the North Sea world; Ribe has produced them in quantity.

On the west coast of Jutland, on a river a few kilometres from the Wadden Sea, someone around 704–710 laid out a market.

The word to hold on to is laid out. Excavation found plots of standard width, divided by shallow ditches, arranged along a street on the north bank of the Ribe river. That is not a settlement that grew; it is a decision, executed by someone with the authority to allocate ground and the expectation that people would come and use it.

They did. The plots fill with the debris of specialised craft: amber-working, glass beadmaking, bronze casting, comb-making from antler, leatherwork, textile production. The raw materials come from a long way off — glass tesserae recycled from Mediterranean mosaics, antler brought in from elsewhere, amber from the west coast beaches of chapter 4. Frisian merchants were almost certainly involved from the start; the pottery and the coins point that way.

For its first century Ribe looks seasonal — busy in summer, quiet in winter — and then it becomes permanent, with a rampart around it by the ninth century. It was a bishop's seat by 948. It has never since been abandoned, which makes it the oldest continuously occupied town in Denmark and one of the oldest in Scandinavia.

Why there? Because the west coast faces the North Sea and the Frisian and English trade, and because the site sits at the last point a seagoing vessel could reach up the river. Every trading town in this chapter sits at a break of transport — where sea meets river, or where cargo must come out of a ship and onto a cart. That is what a town is for.

03 / NARRATIVEHedeby: the machine

Danish terms in this section

Hedeby
German Haithabu, Old Norse Heiðabýr — 'the settlement on the heath'.
halvkredsvold
The semicircular rampart: 1,300 m long, up to about ten metres high.
pælespærring
A pile barrier across the harbour mouth, controlling who came in.
anløbsbro
Jetty — Hedeby's harbour had several, built out into the water.

Now look at the map, because Hedeby's location is not a coincidence and not a compromise. It is the single best-placed site in northern Europe, and everything about the town follows from that.

It sits at the innermost end of the Schlei, a long narrow inlet running west from the Baltic. From there it is under fifteen kilometres overland to the Treene, which flows into the Eider, which reaches the North Sea. So a cargo could come out of the Baltic, be carted a single day's journey, and be afloat again on the way to Frisia or England — avoiding the entire sail around the top of Jutland, with its currents, its Skagerrak weather and its opportunities for other people to rob you.

Simultaneously, the north–south land route up the Jutland peninsula — the road that becomes the Hærvejen of chapter 1 — passes immediately by. Hedeby is where the east–west sea traffic and the north–south land traffic cross.

Chapter 8 described how Godfred stocked it in 808 by destroying a rival market and shipping its merchants here. Within a century Hedeby was the largest town in Scandinavia.

The archaeology is extraordinarily good, because much of the site is waterlogged. Excavations since 1900 have recovered more than three hundred and forty thousand objects from an area of twenty-four hectares. The town was laid out around a stream, with plank-paved streets running parallel and perpendicular to it; the houses were small, rectangular, close-packed, with pit-house workshops concentrated in the north-west. The harbour had jetties running out into the water and a barrier of driven piles across the entrance.

In the mid-tenth century — a nervous period — the whole twenty-four hectares was enclosed by the halvkredsvold, a semicircular rampart 1,300 metres long and in places about ten metres high, later joined to the Danevirke so that town and frontier became one system. The rampart is still standing and still walkable.

And Hedeby struck coins. From early in the ninth century, imitating Frankish types from Dorestad, these are usually called the first coins minted in Scandinavia — though the claim is contested, because Ribe has produced small silver sceattas in such quantity, and of such specific types, that a number of numismatists argue they were struck there in the eighth century. If that is right, the first Scandinavian mint is a hundred years older and stands on the other coast. Either way, a king who mints coins is asserting something about who guarantees value inside his territory.

04 / NARRATIVEWhat a king wanted with a town

It is worth being blunt about the motive, because a romantic reading of Viking-age towns as spontaneous flowerings of trade misses the point entirely.

A merchant with a shipload of furs needs three things: somewhere to unload, somewhere to sleep without being murdered, and confidence that the person he sells to will not simply take the cargo. The commodity a ruler sells him is peace — market peace, enforced. In return the ruler takes a cut of everything that moves.

Concentrate the trade in one defensible place and the cut becomes collectable. That is why the towns are planned rather than organic, why they are fortified, why kings founded them and moved merchants into them by force, and why a king would burn a competitor's market to fill his own.

The revenue mattered enormously. A Viking-age Danish king had no land tax worth the name and no bureaucracy. What he had was plunder, tribute, and tolls. Two of those three are unreliable. The third arrives every season, in silver, at a place he controls — and it funds the fleet, the retinue, the ramparts and eventually the ring fortresses of chapter 10.

The third Danish town, Aarhus, appears in the tenth century on the east coast, fortified from early on, and joins Ribe and Hedeby as a bishop's seat in 948. Three towns, all royal, all at transport breaks, all defended. It is a policy, not an accident.

05 / NARRATIVEThe road east

Danish terms in this section

dirham
The silver coin of the Islamic world; tens of thousands have been found in Scandinavia.
Rus
The Scandinavians who operated on the eastern rivers, and the origin of the name Russia.
Miklagård
'The great city' — the Norse name for Constantinople.
Serkland
The Norse name for the lands of the caliphate.

Here is a fact that ought to be more startling than it usually is: the silver of the Danish Viking Age was largely minted in the Islamic world, and much of it arrived by river.

The mechanism is the eastern route, and it belongs mainly to Swedish rather than Danish enterprise, but the silver reached everybody. Scandinavians — called the Rus in the eastern sources — worked up the rivers from the Gulf of Finland, portaging between watersheds, down the Volga to the Caspian and the markets of the caliphate, or down the Dnieper to the Black Sea and Constantinople.

What went east was furs above all — squirrel, marten, sable, fox, beaver — plus walrus ivory, honey, wax, amber and swords. And slaves, in very large numbers. The Arabic sources are explicit about this, and the word most European languages use for a person owned by another still carries the ethnicity of the people most often taken.

Ibn Fadlan on the Volga, 922

Ahmad ibn Fadlan was an envoy sent from Baghdad to the king of the Volga Bulgars, and while he was there he met a party of Rus traders camped on the riverbank. He wrote down what he saw, at length, with the fascinated distaste of a cultivated man watching people he considers filthy.

He describes them as physically magnificent — tall, fair, tattooed from fingernails to neck — and as the dirtiest of God's creatures, because they washed communally from a shared basin in a manner he found appalling. He describes the women's paired brooches and their bead necklaces, one bead bought for each ten thousand dirhams the husband owned. He describes the traders praying to wooden idols for a buyer who would purchase without haggling.

And he describes, in detail that no other source comes close to, the funeral of a Rus chieftain over ten days: the ship drawn up on shore, the slave girl who agreed to die with him and was given drink and passed among the men, the old woman the Rus called the Angel of Death, the killing, and the burning of ship and body together while a bystander told him the Arabs were fools for putting their dead in the ground where worms could reach them.

It is the single most detailed eyewitness account of Viking-age people anywhere — and it is describing Scandinavians two thousand kilometres from Denmark, filtered through an appalled Muslim diplomat who did not speak their language. Every word of it has been argued over.

Ahmad ibn Fadlan · on the Volga, 922

What came back was silver, by weight, along with silk, glass, spices and beads. The flow begins around 800, peaks in the tenth century, and then stops rather suddenly in the late tenth century when the silver mines of the caliphate ran into difficulty. Scandinavia's supply switches to German and English silver — the latter arriving as tribute, which is the English conquest's story (11).

06 / NARRATIVEWhat the towns made

Danish terms in this section

kam
Comb — of riveted antler plates, and the commonest manufactured object in the Viking world.
ovale spænder
Oval brooches: the paired shoulder brooches of Scandinavian women's dress, cast to standard patterns.
vadmel
Coarse woollen cloth — the material of clothing, and of sails.
digel
Crucible; town layers are full of the broken ones.

A town is not only a place where goods change hands. It is a place where they are made, and the Danish town layers are packed with the evidence.

The commonest product is the comb. Viking-age combs are composite objects: thin plates of red deer antler, shaped, toothed and riveted between side-bars, and they are found everywhere from Dublin to the Volga. Antler is springy and does not snap the way bone does, which is why it was worth importing to a town that had no deer of its own. Comb-making leaves an unmistakable debris of sawn antler offcuts, and it turns up in Ribe, Hedeby, Birka and Kaupang alike — often, on the evidence of tool marks, from craftsmen who moved between them.

Glass beads were made by melting down cubes of coloured glass — mostly tesserae salvaged from Roman and Byzantine mosaics — and drawing or winding them. Ribe has produced the furnaces. Bronze casting supplied the oval brooches that Scandinavian women wore in pairs at the shoulders, cast in clay moulds to designs so standardised that a brooch from Jutland is barely distinguishable from one found in Russia. Amber was cut into beads and gaming pieces. There was ironwork, leatherwork, shoemaking, and the manufacture of weapons.

The most expensive thing in the Viking Age

And there was cloth — which is where the numbers become surprising.

A longship needs a sail, and a Viking-age square sail of around ninety square metres takes something on the order of two hundred kilograms of wool. That wool has to be grown, plucked or sheared, sorted, combed, spun into two different yarns for warp and weft, woven on an upright loom, and then finished and treated with grease. Estimates of the total labour run into several person-years for a single sail.

Set that against the hull. A ship's timber can be felled and worked by a competent crew in a matter of months. The sail takes longer than the ship — and it was almost certainly made by women, in households, over years.

This is one of the more useful correctives available about the period. The technology that made the Viking Age possible was not only the shipwright's; it was the spinner's. A fleet of a hundred ships represents a textile industry running for a very long time, and a society organised to produce it.

07 / NARRATIVEThe country behind the town

Danish terms in this section

gård
Farm — the fenced holding of one household, with its longhouse, byre and workshops.
hjulplov
The wheeled plough with a mouldboard, which turns the sod instead of scratching it.
træl
Thrall: an unfree person, owned, with no standing at law.
hovedgård
The magnate's farm — several times the size of its neighbours, and the local centre of power.

Full-time specialists do not grow food. A comb-maker working antler all day is eating grain and meat that somebody else raised, and a town of a thousand people is a permanent demand on the country around it.

That is genuinely new. Every settlement in chapters 1 to 7 fed itself. From now on there is a population in Denmark that does not — and since nine people in ten still lived on a farm, the towns of this chapter are the visible tip of something the sources never describe.

Fortunately the postholes do. Vorbasse, the west Jutland settlement chapter 6 watched moving around its own territory for a thousand years, is still there in the Viking Age, and by the tenth century it has become something different: seven or eight large fenced farmsteads laid out to a common plan, each with a longhouse of thirty metres or more, a byre for a substantial herd, outbuildings, a well, and — on several of them — workshops for iron and precious metal. The village is bigger, richer, more regular, and more unequal than anything before it.

The farming improved. The hjulplov, with a coulter, share and mouldboard, cuts the sod and turns it over rather than merely scoring it as chapter 3's ard had done, which makes the heavy eastern clays properly workable for the first time. Rye becomes a major crop alongside barley and oats. Iron is now cheap and everywhere: the bog-iron industry of chapter 5 has gone industrial, with production sites in west and central Jutland running to thousands of individual furnace pits.

Above the ordinary farms sit the magnate residences — at Lejre on Zealand, at Tissø, at Erritsø near the Little Belt: enormous halls with workshops and dependent settlements around them. These are where the surplus was gathered and where it was spent, and their owners are the men who supplied the ships of chapter 8.

The people who were owned

And there is one more thing on these farms, which chapter 8 promised would be paid for here.

Danish society ran on unfree labour. A træl was property: he could be sold, given away or killed, could not bring a case, and his children were born into the same condition. Later Scandinavian law codes treat thralls as a normal part of a household's assets, listed with the livestock, and the Icelandic sources take their presence entirely for granted.

Where did they come from? Some were born to it, and some were Danes who had fallen into it through debt or judgement. But a great many came in from outside, taken in the raids of chapter 8 and sold in the markets of this one — Hedeby was a slave market as well as a comb market, and the Arabic and Frankish sources describe the northern trade in people without embarrassment. The long-distance flow that carried captives east to the caliphate had a domestic branch, and it ended on farms like the ones at Vorbasse.

This is the connection the two halves of the Viking Age make between them, and it is not a comfortable one. The silver that built the towns was substantially paid for in people; and the agricultural surplus that fed the towns was substantially produced by people who had arrived the same way. Thralldom does not end in Denmark until the thirteenth century, and Jyske Lov (14) is where it finally disappears from the law.

08 / NARRATIVEMoney that is not money

Now the practical problem. A Danish merchant at Hedeby might be offered payment in Frankish deniers, Anglo-Saxon pennies, Abbasid dirhams from three different mints, a cut-up brooch, and a length of silver wire. What is that worth?

The answer, for most of this period, is: whatever it weighs. This is a weight economy, or bullion economy. Silver is valued by mass and purity, not by the authority stamped on it. A coin from Baghdad and a fragment hacked off a Irish reliquary are the same thing — silver — and are treated identically.

Hence hacksilver: hoards full of deliberately chopped-up ornaments, ingots and coins, because you cut off the amount you need. Hence also the standard kit found in merchants' graves and in town layers all over the Viking world — a small folding balance and a set of weights, often neatly cubo-octahedral, made to a shared standard that worked from Ireland to the Volga. Nicks and scratches on coins are testing marks: someone checking whether the silver goes all the way through.

Two things follow. First, this is a remarkably sophisticated system for one usually described as primitive — it lets people transact across an enormous area with no shared political authority and no trust, because the scales settle everything. Second, it makes a king's coinage nearly pointless at first, which is why the early Hedeby coins circulate as metal rather than as currency, and why a proper coin economy in Denmark has to wait for Harald Bluetooth (10) and beyond.

09 / NARRATIVESeen from outside

Almost everything written about these towns was written by people passing through — Ibn Fadlan on the Volga was one, and two more stand out. They could hardly be less alike.

Ottar at the West Saxon court, c. 890

A Norwegian named Ottar — Ohthere, in the English spelling — came to the court of King Alfred of Wessex and described where he had come from. Somebody wrote it down, in Old English, and it was inserted into the translation of a Latin world history being produced under Alfred's patronage.

He said he lived furthest north of all Norwegians, that he was a wealthy man by the standards of that country, that his wealth was mostly in reindeer, and that he had once sailed north around the top of the peninsula out of curiosity to see how far the land went. He described paying tribute in furs, feathers, whalebone and ship-rope. And he described the voyage south: down the Norwegian coast to Kaupang, then five days across to Hedeby.

A second traveller, Wulfstan, described the run east from Hedeby along the Baltic coast to Truso in seven days.

What makes these accounts extraordinary is the direction of information. Every other written source in chapters 8 and 9 is an outsider describing Scandinavians. Here a Scandinavian describes his own world, to a foreign king, and the king's clerks take it down. It is as close as this series gets, before the Jelling stones (10), to hearing a northern voice.

Ottar of Hålogaland · at Alfred's court, c. 890

al-Tartushi at Hedeby, c. 960

Ibrahim ibn Ya'qub al-Tartushi was a Jewish merchant and traveller from Córdoba — then one of the largest and most sophisticated cities in the world — and he came north on some kind of diplomatic errand. He was not impressed.

He called Hedeby a very large town at the far end of the world's ocean, with fresh-water springs inside it. Its people, he reported, worshipped Sirius, apart from a small number of Christians who had a church there. Anyone who sacrificed an animal hung it on a pole outside his door so the neighbours could see he had made an offering — cattle, ram, goat or pig. The town was poor in goods and wealth. People mostly ate fish, which were plentiful. Infants were thrown into the sea for economic reasons. Women had the right to divorce. Both sexes wore eye makeup, which he thought improved their looks.

And the singing was the worst he had ever heard: a rumbling in the throat, he said, like a dog's, only more bestial.

He is describing the largest town in Scandinavia, at its height, and finding it a squalid frontier village — which is a useful corrective in both directions. Hedeby was enormous by northern standards and negligible by Andalusian ones. It is worth remembering, when reading his details about infants and divorce, that he did not speak the language.

Ibrahim ibn Ya'qub al-Tartushi · Hedeby, c. 960

10 / NARRATIVELiving in one

The archaeology supports al-Tartushi more than the tourist brochures do.

Hedeby's houses were small and packed tightly along plank streets. There was no drainage worth the name. Waste went into pits or into the stream that also supplied the water. The parasite eggs recovered from Viking-age town deposits are abundant, and analysis of cesspit contents shows intestinal worms as the normal condition. Cattle, pigs and poultry lived among the houses.

The cemeteries show what that meant. Life expectancy was short — reaching forty was uncommon — and the skeletal evidence shows tuberculosis, heavy dental wear, and childhood malnutrition recorded in tooth enamel. Infant burials are numerous.

They also show sharp inequality. Most Hedeby graves are simple and correspond to the small houses. But from the tenth century there are richly furnished chamber graves, and one princely burial in which the dead man and two companions were laid under a war or travelling ship some twenty metres long, dug into the ground above them.

A town, in other words, produced the first sizeable concentration of poor people in Danish history, and simultaneously a class rich enough to be buried under a ship. Both are new.

Meanwhile in Europe

Hedeby's population of perhaps one to fifteen hundred should be set against the standards of the day. Constantinople had several hundred thousand people; Córdoba, where al-Tartushi came from, was comparably vast; Baghdad was probably the largest city on earth. London and Paris were modest by those standards, but both were larger than anything in Scandinavia.

The emporia of the North Sea world — Dorestad, Quentovic, Ipswich, York, Birka, Kaupang, Wolin, Truso — belong to one system, rise together, and mostly fall together. What connects them is not a common ruler but a common shortage: silver, and the willingness to move goods a long way to get it.

11 / NARRATIVEChristianity arrives with the merchants

One more thing came up the trade routes, and it turned out to matter more than the silk.

Chapter 8 left the monk Ansgar arriving in 826 in the baggage of a failed Danish claimant. He kept at it. Around 848 he obtained permission from King Horik I to build a church at Hedeby, and later another at Ribe. Rimbert, who wrote his life, records that a bell was hung, which offended some of the town's pagans considerably.

Ansgar converted almost nobody who mattered, and after his death the mission lapsed. But the significant thing is where the churches were: in the two towns, not in the countryside. Christianity entered Denmark as a merchants' religion, along a commercial route, because Christian traders wanted somewhere to worship and Christian rulers to the south preferred dealing with Christians.

The widow who kept going to Dorestad

Rimbert's life of Ansgar, written around 870, is hagiography and should be read as such. But in among the miracles it records something no other source does: the names and circumstances of ordinary Scandinavians who had become Christian, and what it cost them.

One is a woman at Birka called Frideborg, a widow of some means, who had been baptised and who wanted to receive communion at her death — difficult, in a town with no resident priest. She kept wine by her for years for the purpose. And she instructed her daughter Catla that when she died, her property was to be given to the poor; since Birka had few poor to give it to, Catla was to take the money to Dorestad, the Frisian emporium of chapters 8 and 9, where there were beggars enough and churches to advise her.

Catla did it. She sailed the North Sea route with the money, gave it away in Dorestad, and came home.

Set aside whether the story is edifying. Look at what it assumes without needing to explain: that a woman in a Scandinavian trading town controlled her own property and disposed of it by her own instruction; that her daughter could take ship on a route hundreds of kilometres long, in a merchant's world, without a male escort worth mentioning; and that this was unremarkable enough to tell in passing.

Frideborg lived in Birka rather than Denmark, and Rimbert wanted a moral from her. She is still the closest this chapter comes to an ordinary northerner with a name, a household and a decision — and she is a merchant's widow, not a king.

Frideborg and Catla, in Rimbert's Life of Ansgar · Birka to Dorestad, mid-800s

By 948, Hedeby, Ribe and Aarhus all appear as bishoprics under the archbishopric of Hamburg-Bremen. The Danish king at that point was not a Christian and the country was not converted. The church had simply established addresses in the three places where foreigners came.

Fifteen years or so later, a Danish king decided to be baptised, and did it in a way designed to convert the whole country at once. That is Jelling (10).

Meanwhile in the world

The Abbasid caliphate that minted this chapter's silver was, in the ninth century, the centre of world science: the House of Wisdom in Baghdad, the translation of Greek philosophy and medicine into Arabic, algebra, and the astronomical work that eventually reaches Europe. Song China invented paper money in roughly the period when Denmark was still weighing hacksilver.

The connection is direct rather than decorative. A silver coin struck at Samarkand and buried in a Danish field is one object touched by both worlds — and it is by far the commonest form of contact between them.

12 / NARRATIVEThe end of the emporia

These towns do not last. Across northern Europe, the great trading places of the eighth and ninth centuries mostly fail in the tenth and eleventh, and are replaced by towns of a different kind.

Dorestad declined after repeated attacks and a shifting river. Birka was abandoned around 970 and Sigtuna grew instead. Kaupang faded. And Hedeby, having survived a Swedish takeover, a German attack in 934 and a great deal else, was burned in 1050 by Harald Hardrada of Norway — who, according to the sagas, sent fire-ships into the harbour, and the charred remains of boats have been found in the Schlei. What was left was finished off by a Slavic raid in 1066, and the survivors moved across the water to Schleswig.

The causes are general. The eastern silver dried up. Ships got bigger and needed proper harbours rather than beaches. And royal power was consolidating in a way that favoured a different kind of town — one with a stone church, a mint, a bishop and a market, sited where the king and the church wanted it rather than where the sea was convenient.

Ribe survived by becoming that second kind of town. Aarhus did too. Hedeby did not, and its successor Schleswig took over its function a couple of kilometres away.

Which is why you can walk around Hedeby today: it is one of the few major Viking-age towns anywhere that was never built over. There is a rampart, a stream, a reconstructed row of houses, and twenty-four hectares of grass with the largest Viking town in Scandinavia lying underneath it.

Myth-check

"Vikings were raiders; trade came later, once they settled down."
Ribe was laid out around 704, roughly eighty years before Lindisfarne. The commercial network is older than the raiding, and the same people did both.
"Viking towns grew up naturally around good harbours."
They were planned, plotted out and defended, and kings founded them for revenue. Godfred populated Hedeby by destroying somebody else's market and shipping the merchants in.
"The Vikings used coins as money."
For most of this period silver was valued by weight, not by mint. Hence hacksilver, folding scales, standard weights, and test marks cut into coins. Hedeby struck the first Scandinavian coins around 825, but they circulated as metal.
"Danish silver came from raiding England."
Not at first. The great inflow of the ninth and tenth centuries is Islamic silver arriving by the eastern rivers, paid mostly for furs and slaves. English silver becomes dominant only after the eastern supply fails — which is the North Sea Empire (11).
"Denmark converted to Christianity under Harald Bluetooth."
Officially, yes. But there were churches at Hedeby and Ribe from the 850s, and three Danish bishoprics on paper by 948. Christianity arrived with merchants a century before it arrived with a king.

Summary

IF YOU REMEMBER FIVE THINGSThe page in five

  1. Denmark's first towns were planned, not grown. Ribe was laid out in standard plots around 704 and has been occupied ever since; Hedeby was stocked by force in 808 and became the largest town in Scandinavia.

  2. Kings built towns to sell market peace and collect tolls. It was the only reliable revenue a Viking-age king had, and it paid for everything in Harald's building programme (10).

  3. Hedeby sat where the Baltic came within fifteen kilometres of a river running to the North Sea. That portage is the whole explanation for the town.

  4. The silver came from the Islamic world by the eastern rivers, paid for furs and slaves, and was valued by weight rather than by mint — hence hacksilver, folding scales and standard weights.

  5. Christianity arrived with merchants, not with a king: churches at Hedeby and Ribe from the 850s, three bishoprics on paper by 948, in a country that was not converted.

Questions & discussion

FOUR KINDSWork the material

Recall

Did the facts land?

  • What tells us Ribe was planned rather than grown?
  • Why is Hedeby where it is? Give the two crossing routes.
  • What is hacksilver, and what equipment did a merchant carry because of it?
  • Where did most Viking-age silver come from, and what was it paid for?
  • Who were Ottar and al-Tartushi, and why is Ottar's account unusual?

Causal

Why did that follow from this?

  • Why does a king found a town rather than simply taxing existing trade wherever it happens?
  • Why does a weight economy make coinage almost pointless — and what has to change before coins matter?
  • Why did Christianity establish itself in towns first, and what does that say about how religions actually travel?
  • Why did the great emporia fail in the eleventh century when the trade did not?

Counterfactual

Pull one thread and see what moves.

  • Suppose the Schlei portage did not exist — that Jutland were solid across its base. Where does the Danish trading town go, and what happens to the kingdom's centre of gravity?
  • Suppose the Abbasid silver had kept flowing after 1000. Does Sweyn Forkbeard still need to invade England?

Contested

Where historians actually disagree.

  • al-Tartushi reports infanticide, women's right to divorce and eye makeup. He was a foreigner who did not speak the language, on a short visit. Which of his details would you trust, and on what principle?
  • How much of Viking-age wealth rested on the slave trade? The furs are archaeologically invisible and the people more so. What can be inferred from silver distribution alone?
  • Were the emporia royal creations or merchant creations? The evidence for planning is strong, but so is the evidence for Frisian initiative at Ribe before any Danish king appears.
  • Hedeby is Danish history on German soil, and was excavated under the Third Reich as Germanic heritage. How should a site like that be presented now, and by whom?

Go and look

STILL THEREPlaces you can visit

  • Hedeby, Schleswig Walk the semicircular rampart; the reconstructed houses stand on the excavated plots, and the museum holds a Viking ship from the harbour. Never built over.
  • Ribe The oldest town in Denmark, with a medieval core you can walk in an hour. Museet Ribes Vikinger sits on the excavated layers.
  • Ribe VikingeCenter A working reconstruction of the marketplace, with craftspeople using the techniques found in the ground.
