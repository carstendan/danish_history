Sound Dues, the Hanse and a straining union

1412 – 1460 · Erik af Pommern · Philippa · Engelbrekt · Christoffer af Bayern · Christian 1.

A king starts charging every foreign ship that passes Helsingør, and invents an income that will pay for the Danish state for four hundred and twenty-eight years. Then he loses a war, a duchy, two kingdoms and his throne.

01 / NARRATIVEWhat Erik inherited

Danish terms in this section

rigsråd
The council of the realm. In this chapter it stops being an advisory body and starts being the thing that makes and unmakes kings.
livgeding
The estates settled on a queen for her lifetime's maintenance. Philippa's were large, in Sweden, and she governed them.

Erik of Pomerania was about thirty when Margrete died in October 1412, and he had been a crowned king for fifteen years without governing anything. That is the central fact about him. He inherited three kingdoms, an unfinished war in Schleswig, a treasury in good order and a set of officials who had spent their careers taking instructions from somebody else.

He was not a fool and he was not lazy. He was ambitious in exactly the way his foster-mother had not been: where she bought her way out of wars, he fought them, and where she avoided forcing an issue with the Hanseatic towns, he went looking for one. Over forty-seven years he founded towns, built castles, wrote town law, reorganised the coinage and invented the most durable source of revenue in Danish history. He also lost every war he fought.

In 1406 he had married Philippa, daughter of Henry 4. of England, who was twelve at the wedding in Lund. She matters more than a consort usually does in these pages, and she will have a section of her own below. She held large estates in Sweden as her livgeding, spent much of her time there, judged disputes, and governed the three kingdoms outright in 1423–25 while Erik was away — first at the imperial court, then on pilgrimage to Jerusalem. In October 1424, on her own authority, she concluded a currency treaty with Lübeck, Hamburg, Lüneburg and Wismar under which Denmark and the Wendish towns struck to a common standard. The coin is called Philippa's søsling and it was minted at Lund.

That treaty is worth holding onto, because it shows what the alternative policy looked like. Two years later her husband was at war with the same four towns.

02 / NARRATIVEThe verdict at Ofen, 1424

Danish terms in this section

Sønderjylland
South Jutland: the duchy, called Schleswig in German and increasingly in the documents. The distinction between the two names is itself a piece of the argument, and it runs to 1920.
len
A fief. Whether Schleswig was a fief of the Danish crown, held conditionally, or an inheritance the Holstein counts owned outright, is the whole question.

Margrete had been buying Schleswig back piece by piece when she died. Erik's method was to settle the title instead, and on paper it worked beautifully.

The case went to the Emperor Sigismund as arbitrator. Erik travelled to Ofen — Buda — in 1423 to argue it in person, leaving Philippa governing at home. On 28 June 1424 the emperor gave judgment: Sønderjylland was a fief of the Danish crown, and the counts of Holstein had no hereditary right in it. The Holsteiners took the case to Rome to have the verdict overturned; the pope threw it out altogether. Legally, Erik had won completely.

It made no difference whatever, and the reason is the most useful thing in this chapter about how power worked. A verdict is a piece of parchment. Enforcing it meant taking castles in Schleswig from men who held them, and those men were the counts of Holstein, who were allied by marriage, trade and proximity to the richest cities in northern Germany. Erik had a legal instrument and no way to execute it, and the attempt to execute it produced a war that lasted nine years and cost him the duchy, the towns and eventually the throne.

Chapter 14 left the Constitutio Valdemariana of 1326 promising that duchy and kingdom would never be held by the same person; chapter 16 left Gerhard 6. enfeoffed with the duchy in 1386, and chapter 17 saw him killed in Ditmarsken in 1404. The Ofen verdict is the last time a Danish king had a clean legal claim to the whole of it. Everything after this is compromise, and the compromise of 1460 is chapter 19.

03 / NARRATIVEA toll at Helsingør

Danish terms in this section

Øresundstolden
The Sound Dues: a transit toll on foreign shipping passing through the Sound, taken at Helsingør from about 1429 until 1857.
Krogen
'The hook' — the castle Erik built on the spit at Helsingør to enforce the toll. Frederik 2. pulled it down in the 1570s and built Kronborg on the site.
nobel
An English gold coin, the noble or rose-noble. One per ship, per passage, whatever the ship was carrying.

Somewhere in the second half of the 1420s — the accounts do not survive and the exact year is uncertain, though 1429 is when it is beyond dispute — Erik began charging every foreign ship that passed Helsingør one English noble.

The idea was not new. Abel had set a toll on ships going to and past the Skåne market in 1251, and after about 1370 it was collected at Helsingborg. What Erik did was move the collection point across the water to Helsingør, put a castle there to enforce it, raise the rate substantially, and apply it to transit — not to ships trading in Denmark, but to ships merely passing through on their way somewhere else.

Everything about it depended on a fact established in chapter 15. Both shores of the Sound had been Danish since 1360. A toll on a strait requires both banks; with only one, ships hug the other side and pay nothing. Erik made the point structurally: he strengthened Kärnan at Helsingborg, built Malmøhus, founded Landskrona in 1413, gave Helsingør town privileges in 1426, and closed the Great and Little Belts to foreign shipping altogether, so that there was no other way in. He also made Copenhagen the seat of government — the town Absalon had fortified in chapter 13 becomes the capital because of where it sits relative to this line of water.

It is hard to overstate what this meant. Danish kings had lived on land — on crown estates, on occasional taxes granted by assemblies that could refuse, on debasing the coinage and selling provinces, which is the whole miserable arithmetic of chapters 14 and 15. The Sound Dues were different in kind. They were paid by foreigners, they did not require anyone's consent, they arrived in gold, and they went to the king personally rather than to the realm. Until 1771 the money was not part of the state's income at all. It was his.

A crown with an income that nobody at home has to vote is a crown that can afford to ignore people. That is the thread this chapter opens and the rest of the series follows to 1660 and beyond; it is also, immediately, why the Hanseatic towns went to war.

04 / NARRATIVEThe towns declare war

Danish terms in this section

hansestæderne
The Hanseatic towns. Not a state and not quite a league — an association of trading cities, led by Lübeck, that could agree on a blockade and put two hundred ships to sea.
vendiske stæder
The Wendish towns — Lübeck, Rostock, Stralsund, Wismar, Lüneburg, Hamburg — the core group, and the ones Denmark fought.

Six towns declared war in 1426: Lübeck, Hamburg, Lüneburg, Rostock, Stralsund and Wismar. Their grievances were the toll, Erik's opening of the Baltic to Dutch shipping, and his attempt to stir up opposition inside Lübeck itself. They allied with the Holstein counts, blockaded the Scandinavian ports, and set about the coasts: Læsø, Årø, Lolland, Møn and Bornholm were plundered in 1427.

Chapter 13 introduced Lübeck as a small town on the Trave with which Valdemar the Great's Denmark had dealings; it was an ally in 1201 and an enemy by 1227. Chapter 15 ended with the Hanseatic confederation dictating terms at Stralsund in 1370. Here the relationship reaches its natural conclusion: the towns are now capable of fighting the largest state in Europe to a standstill, and of choosing which Danish king they would rather have.

Vignette · Philippa at Copenhagen castle, April 1428

On 6 April 1428 a Hanseatic fleet enters the Sound. The chronicles put it at 260 ships and 12,000 men, which is a chronicler's arithmetic, but it was very large and its object was Copenhagen and the combined Danish-Swedish fleet lying in the harbour.

The king is at Sorø, forty kilometres away, at his devotions, and — the story goes — will not interrupt them. The queen is in Copenhagen. Philippa hands out weapons, promises rewards for courage, and gets what warships are rigged out into the deep water outside the harbour, with a raft of timber built to block the approach. The April attack is beaten off.

What the retelling usually leaves out is that they came back. On 15 June the Hanseatic ships returned and destroyed the fleet in the harbour; three vessels survived. It was the first battle in northern European waters in which ship's artillery was used at range. Philippa then organised a new fleet with Swedish help, and in May 1429 it went south and attacked Stralsund.

She died at Vadstena on 5 January 1430, aged thirty-five, and is buried in the abbey church. She had been queen of three kingdoms for twenty-four years and regent of them for two.

Philippa of England, queen of Denmark, Norway and Sweden · Copenhagen castle · 6 April 1428

Meanwhile in Europe

1429, the year the toll is beyond dispute, is also the year Joan of Arc relieved Orléans and had Charles 7. crowned at Reims — the turn of the Hundred Years' War, though nobody could have known it at the time and she was burned two years later. In Bohemia the Hussite wars had been running for a decade, and Hussite armies were raiding into Germany with a competence that terrified everyone; the Council of Basel opened in 1431 partly to deal with them.

The commercial fact of the century was quieter. Dutch and Zeeland shippers were moving into the Baltic grain trade that the Hanse regarded as its own, carrying more for less, and the Burgundian dukes behind them were assembling the richest territorial bloc in Europe out of the Low Countries. Erik's decision to let them through the Sound was one of the causes of his war, and it was also a correct reading of where the money was going. By the sixteenth century most of the ships paying at Helsingør were Dutch.

05 / NARRATIVEVordingborg, 1435

Danish terms in this section

stilstand
A truce, as distinct from a peace. Most of what was agreed between 1432 and 1435 was truce, repeatedly renewed, which is what a war looks like when neither side can finish it.

The war ground on for nine years and neither side could end it. Flensborg changed hands twice. Danish and Swedish fleets won at sea in 1427 and lost at Copenhagen in 1428. Bergen was sacked twice by privateers allied to the towns. Taxes rose in all three kingdoms to pay for it, and that is what eventually decided the matter, though not on any battlefield.

At Vordingborg in 1435 the war was settled. The Hanseatic towns had their privileges confirmed — which meant, among other things, that the leading towns did not pay the toll. Erik kept the toll itself, which is the one thing he had held onto. And Schleswig went to Adolf 8. of Holstein, as duke, which the Ofen verdict had said could not happen.

He had spent nine years and the credit of three kingdoms on a duchy he had already won in court, and had lost it in fact. What he had built instead — almost incidentally, as a way of paying for the war — was a toll booth in a narrow strait that would outlast the kingdom's frontier disputes, the Hanseatic League, the Kalmar Union and the sailing ship.

06 / NARRATIVEEngelbrekt, 1434

Danish terms in this section

bergsman
A Swedish mine-owner: a free proprietor of a share in an iron or copper mine, neither noble nor peasant. A class with no Danish equivalent, and it matters.
foged
Bailiff. The king's local officer, and the target of every complaint in Sweden between 1430 and 1523.

The taxes that paid for the Hanseatic war fell on Sweden as heavily as on Denmark, and Sweden had something Denmark did not: an export industry, in the iron and copper of Bergslagen, that was wrecked by a blockade of the Baltic. The men who owned the mines were bergsmän — free proprietors, organised, prosperous, and outside the categories that Danish government understood.

In the summer of 1434 they rose, and the rising ran through the country in months. Castles were burned, bailiffs expelled, and by the autumn the rebels held most of Sweden. What made it dangerous was not its violence but its politics: the Swedish council of the realm, which had every reason to fear a popular rising, joined it instead, and used it. In 1435 an assembly at Arboga — later called Sweden's first riksdag, which is a nineteenth-century way of putting it — chose Engelbrekt as captain of the realm.

Vignette · Engelbrekt Engelbrektsson, on an island in Hjälmaren, 4 May 1436

He is a minor nobleman from the mining country of Västmanland, of a family that had come from Germany two or three generations back, and until 1434 he is a local figure: the man who carries his district's complaints about the bailiff to the council, twice, and gets nothing.

Two years later he is captain of the realm and the most powerful man in Sweden, and he is also inconvenient to everybody — to Erik, obviously, but equally to the Swedish council, who wanted the rising's leverage without its leader.

On 4 May 1436, travelling by boat to a meeting in Stockholm, he puts in at an island in Lake Hjälmaren. There he is killed by Måns Bengtsson, son of a nobleman with whom he had a private quarrel. Måns Bengtsson was never punished. He was, within a few years, doing well.

Engelbrekt became a Swedish national figure almost immediately — a rhymed chronicle was written about him within a decade, and he is on the plinth of every nineteenth-century argument about Sweden's separateness. From the Danish side of the story he is something narrower and just as important: the first demonstration that the union could be repudiated from below, by people the union letter of 1397 had not considered at all.

Engelbrekt Engelbrektsson, captain of the realm of Sweden · an island in Lake Hjälmaren · 4 May 1436

Erik came to terms and was accepted back, on conditions. But the pattern was set and everyone had seen it work: the Swedish council had learned that it could raise the country against a Danish king and then negotiate as the country's representative. It did so again in 1464, in 1471, in 1501 and finally in 1521.

07 / NARRATIVEThe deposition, 1439

Danish terms in this section

opsigelsesbrev
A letter of renunciation: the formal document by which subjects withdrew their allegiance. The legal instrument of a deposition, and the council's most powerful weapon.
rigsforstander
Regent, literally 'realm-steward' — the office used when a kingdom had no king and did not yet want one.

Erik had no children. From the mid-1430s he pushed for his cousin Bogislav of Pomerania as successor, and the Danish council refused: a Pomeranian duke with no connection to the country was exactly what they had accepted once already, and once was enough. When Erik began transferring lands and castles to Bogislav anyway, the council read it as an attempt to settle the succession over their heads.

In 1438 he left for Gotland. He had held the castle of Visborg there since the island came to him in 1408, it was strong, and it sat astride the shipping lanes. The council governed without him, suppressed a peasant rising in Jutland, and in 1439 formally renounced their allegiance. Sweden did the same. Norway followed in 1442.

What Erik did next is the detail everyone remembers. He stayed on Gotland with his treasure and lived by piracy, preying on the shipping of the towns and kingdoms that had discarded him, for a decade. He gave up the island in 1449 and went home to Pomerania, where he had been born, and died at Rügenwalde in 1459 — outliving his successor, and very nearly outliving the man after that.

It is easy to make him ridiculous, and Danish tradition has. It is worth resisting. The deposition of 1439 was not the removal of an incompetent; it was the councils of three kingdoms discovering that between them they could dismiss a crowned king of all three and appoint the next one, and that nothing in the union letter of 1397 stopped them. That discovery is the real event, and every Danish accession for the next two centuries is priced accordingly.

08 / NARRATIVEChristoffer, and the price of a crown

Danish terms in this section

håndfæstning
The written conditions accepted at an accession. The series runs from 1282 (chapter 14) to 1660, and this is the stretch where it becomes routine rather than exceptional.
stadsret
Town law: the charter defining a town's courts, council and privileges. Copenhagen's, of 1443, became the model for the Zealand and Skåne towns.

The council had gone shopping before the deposition was complete. Their man was Christoffer of Bavaria, from the Wittelsbach house of the Upper Palatinate, twenty-two years old, the son of Erik's sister Katarina — one of the two Pomeranian grandchildren Margrete had brought south and married off in chapter 17. He was in Denmark for the coup of 1439, was made rigsforstander at once, and was hailed as king at Viborg on 9 April 1440. Sweden took him in 1441, Norway in 1442, and he was crowned at Ribe on 1 January 1443.

He is the last man to function as king of all three kingdoms for most of a reign, and what he paid for it is instructive. The union was reduced to a personal union in name as well as fact. The Hanseatic towns had their privileges reconfirmed. Sønderjylland was granted to Adolf 8. of Schauenburg hereditarily in 1440, which gave away in a charter what the Ofen verdict had won in court. He promised a håndfæstning and never actually sealed one, which was the single concession he managed to withhold. And the great offices of the realm went back to the two largest landowning families: Erik Nielsen Gyldenstierne as rigshofmester, Oluf Axelsen Thott as rigsmarsk.

Against that, a real reign. Copenhagen got a new town law in 1443 and became in practice what it had been becoming for twenty years — the capital; where a crusader in 1413 had toured Zealand town by town looking for the king, from the 1440s you knew where he was. There was energetic legislation for the market towns. And in 1442 he issued an ordinance on the land-peace that is still cited.

Vignette · Henrik Tagesen Reventlow, Aalborg, 12 June 1441

The peasants of Vendsyssel and Himmerland — the vendelboer, north of the Limfjord — had been in arms on and off since 1438. The grievances were taxes raised for a war they had no part in, and the tithe, which Christoffer reimposed in Jutland and on Funen after his coronation.

Their leader was not a peasant. Henrik Tagesen Reventlow was a nobleman, of a Holstein family established in Denmark, and what he was doing at the head of a peasant army is one of the things about the rising that nobody has satisfactorily explained.

On 6 June 1441 the royal army met them at Sankt Jørgensbjerg in Han Herred. The peasants were destroyed. Six days later, on 12 June, Reventlow was executed at Aalborg.

What followed the victory is the part that matters. King and nobility divided the government between them on terms Erik of Pomerania had spent his reign refusing — the great offices back in the hands of the great families, the council asking that the king's Bavarian advisers be kept out of Danish business. A peasant rising was put down, and the people who gained were not the ones who put it down but the ones who stood behind them.

Henrik Tagesen Reventlow, leader of the Jutland rising · executed at Aalborg · 12 June 1441

09 / NARRATIVEOxen, and the road south

Danish terms in this section

studehandel
The ox trade: young cattle raised in Jutland, driven south on the hoof and sold fat in Holstein, Hamburg and the Low Countries.
Hærvejen
'The army road', the ridge route down the spine of Jutland — also called Oksevejen, the ox road, which is what it was mostly used for.
græsning
Grazing. The resource the deserted farms of chapter 17 had left in abundance, and the reason cattle rather than grain.

Chapter 17 left the Danish countryside at its emptiest, with rents down to a level that would hold for four centuries. What grew out of that is the trade that defines the Danish economy until the nineteenth century, and it starts here.

Land that will not pay as arable will pay as pasture, because pasture needs almost no labour and labour was the thing that had become expensive. Jutland had grass and it had a road: the ridge track down the watershed that avoided the river crossings, known later as the Hærvejen and just as often as the ox road. To the south lay Holstein, Hamburg and beyond them the Rhineland and the Low Countries — the densest concentration of town-dwellers in Europe, all of whom ate meat and none of whom could raise it.

So Danish cattle walked to market. They were bred in Jutland, driven south in spring in herds of hundreds, and either sold at the border markets or fattened on Holstein and Dutch pasture for a season before slaughter. The trade grows through the fifteenth century, and by the sixteenth it is counted in tens of thousands of animals a year and is the principal Danish export.

Who profited is the point. Driving cattle to Hamburg required capital, land, and the freedom to move goods across a border — which meant the nobility, whose estates had been assembled out of the deserted farms of the previous chapter, and who were exempt from the tolls that fell on peasant sellers. From the middle of the fifteenth century the Danish nobility is increasingly a class of graziers and exporters rather than of rent-collectors, and its interest in keeping peasants tied to the estate grows accordingly. The vornedskab that binds Zealand peasants to their birthplace, formalised later in this century, belongs to the same story.

Meanwhile the herring of chapters 14 and 15 were beginning to fail. The great Skåne market did not collapse suddenly, but the catches fell away through the fifteenth century, and the Dutch learned to salt at sea, which removed the reason to come to Skanør at all. One Danish export declines as another rises, and both of them run past — or through — the Sound.

10 / NARRATIVEThe Oldenburg begins, 1448

Danish terms in this section

valgkapitulation
Another word for the accession charter, emphasising the bargain: the electors capitulate the king to terms before they elect him.
arvekonge / valgkonge
Hereditary king versus elected king. Norway was the first, Denmark the second, and after 1450 they had the same man on two different legal bases.

Christoffer died suddenly in January 1448, aged thirty-one, of what a chronicle calls a boil. He had married Dorothea of Brandenburg in 1445, when she was fifteen; there were no children, and no obvious heir anywhere.

Sweden moved first and elected Karl Knutsson Bonde, a Swedish magnate, in June. The Danish council looked at the counts of Holstein, offered the crown to Adolf 8., and were refused — he had his duchy and he preferred it. He proposed his nephew instead: Christian, count of Oldenburg, a minor north German dynast with a distant descent from Erik Klipping and, more to the point, no power base of his own in Denmark.

They took him, on terms. Christian 1. signed a håndfæstning in 1448 — the document Christoffer had promised and evaded — which bound him to govern with the council, and he married Christoffer's widow Dorothea, who was eighteen and would outlive him by fourteen years and run a great deal of Danish politics in the interval.

Norway was contested between him and Karl Knutsson for two years and settled in 1450 by a treaty concluded at Bergen: Norway and Denmark were to have the same king in perpetuity, chosen by both. It is a much more solid document than the union letter of 1397, and it held — Norway and Denmark stayed together until 1814, which is chapter 28. Sweden went its own way, came back briefly, and went again. The three-kingdom union of Kalmar had, in practice, ended with Christoffer.

Meanwhile in Europe

The middle of the century is where the medieval world visibly stops. In 1453 the Hundred Years' War ended and Constantinople fell to Mehmed 2., closing the eastern Mediterranean to Genoese and Venetian trade and pushing Europe's commercial centre of gravity further north and west — which is to say, further towards the Sound. Around 1450 Gutenberg was printing at Mainz; by 1482 there was a press in Odense, and by 1500 the technology that makes the Reformation of chapter 20 possible was in every substantial town in northern Europe.

Closer to home, the Burgundian dukes had assembled the Low Countries into the richest state in Europe, and Amsterdam and the Zeeland ports were taking the Baltic grain trade away from Lübeck ship by ship. The Hanse's victory over Denmark in 1435 turned out to be its high-water mark. The Danish crown, which had lost that war, was collecting a toll from the people who won the next one.

What Denmark had acquired, without much noticing, was a dynasty. The house of Oldenburg supplied every Danish monarch from 1448 to 1863 and, through the Glücksburg line, supplies the present one. It arrived because a Holstein count declined a job and recommended a nephew.

11 / NARRATIVEWhat the toll bought

Danish terms in this section

toldbog
The toll ledger. The Helsingør series in the Danish National Archives runs to more than seven hundred volumes; the oldest surviving is from 1497 and the run is unbroken from 1574.
læstetold
Cargo duty, by the last (a unit of ship's burden) — the system Peder Oxe introduced in 1567 to replace the flat charge per ship.

Set the three reigns of this chapter beside each other and the pattern is not encouraging. Erik was deposed. Christoffer was elected on conditions and died at thirty-one. Christian 1. signed away in advance a share of what his predecessors had exercised. Sweden had learned to leave. Schleswig had gone to a Holstein count with a hereditary title. Measured by the map of 1397, everything contracted.

And yet, underneath, a Danish king had found something no Danish king had ever had — money that arrived without anyone's permission. Everything the crown does for the next four centuries is affected by it. Christian 4. builds his fleet and his towns on it; the absolutism of 1660 is possible partly because the crown's largest single income does not depend on the estates of the realm; and the reason foreign powers care about Danish politics at all, from the Dutch in 1650 to the Americans in the 1850s, is a castle on a spit of sand at Helsingør.

Follow it forward and the shape of the series appears. In 1497, the first year for which a ledger survives, the toll brings in about 4,700 dalers. In 1567 Peder Oxe converts it from a charge on ships to a duty on cargo and it multiplies. Kronborg, which Frederik 2. builds in the 1570s on the site of Erik's Krogen, is paid for out of it and is essentially a very expensive receipt. The toll survives the loss of Skåne in 1658, which ought to have killed it, because the other bank was conceded by treaty rather than taken. And it ends in 1857, abolished under pressure led by the United States, against a payment of thirty-three and a half million rigsdaler — fourteen years' income, handed over to buy out a habit begun by a king who was thrown out of every country he ruled.

And there is one more thing in the ledgers, on the first surviving page. The volume for 1497 opens with a single line: Gert Roluffs af Campen gav 1 nobel — Gert Roluffs of Kampen gave one noble. That is the whole entry. A skipper's name, his home port in the Netherlands, and an English gold coin weighing just under eight grams. Nothing about his cargo, his destination, his ship or his business, because in 1497 the toll did not care: one noble per ship, and move along.

He is the first individual human being in a series that runs to more than seven hundred volumes, sixty shelf-metres and something like 1.8 million ship passages, and everything that is known about him is in those six words. From 1567 the entries get longer, because Peder Oxe made the toll a charge on cargo and the cargo had to be listed; by 1845 a single line records tonnes of cotton yarn, steel, refined sugar and lead, the ship's capacity, and a rebate. The archive gets richer as the tax gets greedier. But the first man through paid a flat rate and left a name.

Erik of Pomerania is remembered in Denmark, when he is remembered at all, as the king who lost the union and turned pirate. He is also the man who worked out that the Danish state's most valuable asset was not its land but its position, and every government since has agreed with him.

Myth-check

"Kronborg was built to collect the Sound Dues."
Kronborg is Frederik 2.'s, from the 1570s, and it was paid for out of the toll rather than built to collect it. The castle built for the job was Erik's Krogen, on the same spit, which Frederik pulled down to make room. The distinction matters because it is a century and a half of income between the two buildings.
"Queen Philippa saved Copenhagen while Erik prayed at Sorø."
She did organise the defence in April 1428, and the attack was beaten off, and the king was at Sorø. But the Hanseatic fleet came back on 15 June and destroyed the Danish fleet in the harbour, which the story tends to omit. The king-at-his-devotions detail is a chronicler's flourish of exactly the kind that gets repeated. What is not embellished is her record: regent of three kingdoms 1423–25, and the negotiator of a currency treaty with four Hanseatic towns in her own name.
"The Sound Dues were invented in 1429."
1429 is the year they are beyond dispute; collection had probably begun some years earlier, and the ancestor is Abel's toll of 1251 on ships bound to and past the Skåne market. What was new was the transit principle, the rate, and the enforcement — taxing ships that had no intention of stopping in Denmark at all.
"The Kalmar Union lasted until 1523."
As a formal claim, yes. As a working arrangement in which one king actually governed three kingdoms, it ended with Christoffer of Bavaria in 1448. After that Sweden was ruled by a Danish king only in short spells — 1457–64, 1497–1501, 1520–21 — and the rest of the time by regents of its own. The last seventy-five years of the union are mostly a Danish claim and a Swedish refusal.
"Erik of Pomerania was a hopeless king."
He lost the duchy, the war and three thrones, and the piracy is real. He also won his case at Ofen, made Copenhagen the capital, wrote town law that lasted, founded Landskrona, and created the Sound Dues — an income that funded the Danish crown for 428 years. The Danish verdict on him was written by the council that deposed him, and it has been repeated ever since.

Summary

IF YOU REMEMBER FIVE THINGSThe page in five

  1. Erik of Pomerania won the Schleswig case outright at the imperial court in 1424 and could not enforce it, and the attempt produced a nine-year war with Holstein and six Hanseatic towns.

  2. From about 1429 he charged every foreign ship passing Helsingør one English noble. It worked because both banks had been Danish since 1360 and the Belts were closed, it was paid by foreigners, it needed nobody's consent, and it ran until 1857.

  3. The war ended at Vordingborg in 1435 with the towns' privileges confirmed and Schleswig conceded to Adolf 8. of Holstein — the thing the 1424 verdict had denied him.

  4. Sweden rose in 1434 under Engelbrekt, a mine-owner from Bergslagen, and the Swedish council joined the rising and used it. Erik was deposed in Denmark and Sweden in 1439 and lived on Gotland by piracy for a decade.

  5. Christoffer of Bavaria (1440–48) was the last man to govern all three kingdoms, and paid for it in concessions; after his death the council took Christian of Oldenburg on a signed charter in 1448, founding the dynasty that still reigns.

Questions & discussion

FOUR KINDSWork the material

Recall

Did the facts land?

  • What was the Ofen verdict, who gave it, and in what year?
  • What exactly did a ship pay at Helsingør, and who was exempt?
  • Which six towns declared war in 1426, and what were their three grievances?
  • What happened at Copenhagen in April and in June 1428?
  • Who was Henrik Tagesen Reventlow, and what is odd about him?
  • How did Christian 1. come to the Danish throne?

Causal

Why did that follow from this?

  • Why did a legal victory over Schleswig leave Erik weaker rather than stronger?
  • Why does a toll require both banks of a strait, and what would have happened with one?
  • Why did an income paid by foreigners change the relationship between the Danish crown and the Danish nobility?
  • Why did the war against the Hanse produce a rising in Sweden rather than in Denmark?
  • Why did emptied farmland lead to cattle exports rather than to cheaper grain?

Counterfactual

What if it had gone otherwise?

  • If Erik had enforced the Ofen verdict and held Schleswig, is there a 1460, an 1848 or an 1864?
  • If the toll had never been imposed, what would have paid for the Danish state — and what would the crown have had to concede to get it?
  • Suppose Christoffer had lived to sixty and had sons. Does Sweden still leave?

Contested

Where the argument still is

  • When did the Sound Dues actually begin? 1429 is conventional and defensible; collection may have started around 1425; and whether it is a new tax or an old one moved and raised is partly a matter of definition.
  • How much is Philippa's reputation evidence and how much is it the appeal of the story? The regency and the currency treaty are documented; the arms-distribution at Copenhagen is chronicle.
  • Was the deposition of 1439 a constitutional act or a coup by a landed faction? The instrument was formal renunciation; the beneficiaries were two families.
  • Was Erik's Dutch policy a blunder that started a war, or a correct reading of where northern European trade was moving? By 1550 most ships paying at Helsingør were Dutch.
  • Why did a nobleman lead the Jutland peasants in 1441? Reventlow's motives have never been explained, and the sources for the rising are written by the side that hanged him.

Sources

LIGHT SOURCINGWhat this is built on

  • WORKED FROM Documents, archives and named contemporary sources.
  • — the Ofen verdict, 28 June 1424 Sigismund's arbitration survives, as does the failed Holstein appeal to Rome. A judgment records what a court decided, not what happened afterwards.
  • — the Sound toll ledgers, Helsingør More than seven hundred volumes in the Danish National Archives, oldest surviving 1497, unbroken from 1574, and digitised as Sound Toll Registers Online. For the first seventy years of the toll there are no accounts at all, which is why its start date is argued about.
  • — Hanseatic town records and recesses Lübeck's archive is the best documentation of this war, and it is the archive of one belligerent. Danish naval losses are recorded there with some satisfaction.
  • — the Engelbrekt chronicle A rhymed Swedish chronicle written within a decade of his death, and openly partisan. Nearly everything vivid about Engelbrekt comes from it.
  • — Christoffer's ordinance on the land-peace, 1442, and Copenhagen's town law of 1443 Legislation as evidence of intention and of anxiety: a law about private violence tells you private violence was a problem.
  • — danmarkshistorien.dk Aarhus University. The Sound Dues, Erik of Pomerania, Queen Philippa, Christoffer of Bavaria and the Kalmar Union.
